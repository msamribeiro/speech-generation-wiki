> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Wen-Chin Huang et al.** (Nagoya University / Shanghai Jiao Tong University / Technische Universität Braunschweig / The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen / University of Edinburgh / National Institute of Information and Communications Technology) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13792) · Demo: ✗ · Code: ✓
>
> Reports the fifth VoiceMOS Challenge, which benchmarks 18 teams' automatic predictors of human listening-test ratings on three speech tracks: absolute and comparative quality of enhanced speech, naturalness and emotion ratings of emotional TTS, and speaker and accent similarity of codec-based accented TTS.

## Problem

Human listening tests remain the reference for judging generated and enhanced speech, but they are slow and costly. Automatic MOS predictors are meant to replace them, yet they are rarely compared on shared data, and several rating types used in practice have little prediction work behind them: pairwise comparative ratings (CCR/CMOS), emotion-specific ratings of expressive TTS, and accent similarity. After the 2025 edition widened to music and general audio (the AudioMOS Challenge), community feedback said the core speech-evaluation problems were still open, so the organizers refocused the 2026 edition on speech (§1).

## Method

The challenge ran from May 20 to August 10, 2026 on CodaBench, with evaluation audio released on July 31 and a system-description questionnaire required from each team (§2). Table 1 summarizes the three tracks.

- **Track 1, speech enhancement (ACR and CCR).** Outputs of six systems from the URGENT 2026 speech enhancement challenge, for 840 source utterances in nine languages covering real and simulated noisy conditions. Listeners gave five-point absolute category ratings (ACR, giving MOS) to each sample and rated all 15 system pairs per utterance on a −3 to +3 comparative scale (CCR, giving CMOS; positive means the first sample is preferred). Each sample or pair typically received eight ratings. There is no training split; dev (1,008 ACR samples, 2,520 CCR pairs) and test (4,032 ACR samples, 10,080 CCR pairs) are disjoint in speakers and source utterances (§2.1, Table 1).
- **Track 2, emotional TTS.** Samples in five emotions (neutral, happy, sad, angry, surprised) from emotional TTS systems, with sentences and natural reference audio from ESD and DailyTalk. Four to nine listeners rated each sample for QMOS (synthesis quality: pronunciation, intonation, signal quality), EMOS (match to the ground-truth emotion label), valence, arousal and dominance (all five-point), and a multi-choice perceived-emotion question. QMOS and EMOS predictions were required; V/A/D and emotion categories were optional. Splits: 12,746 / 2,730 / 2,730 samples from 10 / 13 / 14 systems (§2.2, Table 1).
- **Track 3, accented codec-based TTS.** Built on CodecMOS-Accent: 4,000 samples from 24 codec resynthesis and TTS systems, 32 speakers, ten English accents. Given a test sample and a reference sample, a predictor outputs speaker similarity (SPK), accent similarity (ACC), or both. Dev and test contain systems unseen in training. Splits: 2,800 / 600 / 600 samples (§2.3, Table 1).

The organizers released open-source baselines (§3). B01 (Track 1) is the public UrgentMOS checkpoint, a WavLM-based non-intrusive predictor with a preference module that predicts CMOS directly from a pair, used without fine-tuning. B02 (Track 2) uses UTMOS for QMOS, a Gemini LLM-as-judge (gemini-3-flash-preview, prompted with the listener instructions) for EMOS and V/A/D, and emotion2vec+ large category probabilities for perceived emotion, all zero-shot. Track 3 has two ECAPA-TDNN baselines: B03-1 outputs the cosine similarity of pretrained speaker embeddings for both scores, and B03-2 fine-tunes ECAPA-TDNN separately for each score on the training split (AdamW, learning rate 0.001, batch size 16, 20,000 steps).

The primary metric in every track is the utterance-level Spearman rank correlation (UTT-SRCC) between predicted and human scores. For the optional emotion-category subtask the organizers define CAT-ERR: the absolute difference between the predicted value for each category and the fraction of listeners who chose that category, averaged over categories (§4.2).

## Key Results

Participation was 4, 8 and 7 teams on Tracks 1 to 3, from 18 unique teams (15 academic, 2 industry, 1 unknown), with one team entering two tracks (§3, Table 2). Per-team scores appear only as scatter plots (Figure 1); the text gives exact values only for the top Track 1 and Track 2 scores.

- **Track 1.** Every team beat the UrgentMOS baseline. T09 had the top ACR-UTT-SRCC (0.779) and T01 the top CCR-UTT-SRCC (0.487), so comparative ratings were clearly harder to predict than absolute ones. Sign accuracy of CCR predictions rises with the magnitude of the ground-truth CCR: predictors get the preference direction right mainly when the two samples differ a lot perceptually (§4.1, Figures 1a and 2).
- **Track 2.** Most teams beat the zero-shot B02 baseline on QMOS and EMOS. T02 ranked first on QMOS (0.785), EMOS (0.758), valence, arousal and CAT-ERR; the exact values come from the text and Figure 1b places T02 at that QMOS/EMOS point. The authors conclude that EMOS was not necessarily harder to predict than QMOS. Figure 1b also shows two teams (T13 and T18) with competitive QMOS but EMOS correlations near zero, so quality prediction skill did not carry over to emotion similarity for every system. All teams that entered the optional subtasks clearly beat B02 on V/A/D and CAT-ERR. Arousal was consistently the easiest of the three dimensions, Neutral was slightly harder than other emotions for QMOS and EMOS, Neutral was over-predicted in the category task and Surprise under-predicted (§4.2, Figures 1b and 3).
- **Track 3.** Most teams beat both ECAPA-TDNN baselines, and T04 ranked first on both SPK-UTT-SRCC and ACC-UTT-SRCC. Most teams scored higher on speaker than on accent similarity. In Figure 1c the fine-tuned B03-2 sits below the zero-shot B03-1 on accent similarity. The text gives no numbers for Track 3 (§4.3, Figure 1c).

The questionnaires (§5.1) show the winning recipes. T01 fused frozen wav2vec 2.0 and MERT features, used virtual listeners and a listener router, and trained on six public quality corpora with mix-up, supervised contrastive and utterance-ranking losses; its CCR was the difference of two predicted ACRs. T09 ensembled 29 scores from pretrained quality models and fine-tuned Whisper-medium and HuBERT-large branches, with ensemble weights fitted on the Track 1 dev labels. T02 combined listener embeddings, frozen UTMOS features and fine-tuned WavLM encoders, averaging over 64 listener identities and cross-validated models. T04 built eight-member ensembles per target, warm-started speaker models on VoxSim, conditioned on the 25 listeners' embeddings, and applied a system-level de-shrinkage correction to SPK predictions.

The comparisons are fair within each track (shared hidden test sets, a common metric), but the baselines are deliberately weak zero-shot or lightly tuned systems. Beating them shows that in-domain training helps more than it shows absolute predictor quality.

## Novelty Assessment

The new material is the benchmark, not a model. Three prediction targets have had little shared evaluation: CCR/CMOS prediction for enhanced speech, emotion-specific MOS (EMOS, V/A/D and perceived-emotion distributions) for emotional TTS, and reference-based accent similarity for codec-based TTS. The challenge also adds a held-out-systems test design for Track 3 and the CAT-ERR metric for distributional emotion-category prediction. The underlying listening-test datasets come from companion publications (URGENT 2026, the emotional TTS listener-impression dataset, CodecMOS-Accent), so this paper does not itself contribute a corpus. The participant systems reuse established ingredients (SSL encoders, listener-dependent modeling, ensembling), and the paper describes only the top four briefly. Its analysis is qualitative and plot-based rather than a detailed per-system comparison.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper provides a shared test bed and a first set of comparative results for predicting pairwise preferences, emotion ratings and accent similarity of generated speech. Its main findings are that comparative ratings and accent similarity are harder targets than absolute quality and speaker similarity, and that zero-shot predictors, including an LLM-as-judge, trail predictors trained on in-domain listener data. These findings come from a single edition with 4 to 8 teams per track.

## Claims

- **supports:** Automatic predictors reproduce absolute quality ratings much better than pairwise comparative ratings of the same speech.
  > *Evidence:* On the URGENT 2026 enhanced-speech test set, the top ACR-UTT-SRCC was 0.779 (T09) while the top CCR-UTT-SRCC was 0.487 (T01), and the authors conclude that CCR was more difficult to predict than ACR. *(§4.1, Figure 1a)*
- **refines:** Automatic pairwise-preference prediction is reliable mainly when the perceptual difference between the two samples is large.
  > *Evidence:* For all Track 1 systems, sign accuracy of predicted CCR rises steadily with the magnitude of the ground-truth CCR, computed over 0.1-wide bins. *(§4.1, Figure 2)*
- **complicates:** Zero-shot general-purpose judges, including LLM-as-judge prompting with listener instructions, trail predictors trained on in-domain listener ratings for emotion-related assessment of expressive TTS.
  > *Evidence:* The B02 baseline (UTMOS for QMOS, Gemini LLM-as-judge for EMOS and V/A/D, emotion2vec+ for categories, all zero-shot) was beaten by most teams on QMOS and EMOS and by every team that entered the optional V/A/D and emotion-category subtasks. *(§3, §4.2, Figures 1b and 3)*
- **complicates:** Accent similarity of synthetic speech is harder to predict automatically than speaker similarity.
  > *Evidence:* On CodecMOS-Accent with systems unseen in training, most Track 3 teams scored higher SPK-UTT-SRCC than ACC-UTT-SRCC, and the authors conclude that speaker similarity was easier to predict than accent similarity. *(§4.3, Figure 1c)*
- **supports:** The strongest MOS predictors combine pretrained self-supervised or ASR encoders with listener-dependent modeling and ensembling.
  > *Evidence:* Top teams T01, T02 and T04 all modeled individual listeners (virtual listeners, 64 listener identities, 25 listener embeddings), and T09 and T04 relied on 29-score and eight-member ensembles built on wav2vec 2.0, MERT, WavLM, Whisper and HuBERT features. *(§5.1)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- Per-team results are reported only as scatter and bar plots. The text gives exact values only for the top Track 1 and Track 2 scores, and Track 3 has no numeric results, which limits quantitative reuse.
- Participation was small (4, 8 and 7 teams per track), so the observed difficulty orderings (CCR vs. ACR, accent vs. speaker, EMOS vs. QMOS) rest on few systems.
- Only four top systems are described, from questionnaire answers rather than controlled ablations, so the paper cannot isolate which ingredient (listener modeling, SSL features, ensembling, extra training data) drives performance. The authors flag the complexity-versus-interpretability, robustness and generalization trade-off as open (§6).
- Track 1 has no training split, and some participants criticized the lack of advance notice on releasing and using dev-set labels (T09 fitted its ensemble weights on them) and the small, imbalanced datasets (§5.2).
- Utterance-level SRCC is the only primary metric. System-level agreement, calibration and uncertainty are not reported. Participants suggested explainable and uncertainty-aware prediction, long-form and full-duplex dialogue evaluation, cross-lingual and cross-domain settings, and preference-test outcome prediction for future editions (§5.2, §6).
