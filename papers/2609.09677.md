> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Zehan Liu et al.** (X Square Robot) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09677) · Demo: ? · Code: ✓
>
> Adds a small, separately trained observer to a frozen codec-based TTS model that reads the generator's native speech tokens as they are emitted and publishes a never-retreating cursor in the original input text, so a streaming-text TTS system can report which words have been spoken without decoding or re-aligning the waveform.

## Problem

In incremental-text streaming TTS, text arrives from an upstream language model in small chunks and is spoken shortly afterwards. Applications such as synchronized text highlighting, barge-in (interruption) handling and dialogue-history updates need to know which words have actually been spoken at each moment, but text arrival time does not say this, and in codec-based TTS each word spans a variable number of speech-token frames. Existing options either align the waveform (forced aligners, CTC segmentation, timestamp predictors), which needs complete audio or, in an online variant, waits for the waveform decoder and re-encodes audio with a separate acoustic model; or build alignment into the TTS generator itself (transducer TTS, phoneme-interleaved codec LMs), which means changing and retraining the generator. The paper targets online tracking with low compute and bounded lookahead that leaves the TTS model untouched.

## Method

X2-NativeCursor has three parts (Figure 1). **TNPlan**, a text-normalization plan, turns incoming raw text into spoken labels (the normalized reading) and records, for every label, the span of original-text characters it came from. Labels are released to both the TTS generator and the observer only once their reading is fixed, so later input cannot change them; when one symbol expands into several labels (the Chinese reading of "99%" puts the percent expression before the number), all those labels share the span of "99%".

The trainable **observer** consists of a text encoder (label embedding plus a causal convolution of kernel size 3), a native-token encoder (a speech-token embedding followed by four 1-D convolution blocks with dilations 1, 2, 4, 8) and a local matcher. The native-token encoder reads the generator's speech-token frames (for Qwen3-TTS, the first codebook) with a fixed number of future frames L; the main Qwen3-TTS setting uses L = 1, i.e. 80 ms of lookahead. For each frame, the matcher scores seven candidate labels at offsets −2 to +4 around the previous internal position with an additive-attention score that combines the frame feature, the candidate label feature, per-offset parameters and a location state (fractional position, dwell time scaled by a prior label rate, and mean recent advance). A softmax over offsets gives a distribution whose mean updates the internal position, which is therefore allowed to move backward or skip labels. A separate output rule publishes the furthest label reached so far and maps it to the largest original-text span end, so the published cursor never retreats.

![Figure 1. Overview of X2-NativeCursor. TNPlan (1) maps spoken labels to original-text spans. The native-token encoder (2) and local matcher (3) track the current label position before waveform decoding. The output is a cursor in the original text that never moves backward. Dashed paths are used only during training.](assets/2609.09677/figure-1.png)

Training uses label onset times from Qwen3-ForcedAligner as automatic supervision. The loss is a cross entropy on the offset distribution against reference offsets, plus 0.3 times a content loss (predicting the spoken label from each frame feature, sharing the label embedding) plus 0.01 times a rate loss keeping overall advance close to a per-utterance prior rate. The reference cursor is perturbed with probability 0.3 during training. Encoders use hidden size 256; training runs 10 epochs with AdamW (learning rate 10⁻³, cosine decay, batch 24). The observer has 2.166M parameters. A separate observer is trained per TTS backbone to match its token vocabulary and frame period; the generator, speech tokenizer and waveform decoder are never modified.

## Key Results

All tracking scores measure agreement with an automatic aligner, not with human-annotated timings. The observer is trained on 20,235 Qwen3-TTS samples and tested on 800 texts (200 each of plain Chinese, Chinese with numbers, Chinese with symbols, English), with text fed in 2-8 character chunks and a fixed voice (§3.1).

Against the Qwen3-ForcedAligner reference (Table 1), X2-NativeCursor reaches a Chinese-character MAE of 0.151 ± 0.005 at 80 ms lookahead, against 1.253 for the online waveform baseline WindowMMS+PersistentCTC at 320 ms lookahead (about 88% lower) and 0.414 / 0.416 for the two native-token baselines trained with the same supervision (cross-attention readout and CodecCTC+skip-DP, both at 320 ms; about 64% lower). Its Chinese MAE is also lower than the three complete-audio aligners (MMS-FA 0.539, CTC-segmentation 0.371, FunASR timestamp predictor 0.744), which see the whole utterance. English MAE is 1.247 against 2.226 for the online waveform baseline and 1.568-1.629 for the other systems that report English; English labels are whole words averaging 4.58 characters, so English errors are coarser. Onset F1 at 80 ms tolerance is 0.924 (next best 0.828), onset AAS is 51.1 ms (next best 95.6 ms), and median onset lag is 0 ms against 80 ms for most baselines.

To check dependence on the training reference, 604 samples are re-scored against MMS-FA shifted 40 ms earlier (zh* column). X2-NativeCursor (0.206) stays ahead of the online waveform baseline (1.134), both learned native-token baselines (0.311) and CTC-segmentation (0.250), but the offline Qwen3-ForcedAligner itself scores lower (0.158), so under this second reference the observer is not the most accurate system overall.

Ablations (Table 2, offline replay; MAE averaged over the four text groups) show the text encoder is essential (0.343 to 1.465 without it). Removing backward and skip moves is catastrophic for English (0.927 to 10.278) while Chinese changes little (zh-plain 0.131 to 0.124, zh-sym 0.170 to 0.168, slightly lower without them). Removing lookahead raises MAE to 0.471; the position/rate features matter little (0.355). With three unseen voices, MAE rises to 0.788, ranging 0.320-1.213 across voices.

Alignment RTF is 0.0180 against 0.3598 for the online waveform baseline (about 95% less computation); median observer cost is 1.33 ms per native frame, and on one A800 GPU the median cursor-update time rises from 1.70 ms to 2.45 ms from 1 to 16 concurrent sessions, with a 90th percentile of 4.92 ms at 16 sessions (§3.4, Figure 2), far below the 80 ms of speech per frame. Retrained on CosyVoice2 (40 ms frames), the selected run reaches Chinese MAE 0.284 (95% CI 0.237-0.343), with the other seeds at 0.254 and 0.241 (§3.5).

## Novelty Assessment

The new element is an architectural and inference-procedure one: a lightweight observer that aligns streaming text to a TTS generator's own discrete speech tokens, with a local offset-distribution matcher whose internal state may revise while a separate monotonic output rule maps progress back to original-text spans through the normalization plan. Prior alignment either operates on decoded waveforms or is built into the generator (transducer TTS, phoneme-interleaved codec LMs); attaching a separate tracker to native tokens of an unchanged generator is a distinct design point. The components themselves (dilated convolutions, additive attention, forced-aligner supervision) are standard, and the evaluation scope is narrow: one fixed voice for the main results, automatic references only, and an in-house test set. The comparison with complete-audio aligners is partly circular for the main column, since the training reference is also the evaluation reference; the paper addresses this with the second-reference check.

## Field Significance

Moderate — the paper demonstrates that the discrete speech tokens a codec-based TTS model emits carry enough timing information to track text progress online, at low lookahead and a small fraction of the compute of waveform re-alignment, without modifying the generator. It addresses a practical requirement of incremental-text streaming TTS in dialogue systems (knowing what has been spoken for highlighting and interruption) that generation-quality benchmarks do not measure, and shows the same observer design retrains for a second backbone.

## Claims

- **supports:** The discrete speech tokens emitted by a codec-based TTS generator carry enough timing information to track which input text has been spoken online, without decoding or re-encoding the waveform.
  > *Evidence:* An observer reading only Qwen3-TTS native tokens with 80 ms lookahead reaches 0.151 Chinese-character MAE against an automatic aligner, versus 1.253 for an online waveform aligner with 320 ms lookahead, and keeps its lead over that baseline (0.206 vs. 1.134) under a second automatic reference. *(§3.2, Table 1)*
- **supports:** Text progress in streaming TTS can be tracked by an external module attached to a frozen generator, rather than by building alignment into the generator architecture.
  > *Evidence:* The same observer design, retrained per backbone with the generator, tokenizer and decoder unchanged, works on Qwen3-TTS (0.151) and CosyVoice2 (0.284, 95% CI 0.237-0.343) at an alignment RTF of 0.0180 versus 0.3598 for waveform re-alignment. *(§3.4, §3.5)*
- **refines:** Online alignment for streaming speech benefits from separating a revisable internal position estimate from a monotonic published output, especially when text units are coarse.
  > *Evidence:* Forbidding backward and skip moves in the internal estimate raises English MAE from 0.927 to 10.278 (word-level labels), while Chinese character-level MAE barely changes. *(§3.3, Table 2)*
- **complicates:** Token-level progress trackers trained on speech from a single voice are sensitive to the synthesis voice, so speaker variation in the generator's output is a robustness risk.
  > *Evidence:* With three voices unseen in training, average MAE rises from 0.343 to 0.788, with per-voice MAE ranging from 0.320 to 1.213. *(§3.3, Table 2)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

All accuracy numbers measure agreement with automatic aligners (Qwen3-ForcedAligner, which also supplies the training targets, and MMS-FA); no human-annotated word timings are used, so absolute tracking accuracy relative to perceived speech is not established. Under the MMS-FA reference the offline Qwen3-ForcedAligner (0.158) is closer than the observer (0.206).

Main results use one fixed voice, and accuracy degrades markedly on unseen voices (0.788 average, up to 1.213 for one voice), so deployment with zero-shot voice cloning or many speakers is untested. The observer must be retrained for each backbone, and CosyVoice2 is shown with a single test condition. English tracking is much coarser than Chinese (word-level labels; MAE 1.247 characters). The test set (800 texts) is in-house, text chunking is simulated (2-8 characters) rather than taken from a live LLM, and the downstream uses that motivate the work (highlighting, interruption handling, dialogue-history updates) are not evaluated end to end. TNPlan's normalization rules are described only for Chinese examples.
