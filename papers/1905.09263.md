> [!abstract] NeurIPS · 2019 · Conference
> **Yi Ren et al.** (Zhejiang University / Microsoft Research / Microsoft STC Asia) · [→ Paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/hash/f63f65b503e22cb970527f23c9ad7db1-Abstract.html) · [→ Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09263) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✗
>
> FastSpeech replaces autoregressive mel-spectrogram decoding with a fully parallel feed-forward Transformer, using an explicit per-phoneme duration predictor and length regulator to bridge the phoneme and frame sequences.

## Problem

Neural TTS systems of the time (Tacotron 2, Transformer TTS, Deep Voice 3) generated mel-spectrograms autoregressively, frame by frame, with soft encoder-decoder attention aligning text to speech. The paper identifies three consequences. Inference is slow because mel sequences run to hundreds or thousands of frames. Synthesis is not robust: error propagation and wrong attention alignments cause skipped and repeated words. Control is weak, since nothing in the model exposes the text-speech alignment, so speaking rate and pauses cannot be set directly. Parallel waveform vocoders (Parallel WaveNet, ClariNet, WaveGlow) had already removed the autoregressive bottleneck at the waveform stage, but they still consumed autoregressively generated mel-spectrograms.

## Method

FastSpeech maps a phoneme sequence to an 80-dimensional mel-spectrogram in one parallel pass. The backbone, called the Feed-Forward Transformer (FFT), stacks 6 FFT blocks on the phoneme side and 6 on the mel side. Each block is multi-head self-attention followed by a 2-layer 1D convolutional network with ReLU (replacing the Transformer's position-wise dense layers), with residual connections, layer normalization and dropout. There is no encoder-decoder cross-attention. The hidden size is 384 with 2 attention heads, and the model has 30.1M parameters (Table 5).

The length regulator sits between the two FFT stacks. Given a duration d_i for each phoneme, it repeats each phoneme hidden state d_i times so that the expanded sequence matches the mel length. A scalar α rescales all durations before rounding, which sets the speaking rate (for example α = 1.3 for slower, 0.5 for faster speech). Lengthening the duration of space characters inserts breaks between words, giving partial prosody control (§3.2).

The duration predictor (2 conv layers with layer norm and dropout, then a linear output) sits on top of the phoneme-side FFT. It is trained jointly with the main model under an MSE loss on log-domain durations, and it is only used at inference; training uses extracted durations directly (§3.3). Duration targets come from an autoregressive Transformer TTS teacher with a similar parameter count (30.7M). For each training utterance, the method takes the teacher's decoder-to-encoder attention, picks the head with the highest "focus rate" (mean over frames of the maximum attention weight, a measure of diagonality), and counts how many frames attend most strongly to each phoneme.

The teacher also supplies sequence-level knowledge distillation: FastSpeech is trained on the teacher's generated mel-spectrograms paired with the source text, rather than on ground-truth mels (§4.3). Both models train on LJSpeech (12,500 training clips) with phonemes from an internal grapheme-to-phoneme tool, for about 80k steps on 4 V100 GPUs. A pretrained WaveGlow vocoder converts the predicted mels to waveforms.

## Key Results

All experiments use single-speaker LJSpeech, with a 300-utterance test set.

- **Quality (Table 1):** MOS, with at least 20 native English listeners per sample, is 3.84 ± 0.08 for FastSpeech. This compares with 3.88 ± 0.09 for Transformer TTS and 3.86 ± 0.09 for Tacotron 2, all using WaveGlow. FastSpeech is numerically the lowest of the three neural systems, but the confidence intervals overlap, so quality is roughly on par rather than better. Merlin (WORLD) scores 2.40, and the WaveGlow resynthesis ceiling (GT Mel + WaveGlow) is 4.00.
- **Speed (Table 2):** On one V100 with batch size 1, mel generation latency drops from 6.735 s (Transformer TTS) to 0.025 s, a 269.40x speedup. End-to-end with WaveGlow, latency drops from 6.895 s to 0.180 s (38.30x), so the vocoder dominates FastSpeech's total latency. FastSpeech latency barely grows with output length, while Transformer TTS latency grows strongly (Figure 2).
- **Robustness (Table 3):** On 50 hard sentences (URLs, file paths, code identifiers, digit strings), Tacotron 2 has 4 repeats, 11 skips and 12 error sentences (24%). Transformer TTS has 7 repeats, 15 skips and 17 error sentences (34%). FastSpeech has none (0%). Errors are counted by sentence, at most once per error type, and the paper does not report a WER.
- **Control (Figures 3–4):** Speaking rate is adjusted from 0.5x to 1.5x, and word breaks are inserted at chosen positions. The evidence is qualitative only: mel-spectrogram plots and demo audio, with no listening test or pitch measurement.
- **Ablations (Table 4, CMOS):** Replacing the 1D convolution with a dense layer of similar size gives −0.113 CMOS. Removing sequence-level distillation gives −0.325 CMOS.

The speed comparison is fair in parameter count (30.1M vs. 30.7M) but is measured only against the paper's own teacher, not against Tacotron 2. The comparison with the concurrent parallel model ParaNet is argued in prose only (§2), not measured.

## Novelty Assessment

The architectural contribution is real. Removing the encoder-decoder attention altogether and replacing it with explicit, hard per-phoneme durations expanded by a length regulator is a structural change in how TTS bridges text and frame sequences. It is not just a faster decoder. The paper argues that this one change addresses speed, robustness and controllability together, and its Table 3 robustness result follows directly from the hard alignment.

Several ingredients are borrowed. Self-attention comes from the Transformer, and convolution in place of the feed-forward layers follows earlier convolutional seq2seq work. Sequence-level knowledge distillation comes from non-autoregressive machine translation, and explicit duration models were standard in statistical parametric TTS. The novelty lies in combining them into a fully parallel neural acoustic model. The method still needs an autoregressive teacher, both for duration targets and for the distilled training mels. Without distillation, the ablation shows a clear quality drop (−0.325 CMOS), so the teacher pipeline is part of the method's cost, not an optional extra.

## Field Significance

> [!important]
> Foundational — FastSpeech demonstrates that a non-autoregressive, attention-free acoustic model with explicit phoneme durations can match autoregressive mel-spectrogram quality on LJSpeech, with a two-orders-of-magnitude faster mel generation and no word skipping or repetition on hard inputs. The paper's duration predictor plus length regulator design introduces an explicit alignment interface that makes speaking rate and pause placement directly controllable.

## Claims

- **supports:** Non-autoregressive acoustic models with explicit duration-based length regulation can match the naturalness of autoregressive attention-based mel-spectrogram models on single-speaker read speech.
  > *Evidence:* FastSpeech reaches 3.84 ± 0.08 MOS against 3.88 ± 0.09 for Transformer TTS and 3.86 ± 0.09 for Tacotron 2 on LJSpeech, all using the same WaveGlow vocoder, with overlapping confidence intervals. *(§5, Table 1)*
- **supports:** Replacing soft encoder-decoder attention with hard per-phoneme duration alignment removes word skipping and repetition on difficult inputs.
  > *Evidence:* On 50 particularly hard sentences, Tacotron 2 fails on 24% and Transformer TTS on 34% of sentences (skips and repeats), while FastSpeech has 0 repeats, 0 skips and 0 error sentences. *(§5, Table 3)*
- **supports:** Parallel mel-spectrogram generation makes acoustic-model latency nearly independent of output length and moves the end-to-end bottleneck to the vocoder.
  > *Evidence:* Mel generation latency falls from 6.735 s to 0.025 s (269.40x) at a similar parameter count, but end-to-end latency with WaveGlow is 0.180 s (38.30x), and latency vs. mel length is flat for FastSpeech but rising for Transformer TTS. *(§5, Table 2, Figure 2)*
- **refines:** Non-autoregressive TTS students depend on an autoregressive teacher's outputs for both alignment targets and training data quality.
  > *Evidence:* Duration targets come from the attention head with the highest focus rate in a Transformer TTS teacher, and training without sequence-level knowledge distillation from teacher-generated mels costs −0.325 CMOS. *(§3.3, §4.3, Table 4)*
- **supports:** Explicit duration modelling exposes a direct control interface for speaking rate and pause placement.
  > *Evidence:* Scaling durations by α changes speed from 0.5x to 1.5x, and lengthening space-character durations inserts word breaks, shown qualitatively in mel-spectrograms without listener evaluation. *(§3.2, §5, Figures 3–4)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

The method is not self-contained: it needs a fully trained autoregressive Transformer TTS teacher. The teacher supplies duration targets through a heuristic choice of attention head and also provides the distilled training mels. Any alignment errors in the teacher pass directly into the student's duration targets, and the paper does not analyse how teacher quality affects the student.

- All experiments use a single English speaker (LJSpeech). Multi-speaker and low-resource settings are named as future work (§6).
- Controllability evidence is qualitative only. Speed changes and inserted breaks are shown in mel-spectrogram plots and demo audio, with no listening test, pitch measurement or intelligibility check at non-default rates.
- The robustness test counts errors per sentence on a 50-sentence set and reports no WER. Detection of skips and repeats is not described.
- Quality is on par with, not above, the autoregressive baselines (numerically lowest of the three neural systems in Table 1). End-to-end speed is bounded by the WaveGlow vocoder, which the paper plans to address with joint parallel vocoder training (§6).
- Scaled durations are rounded to integer frame counts (§3.2). The paper says only "part of" prosody (word breaks) is controllable, and the model has no explicit pitch or energy input.
