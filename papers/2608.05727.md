> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **June Young Yi et al.** (Seoul National University) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05727) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✓
>
> LILAC builds a 0.75 kbps speech codec whose encoder is an invertible transform that keeps some coordinates and drops the rest, so decoding and re-encoding any valid token stream returns exactly the same tokens, with a short algebraic proof that holds for any trained weights.

## Problem

Neural codecs train the encoder, quantizer and decoder for one-pass reconstruction. Nothing in that objective requires a token stream to survive a decode and re-encode cycle. This matters wherever generated or edited audio is tokenized again: generation, editing, storage and retransmission pipelines. Prior work on code drift (O'Reilly et al., 2025) added fine-tuning losses that reduce drift without removing it, so residual errors can still accumulate over repeated cycles. The paper measures this drift across 12 baseline configurations and finds that every one rewrites at least 15% of its tokens on the first pass.

## Method

The paper separates **codec idempotence**, E(D(c)) = c for every code c, from **audio idempotence**, D(E(x)) = x. Audio idempotence needs an injective, near-lossless code space; codec idempotence only requires the decoder to return a waveform inside the set of waveforms that encode to c. LILAC (Lifting-Inspired Low-rate Audio Codec) builds this in. An invertible analysis transform A maps audio to retained coordinates z and discarded coordinates f. The encoder quantizes z with Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ). The decoder predicts the discarded coordinates with a learned fill network and applies the exact inverse of A. Re-encoding recovers z exactly and drops the predicted fill again. Because FSQ is clamping plus rounding, which are both idempotent, E(D(z_q)) = z_q holds whatever the weights or fill network are. The same argument covers a stochastic fill with an extra noise input.

The analysis transform uses normalizing-flow primitives. Invertible 1x1 convolutions are constrained to orthogonal kernels, so inversion is a transposed convolution. Additive coupling blocks with ConvNeXt-1D functions split the signal into even and odd time indices and stack the halves channel-wise; inversion is subtraction, with no division. The 24 kHz waveform is reshaped to 5 channels and passed through an anti-imaging stem, which uses a small affine coupling. Four squeeze-and-mix stages then grow it to 80 channels. Five further stages halve time and discard 80, 80, 80, 80 and 140 channels, which leaves 20 coordinates per frame at a 2560-sample hop. With 4 bits per coordinate this gives 80 bits per frame, 9.375 Hz and 0.75 kbps. The decoder shares the transform weights with the encoder. A convolutional code-context tower and a context ladder upsampled x2 per stage condition stage-specific residual predictors that fill each discarded block.

![LILAC's encoder (top), shared-weight inverse (middle), and decoder-only fill networks (bottom). Projection stages transmit the retained coordinates and discard the rest; the decoder predicts the discarded blocks before inversion. Re-encoding recovers z q and drops those predictions, closing the exact red loop for any fill. The flagship uses C = 20, d = 7.](assets/2608.05727/figure-2.png)

Training follows a standard vocoder-style recipe: multi-resolution mel loss (weight 15), multi-resolution STFT loss (1), hinge adversarial loss against multi-period and multi-scale STFT discriminators (1), and feature matching (2). The adversarial terms start after a 5,000-step warm-up. The model has 58.5M parameters: 43.1M in the shared transform and 15.4M in the fill networks. It trains on HiFiTTS-2 (about 31,700 h, resampled to 24 kHz) with every LibriSpeech/LibriTTS(-R) reader removed. Sample-rate, low-pass and loudness augmentation are used. The invertible 1x1 convolutions run in full precision on TPU to keep the round trip exact. The released model averages checkpoints from 889k to 898k steps with SWA.

## Key Results

**Idempotence.** All 7,457 LibriSpeech test-clean and LibriTTS-R test samples re-encode to the identical token stream. On 100 LibriSpeech clips cycled 100 times, first-pass token agreement is 0.805 for EnCodec (1.5 kbps), 0.329 for Mimi (about 1.1 kbps), 0.109 for FocalCodec (0.65 kbps) and 0.017 for DAC (about 24 kbps). FocalCodec falls to 0.0005 agreement by cycle 100 and Mimi to 0.065 (supplement Table 18). Over the 12-configuration panel, first-pass agreement runs from 0.847 (BigCodec) down to 0.109 (FocalCodec). DAC's UTMOS falls from 4.058 to 1.260 by cycle 100 and FocalCodec's from 4.059 to 2.677 (supplement Table 20). The LILAC rows in these drift tables come from a low-quality probe checkpoint (UTMOS 1.299), so they show only the structural fixed point, not flagship quality.

**Single-pass quality at matched rate (< 1 kbps, Table 2).** LILAC has the highest PESQ, STOI and SI-SNR in the sub-1 kbps panel on all three out-of-domain sets. On LibriSpeech these are 2.60, 0.935 and +2.2 dB; on LibriTTS-R 2.60, 0.944 and +6.0 dB; on VCTK 2.59, 0.867 and +5.3 dB. The next-best sub-1 kbps PESQ values are 2.11, 2.41 and 2.29. It is not best on the other metrics:

- **UTMOS.** It is highest only on LibriTTS-R: 4.238 against 4.217 for FocalCodec 12.5 Hz. On LibriSpeech it is third with 4.141, behind FocalCodec 12.5 Hz (4.226) and 25 Hz (4.145). On VCTK it is second with 4.104, behind FocalCodec 12.5 Hz (4.143). Above 1 kbps, LFSC (4.268) and DualCodec (4.252) exceed it on LibriTTS-R (supplement Table 8).
- **dWER.** It ranks fifth of nine codecs on LibriSpeech (0.101 against 0.067 for FocalCodec 50 Hz) and on LibriTTS-R (0.086 against 0.058). It ranks third on VCTK (0.041 against 0.029 for SNAC).
- **SCOREQ.** It is mid-pack: 0.299 on LibriSpeech against 0.242 for FocalCodec 50 Hz, and 0.252 on LibriTTS-R against 0.164 for WavTokenizer 75 Hz.

Every sub-1 kbps baseline trains on LibriTTS or an undisclosed corpus, while LILAC is out-of-domain on all three sets (supplement Tables 6 to 7). Across five seeds trained to 600k steps, the UTMOS standard deviation is 0.021 to 0.022 (Table 1).

**Re-encoding stability on downstream endpoints (Table 3).** LILAC keeps speaker EER at 4.0% and dWER at 0.103 at every depth K. At K = 1 it is fourth of five on both endpoints, behind DAC, FocalCodec and Mimi. At K = 10, Mimi (EER 28.17%) and FocalCodec (27%) cross above LILAC with Holm p = 0.0008. DAC at about 24 kbps still does better at K = 10 (EER 3%, dWER 0.087). EnCodec has the higher EER at every depth (10% to 12%), but its dWER at K = 1 (0.115) is close. At K = 100 LILAC has the lowest EER and dWER of the five: the next best are EnCodec at 12% and 0.143. The main text says LILAC "beats all other codecs by a wide margin" after repeated re-encoding; the table supports this at K = 100, not at K = 10 against DAC.

**Single-pass downstream transfer (supplement Table 22).** This panel covers 11,926 clips from four corpora and uses an earlier pre-promotion checkpoint. LILAC is last of the five learned codecs on dWER (9.9%) and second-worst on speaker EER (7.72%; Mimi 8.26%).

**Listening test (supplement Table 29).** The test is a crowdsourced MUSHRA-style test with 40 screened listeners, nine items and ICC(2,1) = 0.655. LILAC scores 51.4 against 48.5 for FocalCodec (0.65 kbps). The difference of +2.9 has a 95% CI of [-3.3, +9.0] and Holm p = 0.077, so it is not significant. The higher-rate systems all score higher: WavTokenizer 59.6 (0.90 kbps), SNAC 59.9 (0.98 kbps) and DualCodec 74.8 (1.23 kbps). The hidden reference averages 90.6.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is architectural and genuinely new for neural speech codecs. An invertible lifting-style transform splits coordinates into retained and discarded sets, and only the discarded ones are learned. This makes codec idempotence a structural property rather than a training target. The proof is short and does not depend on fill quality. Its components are established: additive coupling (NICE), orthogonal invertible 1x1 convolutions (Glow), FSQ and a HiFi-GAN-style loss recipe. The novelty lies in how they are arranged and in the explicit codec-versus-audio idempotence framing.

The empirical side is honest about the costs. Single-pass quality is competitive rather than state of the art, the listening-test gain over the rate-nearest peer is not significant, and the main motivation (more stable downstream generators trained on the tokens) is not tested. The drift comparison mixes bitrates from 0.65 to about 24 kbps, so the authors frame conclusions as within-codec. The out-of-domain handicap is real: every sub-1 kbps baseline trains on LibriTTS or undisclosed data, while LILAC excludes all evaluation readers.

## Field Significance

Moderate — LILAC shows that exact codec idempotence can be obtained by construction at a sub-1 kbps rate with single-pass quality comparable to its rate peers. This gives pipelines that re-tokenize generated or edited audio a token interface that does not drift. Its value to speech generation depends on downstream evidence that the paper itself leaves for future work.

## Claims

- **supports:** Neural codecs trained only for one-pass reconstruction are not stable under repeated decode and re-encode cycles; tokens drift and perceptual quality and intelligibility degrade as cycles accumulate.
  > *Evidence:* All 12 baseline configurations rewrite at least 15% of tokens on the first pass (first-pass agreement 0.109 to 0.847). FocalCodec agreement falls to 0.0005 by cycle 100, and DAC UTMOS drops from 4.058 to 1.260 with dWER rising to 1.45. *(§Idempotence Verification, Figure 1; supplement §4, Tables 18 to 20, §4.1)*
- **supports:** Codec idempotence can be guaranteed structurally, independent of training, when the encoder is an invertible transform followed by coordinate selection and an idempotent scalar quantizer, and the decoder only predicts the discarded coordinates.
  > *Evidence:* An algebraic proof shows E(D(z_q)) = z_q for any fill network. All 7,457 LibriSpeech and LibriTTS-R test samples re-encode to identical tokens, and speaker EER (4.0%) and dWER (0.103) stay fixed from K = 1 to K = 100. *(§Codec Idempotence; §Idempotence Verification; Table 3)*
- **complicates:** A structural idempotence guarantee at very low bitrate does not by itself bring single-pass quality gains, and the drift-stability advantage only appears after several re-encoding cycles.
  > *Evidence:* At 0.75 kbps LILAC is mid-pack on dWER and SCOREQ, its MUSHRA gain over FocalCodec is not significant (+2.9, p = 0.077), and it is last on single-pass downstream dWER. At K = 1 it ranks fourth of five on speaker EER and dWER, overtaking Mimi and FocalCodec only by K = 10. *(§Human Evaluation; §Limitations; Table 2; Table 3; supplement Tables 22, 29)*
- **refines:** Token-level stability and perceptual stability under re-encoding are distinct properties; a codec can keep its output quality while rewriting a large fraction of its tokens, or settle onto a different stable token stream.
  > *Evidence:* SpeechTokenizer's UTMOS changes by +0.00 after 100 cycles while first-encoding agreement falls to 0.573. EnCodec's cycle-to-cycle agreement reaches 0.9994 at cycle 100 while agreement with the first encoding has settled at 0.714. *(§Supplement 4.1; supplement Table 18)*
- **refines:** At very low bitrates, codecs separate into waveform-coherent and non-waveform-coherent groups: some reach high predicted naturalness while discarding adherence to the original waveform.
  > *Evidence:* FocalCodec variants score UTMOS up to 4.226 on LibriSpeech with SI-SNR around -34 to -37 dB, while LILAC, WavTokenizer 75 Hz and SNAC keep SI-SNR near or above 0 dB. *(§Naturalness and Intelligibility at Matched Rate; Table 2)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> The main motivation, that idempotent tokens make downstream generators or editing pipelines more stable, is not tested: no TTS model or speech LM is trained on LILAC tokens. The re-encoding results only show that re-encoding the codec's own output does not drift.

- Single-pass quality is competitive, not state of the art. dWER and SCOREQ lag the best sub-1 kbps peers, and on the single-pass downstream panel LILAC is last on dWER, although that panel uses an earlier checkpoint.
- The listening test is crowdsourced and not BS.1534-conformant, with self-selected playback, nine items and 40 listeners. The authors note that MUSHRA scores largely track bitrate.
- Comparisons span different bitrates. The drift panel ranges from 0.65 to about 24 kbps, and LILAC's drift trajectories use a low-quality probe checkpoint.
- LILAC's GPU real-time factor lags the other codecs because it uses many small-channel convolutions; the paper gives no RTF figures.
- In ablations, scaling the fill network or untying encoder and decoder weights does not help, while an oracle fill lowers median SCOREQ from 0.256 to 0.205. The authors conclude that progress needs architectural changes to the transform or the fill.
- A stochastic fill that keeps the idempotence guarantee is proposed but not trained or evaluated.
