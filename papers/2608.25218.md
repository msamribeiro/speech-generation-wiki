> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Freeman Jiang et al.** (Sesame AI / Mundo AI / Carnegie Mellon University / National Taiwan University / Academia Sinica / Oto / Brno University of Technology) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25218) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✓
>
> TurnBench pairs a 30-hour, triple-annotated corpus of dyadic human conversation in six controlled conversation types with one scoring protocol for end-of-turn and interruption detection, and uses it to compare 14 turn-taking systems, from energy VAD to full-duplex speech LMs.

## Problem

Turn-taking work splits the problem into separate tasks (end-of-turn detection, interruption handling, backchannel prediction), each scored on its own annotation, even though the same preceding speech cues all of them. Existing full-duplex benchmarks (the Full-Duplex-Bench series, Talking Turns) each define backchannels, fillers and interruptions in their own way, and these definitions are not grounded in conversation analysis. Gains across benchmarks may therefore reflect the annotation convention rather than the system. Annotated corpora also cover a single register each (telephone, meetings, map task), and large full-duplex corpora built for synthesis training carry no turn-taking labels (§I, §II, Table I).

## Method

**Corpus.** 106 voice actors in 53 pairs record 154 dialogues (about 30 hours, mean 11.7 min) in separate sound-isolated studio booths at 48 kHz, one channel per speaker. Each session is assigned one of six conversation types (Casual, Task-Oriented, Instructional, Collaborative, Argumentative, Narrative), each 13-21% of the corpus. A warm-up sets the type and topic, but speakers never receive instructions about interruptions, backchannels, overlap or dominance. The authors treat conversation type as a variable set at recording time (§III-A, §III-D).

**Annotation.** VAD segments the audio. Three independent annotators tag each segment with one of 17 fine labels grounded in conversation analysis (Transition-Relevance Places, backchannels). The fine labels collapse into seven canonical categories: TURN, INTERRUPTION, NON-FLOOR-TAKING INTERRUPTION, BACKCHANNEL, LAUGHTER, AWKWARD SILENCE and NONCONTENT (§III-B, §III-E, Table II). The gold standard keeps a segment when at least two annotators agree on the canonical label and their endpoints agree within ±200 ms. Spans with no majority become excluded intervals (§IV-A). A separate 104-hour training set, speaker-disjoint from the benchmark, uses the same protocol. The benchmark itself has a 25/75 speaker-disjoint dev/test split (38/116 dialogues); test labels are withheld (§IV-C).

**Tracks and scoring.** There are two causal boundary-detection tracks. In End-of-Turn (EOT), positives are segment ends where the floor passes to the other speaker (8,197 anchors), and negatives are mid-turn pauses (4,254). In Interruption (INT), positives are consensus floor-taking interruption onsets on the interrupter's channel (1,151), and negatives are BACKCHANNEL and NONCONTENT spans (17,511). Non-floor-taking interruptions are excluded because they look identical to real interruptions at onset (§IV-A, §V-A, §V-B). A prediction matches a gold positive inside the window [t − 0.25 s, t + 3.0 s]. Firing inside a negative span counts as at most one false positive. The protocol reports recall, false positive rate (FPR) and signed latency Δt = t_pred − t_gold; negative values mean the system committed before the gold boundary. The leaderboard ranks by test recall under a 0.15 FPR ceiling (§V-C).

**Baselines.** The 14 systems are an RMS-energy VAD, OpenAI Realtime Server VAD and Semantic VAD, Kyutai SVAD, SmartTurn v3, VAP, a Mimi-codec endpointer (Mimi-EP), ESPnet's Switchboard turn-taking predictor (mixed and per-channel), causal WavLM-Base/Large predictors, a WavLM-Large predictor adapted from the ANCHOR framework, and two full-duplex models (Gemini 3.1 Live and Moshi) (§VI). Systems with a training pipeline are fine-tuned on the TurnBench training split. Published and commercial systems run as-is. For each system that outputs probabilities, the threshold is the highest-recall point within a 0.1 FPR budget on dev, then frozen for test (§VII, Figure 2). The full-duplex models replace one speaker in a live session. Their EOT decisions are read as speech onsets in their output audio (pyannote VAD), so their latency includes response generation time. They are scored on EOT only (§VI-D).

## Key Results

All results are on the test split (Table IV).

- **The corpus differs by type as intended.** Argumentative dialogues give the most consensus interruptions (377). Casual and Collaborative have the most speaker changes and overlap. Instructional and Narrative have the longest turns and the fewest interruptions (§IV-B, Table III). Agreement is high: frame-level Fleiss' κ is 0.78, onset boundary F1 is 0.94-0.96, and 85.8% of annotator events survive the gold filter. Retention is lower in the high-overlap types (83.5-84.9% vs 87.8-89.1%).
- **Humans start early.** Floor transfers begin a median 151 ms before the current turn ends when floor-taking interruptions are excluded (281 ms with them). TurnBench tempo is close to Switchboard (19.0 vs 18.5 events/min), but 64% of TurnBench floor transfers start in overlap, against 43% in Switchboard (§IV-B).
- **EOT.** Among systems within the FPR budget, VAP has the highest pooled EOT recall: 0.845 at 0.055 FPR and 368 ms median latency. ESPnet's predictor follows (0.826 / 0.078), then WavLM-Large (anchor) (0.800 / 0.054). The RMS VAD and OpenAI Server VAD reach higher recall (0.718 and 0.955) only at EOT FPRs of 0.632 and 0.525. OpenAI Semantic VAD is very conservative: 0.303 recall at 0.018 FPR and 793 ms latency.
- **INT.** VAP also has the highest in-budget INT recall: 0.945 at 0.107 test FPR and 994 ms latency, ahead of Mimi-EP (0.899 / 0.106), Kyutai SVAD (0.898 / 0.081) and WavLM-Large (anchor) (0.868 / 0.054). Systems that commit at the interrupter's onset are faster but less accurate. OpenAI Server VAD fires on nearly half of the backchannels (0.458 FPR). SmartTurn v3 commits in 159 ms but recovers only 0.107 of interruptions (§VII-b).
- **Effect of conversation type.** EOT recall varies little across types for a given system (VAP: 0.82-0.88). INT false positives depend strongly on type: Casual INT FPR is higher than Argumentative INT FPR for all 12 systems scored on INT. This matches the backchannel rate (5.2 vs 3.09 BC/min) (Table III, Table IV).
- **Full-duplex models.** Gemini 3.1 Live is conservative: 0.657 EOT recall at 0.022 FPR, with the highest EOT latency of any system (1234 ms). Moshi stays within the FPR budget but reaches only 0.233 EOT recall, and it falls progressively silent over long sessions (§VII).
- **Gap to humans.** The only system with a negative median EOT latency is the RMS VAD (−117 ms), and its FPR is far outside the budget. No in-budget system comes near the human median of −151 ms.

The comparison is fair in one important way: the dev-tuned operating point is applied to every probabilistic system under the same commit rule. It is uneven in two others. Some systems are fine-tuned on TurnBench data and others are scored zero-shot. Full-duplex latency also includes response generation and, for Gemini, network delay.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is resource and protocol, not modelling. The corpus is new in three ways. Conversation type is controlled at recording time, every segment has three annotators, and the label set derives from conversation analysis. Per Table I, no prior annotated resource combines several registers, linguistically grounded labels and model evaluation. The scoring design is also careful: excluded intervals for ambiguous events, negative spans truncated at contrary evidence, and causal commit times with no threshold sweep at test time. All of these make heterogeneous systems comparable. The baselines are existing systems; the WavLM predictors are standard frozen-frontend classifiers trained for comparison. The type-dependence of interruption false positives is the main new empirical finding. Its link to backchannel density is a correlational reading of Tables III and IV, not a controlled ablation.

## Field Significance

Moderate — TurnBench provides a shared, linguistically grounded test bed in which endpointers, turn-taking predictors and full-duplex speech LMs are scored on the same annotations. It shows that, under one protocol, no tested system is fast, selective and high-recall at once. It also shows that the conversation register, not only the system, determines how often interruption detectors fire falsely. The authors state that the paper is accepted to SLT 2026; this page summarizes the arXiv preprint.

## Claims

- **complicates:** Turn-taking detection performance measured on a single conversational register does not transfer uniformly to other registers; interruption false alarms rise sharply in backchannel-dense conversation even when end-of-turn recall stays stable.
  > *Evidence:* Across six recorded conversation types, VAP's EOT recall ranges only 0.82-0.88, while Casual INT FPR is higher than Argumentative INT FPR for all 12 systems with an INT track (e.g. OpenAI Server VAD 0.54 vs 0.38; WavLM-Base causal 0.17 vs 0.08). Casual has the highest backchannel rate (5.2 BC/min vs 3.09). *(§IV-B, §VII, Table III, Table IV)*
- **supports:** Acoustic-only endpointing that commits on silence or energy is over-eager, and adding linguistic or predictive modelling is needed to keep false positives low in spoken dialogue.
  > *Evidence:* The RMS VAD (0.632 EOT FPR) and OpenAI Server VAD (0.525 EOT FPR) far exceed the 0.1 budget, while semantic endpointers, voice activity projection and supervised predictors stay near or under it. The two OpenAI modes differ only in endpointing, and their EOT FPR drops from 0.525 to 0.018. *(§VII-a, Table IV)*
- **supports:** Interruption detection in spoken dialogue trades decision latency against selectivity, because an interruption cannot be told apart from a backchannel at its onset.
  > *Evidence:* Onset-driven systems commit quickly but either fire on backchannels (OpenAI Server VAD, 0.458 INT FPR) or miss interruptions (SmartTurn v3, 0.107 recall at 159 ms). VAP, Mimi-EP and WavLM-Large (anchor) wait about 1.0-1.4 s and reach 0.87-0.95 recall at 0.05-0.11 FPR. *(§VII-b, Table IV)*
- **complicates:** Current full-duplex spoken dialogue models do not take turns at human-like timing; they respond late or rarely, while humans begin turn transfers before the current turn ends.
  > *Evidence:* Humans start smooth floor transfers a median 151 ms before turn end. Gemini 3.1 Live reaches 0.657 EOT recall with 1234 ms median latency, and Moshi only 0.233 EOT recall, falling silent over long sessions. No in-budget system has a negative median EOT latency. *(§IV-B, §VII, Table IV)*
- **refines:** Turn-taking benchmark labels are reliable when they derive from conversation-analytic categories with multi-annotator consensus, but the main disagreement concentrates on whether overlapping speech claims the floor.
  > *Evidence:* Frame-level Fleiss' κ is 0.78 and onset boundary F1 is 0.94-0.96, yet only 83.5-84.9% of annotator events survive consensus in high-overlap types, against 87.8-89.1% in the others. Most dropped events agree on timing but differ between turn, interruption and backchannel. *(§IV-A, §IV-B, Table III)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- All dialogues are English, recorded in a studio and dyadic. Behaviour on telephone or far-field audio, multi-party talk and other languages is untested (§VIII).
- Full-duplex models are scored on EOT only. Their decisions come from VAD onsets in their output audio, so their latency mixes turn-taking with response generation (and network delay for Gemini). The authors state that they have no method yet for scoring interruptions in full-duplex models (§VI-D, §VIII).
- Majority consensus drops events that annotators disagree on. These are concentrated in overlapping speech, the hardest cases for interruption detection. The per-annotator tracks are released but not analysed (§IV-A, §VIII).
- Training conditions differ across baselines: some are fine-tuned on the TurnBench training set, others run as-is. Rankings therefore mix the effect of in-domain data with the effect of model design (§VI).
- The voice actors receive a conversation type and topic, so the dialogues are elicited rather than naturally occurring. Their tempo and gap distributions are close to Switchboard, but the share of overlapping floor transfers is higher (64% vs 43%) (§IV-B).
- The benchmark measures only detection and timing. It does not assess what a dialogue system says or how its speech sounds once it takes the floor.
