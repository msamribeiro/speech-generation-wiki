> [!abstract] NeurIPS · 2017 · Conference
> **van den Oord et al.** (DeepMind) · [→ Paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/hash/7a98af17e63a0ac09ce2e96d03992fbc-Abstract.html) · [→ Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00937) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✗
>
> Introduces the VQ-VAE, an autoencoder whose encoder output is snapped to the nearest entry of a learned codebook, so that raw waveforms (and images and video) are represented as sequences of discrete tokens that a separate autoregressive prior can model.

## Problem

Variational autoencoders with continuous Gaussian latents train well, but discrete latents had been hard to learn: estimators such as NVIL, VIMCO and Gumbel-softmax relaxations suffer from high gradient variance or bias, and they had mostly been tested on small datasets with low-dimensional latents. A second problem is "posterior collapse": when a VAE has a powerful autoregressive decoder (PixelCNN, WaveNet), the decoder can model the data alone and the latents are ignored. The authors argue that discrete codes suit speech and language, which are naturally symbolic, and that a latent space should capture structure spanning many samples (phonemes, objects) rather than local noise.

## Method

The encoder maps the input to a grid of D-dimensional vectors z_e(x): 1D for speech, 2D for images, 3D for video. Each vector is replaced by its nearest neighbour in a learned embedding table (codebook) of K vectors, and the decoder reconstructs the input from these quantized vectors z_q(x). The posterior is therefore a deterministic one-hot categorical distribution. With a uniform prior during training, the KL term of the ELBO is a constant (log K) and is dropped (§3.1).

![Figure 1: Left: A figure describing the VQ-VAE. Right: Visualisation of the embedding space. The output of the encoder z ( x ) is mapped to the nearest point e 2 . The gradient ∇ z L (in red) will push the encoder to change its output, which could alter the configuration in the next forward pass.](assets/1711.00937/figure-1.png)

The nearest-neighbour lookup has no gradient, so the gradient of the reconstruction loss is copied straight from the decoder input to the encoder output (a straight-through estimator). The training loss has three terms (§3.2, Eq. 3):

- a reconstruction term, which trains the encoder and decoder;
- a vector quantisation (VQ) term, ‖sg[z_e(x)] − e‖², which moves codebook vectors toward the encoder outputs (sg is stop-gradient);
- a commitment term, β‖z_e(x) − sg[e]‖², which keeps encoder outputs close to their assigned codes. β = 0.25 in all experiments, and results did not vary for β between 0.1 and 2.0.

Appendix A.1 describes an alternative codebook update with exponential moving averages (γ = 0.99), but the paper's experiments use the loss term. After the autoencoder is trained, a separate autoregressive prior is fitted over the discrete codes: a PixelCNN for images and a WaveNet for audio (§3.3). New samples come from ancestral sampling of the prior followed by decoding. Joint training of prior and autoencoder is left to future work.

For audio (§4.3), the decoder is a dilated convolutional network similar to WaveNet, conditioned on the discrete latents and a one-hot speaker embedding. On VCTK (109 speakers), the encoder uses 6 strided convolutions (stride 2, window 4). This gives a single latent feature map 64x shorter than the waveform, with a codebook of K = 512. For the prior experiments on 460 LibriSpeech speakers, the latent sequence is 128x shorter than the waveform. A WaveNet prior is then trained on 40,960-sample chunks (2.56 s, 320 latent steps). The phoneme analysis uses a third configuration: 128 codes at 25 Hz (downsampling factor 640). Model sizes are not reported.

## Key Results

All audio results except one are qualitative and are presented as listening samples on the demo page.

- **Likelihood (CIFAR10, §4.1):** With the same encoder-decoder architecture, the VQ-VAE reaches 4.67 bits/dim, compared with 4.51 for a continuous VAE and 5.14 for VIMCO with discrete latents. All three values are lower bounds, and lower is better. The VQ-VAE is therefore 0.16 bits/dim worse than the continuous VAE but 0.47 bits/dim better than the discrete-latent VIMCO baseline.
- **Content-preserving speech reconstruction (VCTK, §4.3, Figure 6):** Reconstructions from the 64x-compressed codes keep the spoken content, but the waveform differs and prosody changes. The authors read this as evidence that the codes keep content and drop low-level detail.
- **Unconditional speech from a latent prior (LibriSpeech, §4.3):** A WaveNet prior over the discrete codes produces samples that, according to the authors, contain clear words and part-sentences. They contrast this with the "babbling" of an unconditional sample-level WaveNet. No listening test or objective metric backs this comparison.
- **Speaker conversion (§4.3, Figure 6):** Codes are extracted from one speaker's utterance and decoded with a different speaker ID. The authors report that the content is preserved in the new voice. There are no speaker-similarity, intelligibility or naturalness measurements.
- **Phoneme alignment of codes (§4.3):** The latent codes are compared with ground-truth phoneme sequences that were never used in training. Each of the 128 codes is mapped to its most likely phoneme, giving 49.3% accuracy on 41-way phoneme classification, against 7.2% for a random latent space. The paper does not say which speech corpus this analysis uses.
- **Images and video (§4.2, §4.4):** 128x128x3 ImageNet images are compressed to 32x32 latents (K = 512, about 42.6x fewer bits) with only slightly blurrier reconstructions. A PixelCNN prior over these latents generates new images that the decoder maps back to pixels. A two-stage VQ-VAE with a PixelCNN decoder still uses its latents, the setting where the authors note VAEs usually suffer posterior collapse. Action-conditioned DeepMind Lab video is generated entirely in latent space.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is architectural. It combines nearest-neighbour vector quantization, a straight-through gradient, and codebook and commitment losses into a discrete-latent autoencoder that trains without the high-variance estimators earlier discrete VAEs needed. It also separates training into two stages: first a quantized autoencoder, then an autoregressive prior over its codes. Each ingredient existed before (VQ for dictionary learning, the straight-through estimator, autoregressive priors in VAEs). What is new is the combination and the demonstration on large images, video and raw waveforms.

For speech, the evidence is thin by later standards. Apart from the phoneme-mapping probe, the audio claims (content preservation, word-like unconditional samples, speaker conversion) rest on samples and the authors' descriptions, with no MOS, WER or speaker-similarity numbers. The one quantitative comparison with continuous latents is on CIFAR10 images, not audio. The audio model is also not designed or evaluated as a codec in the compression sense: there is no bitrate-quality analysis, and its reconstructions deliberately alter prosody.

## Field Significance

> [!important]
> Foundational — Introduces a learned vector-quantized bottleneck that turns raw speech into a short sequence of discrete codes. A separate autoregressive model can then generate those codes, and a waveform decoder renders them back to audio. The paper also shows that such codes, learned without labels, partly align with phonemes. When conditioned on speaker identity, they can be decoded in another voice, which points toward discrete-token speech generation and unsupervised content-speaker separation.

## Claims

- **supports:** Vector quantization with a straight-through gradient estimator can learn discrete latent representations whose reconstruction quality approaches that of continuous-latent autoencoders.
  > *Evidence:* On CIFAR10 with matched encoder-decoder architectures, VQ-VAE reaches 4.67 bits/dim against 4.51 for a continuous VAE and 5.14 for VIMCO with discrete latents (all lower bounds). *(§4.1)*
- **supports:** Discrete codes learned from raw speech by reconstruction alone, without linguistic labels, partially align with phonetic units.
  > *Evidence:* Mapping each of 128 codes (25 Hz) to its most likely phoneme gives 49.3% accuracy on a 41-way phoneme classification, compared with 7.2% for a random latent space; phoneme labels were never used in training. *(§4.3)*
- **supports:** Modelling speech with an autoregressive prior over a compressed discrete latent sequence captures longer-range linguistic structure than autoregressive modelling of raw samples.
  > *Evidence:* A WaveNet prior trained on 2.56 s chunks of 128x-compressed latents (320 steps) from 460 LibriSpeech speakers produces samples the authors describe as containing clear words and part-sentences, whereas unconditional sample-level WaveNet output sounds like babbling; the comparison is qualitative only. *(§4.3)*
- **supports:** A strongly compressed discrete speech bottleneck paired with an explicit speaker-conditioned decoder can retain linguistic content while discarding speaker identity, enabling voice conversion by swapping the speaker condition.
  > *Evidence:* Codes extracted from one VCTK speaker and decoded with another speaker's one-hot ID keep the content in the second voice, as presented through audio samples and a waveform comparison; no conversion metrics are reported. *(§4.3, Figure 6)*
- **complicates:** Aggressively downsampled discrete speech representations trade fine acoustic and prosodic detail for abstraction, so reconstructions keep content but are not faithful to the original signal.
  > *Evidence:* With 64x temporal compression on VCTK, reconstructions keep the text content, but the waveform differs substantially and the prosody of the voice changes. *(§4.3, Figure 6)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

The most important limitation for speech is that the audio evaluation is almost entirely qualitative. Reconstruction, unconditional generation and speaker conversion are shown through demo samples, a waveform figure and the authors' descriptions, with no MOS, WER, speaker-similarity or listening-test results. These speech claims cannot be compared quantitatively with other systems from the paper alone.

- The only quantitative audio result is the phoneme-mapping probe, and the paper does not name the corpus it uses. The authors note that a more advanced mapping, for example over code bigrams or trigrams, could give higher accuracy, so 49.3% is a rough measure of alignment (footnote to §4.3).
- The three audio configurations differ in downsampling and codebook size: 64x with K = 512, 128x, and a factor of 640 with K = 128. No configuration is evaluated across all experiments, and the size-quality trade-off is not studied.
- The likelihood comparison with continuous latents is only on CIFAR10 images. Even there, the VQ-VAE bound is slightly worse than the continuous VAE's.
- The prior is trained after the autoencoder and kept separate, and the authors leave joint training to future work (§3.3). Codebook usage and collapse are not analysed.
- Speaker information is removed only implicitly, through compression and the speaker-conditioned decoder. No explicit disentanglement objective or ablation shows how much speaker information remains in the codes.
- No model sizes, training durations or code release are reported, which limits reproducibility.
