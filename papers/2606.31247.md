> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Jiaqi Li et al.** (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen / ByteDance) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31247) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✓
>
> FlexiSLM is a 7B thinker-talker spoken language model that represents both input and output speech at dynamic frame rates, and lets the user set the average output frame rate (12.5 Hz down to 4.0 Hz) at inference time without retraining.

## Problem

Existing spoken language models tokenize speech at a fixed frame rate (12.5 Hz for Kimi-Audio, 25 Hz in and 50 Hz out for Qwen2.5-Omni). A fixed rate spends the same compute on silences and redundant stretches as on dense speech, and it gives no way to trade quality for speed at deployment time. Dynamic-frame-rate tokenization, which merges redundant frames, had been shown in a codec and in a small (0.3B) TTS pipeline (FlexiCodec), but not inside an end-to-end speech-in, speech-out language model. There, both the efficiency payoff and the difficulty are larger. Controlling the frame rate through a merging threshold is also indirect: the same threshold gives very different rates across utterances.

## Method

FlexiSLM is a parallel speech-text, thinker-talker model. An audio encoder (the pretrained Qwen2.5-Omni encoder, 640M parameters, 25 Hz features) feeds an **input-side Frame Merging Module**, which compresses the features into a dynamic-rate sequence at or below 12.5 Hz. The module computes cosine similarity between neighbouring frames and greedily averages runs whose similarity exceeds a threshold τ. It then interleaves original and averaged frames and passes them through a small local-attention Transformer (20M parameters), keeping the outputs at the averaged positions. The module copies FlexiCodec's merging mechanism but is trained from scratch inside FlexiSLM. The merged sequence goes to the Thinker, an LLM initialized from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.

![Figure 2: Overall architecture of FlexiSLM. Input speech is encoded into continuous features, compressed by the input-side Frame Merging Module into dynamic-rate representations, and processed by the LLM Backbone. The Talker Transformer then predicts FlexiCodec semantic FSQ tokens and their frame lengths conditioned on the target frame rate, enabling controllable dynamic-rate speech generation. The input-side Frame Merging Module shares FlexiCodec's implementation but uses separate parameters. We use public pretrained weights for the FlexiCodec and flow matching audio decoder.](assets/2606.31247/figure-2.png)

On the output side, a 630M-parameter autoregressive **Talker** Transformer reads the Thinker's last-layer hidden states and predicts two parallel streams through two LM heads: FlexiCodec semantic FSQ tokens (the codec's RVQ acoustic tokens are not used) and a frame-length token for each speech token. The FSQ stream is delayed 5 tokens behind the text stream so that speech never runs ahead of its text. The length stream is delayed one further position, so each duration is predicted after its token. Speech is reconstructed by FlexiCodec's frozen, pretrained non-autoregressive flow-matching mel decoder (Voicebox-style, conditioned on a reference prompt) followed by a Vocos vocoder at 24 kHz. An optional Talker-to-Thinker connection, borrowed from DrVoice, feeds the emitted speech-token embeddings back into the Thinker.

The key new mechanism is **direct frame-rate conditioning**. In training, merging thresholds are sampled at random (τ ∼ U(0.85, 1.0) for output; input target rates from U(4, 12.5) Hz). The empirical average frame rate that results for each utterance is encoded with a sinusoidal embedding and given to the Talker and the merging module as a condition. At inference the user supplies the target rate directly instead of tuning τ. The input frame rate is set deterministically by picking a per-utterance threshold that hits the target frame count.

Training has three stages, all with cross-entropy on text, speech-token and frame-length streams (weights 2, 1, 1):

1. Talker pre-training on about 100K hours of English TTS data (Emilia and MLS), with the Thinker frozen.
2. Multi-task LoRA fine-tuning of the Thinker, together with the new input merging module and the Talker. The mixture covers speech-to-speech dialogue, text dialogue, TTS, ASR and audio understanding.
3. Full-parameter fine-tuning with the Talker-to-Thinker connection enabled.

The main dialogue corpus, FlexiSLM-Data (1.4M samples, 9.9K hours), is synthetic. Text prompts from public QA and instruction datasets were answered by Qwen3-Omni-30B. The answers were voiced by Qwen3-TTS with one fixed speaker, and the user prompts by Fish-Audio TTS with Emilia speaker prompts. Samples were then filtered with an LLM correctness check and a Whisper WER check (WER ≤ 20%).

## Key Results

**Spoken QA and ASR (Table 3, Kimi-Audio-Evalkit, GPT-5.5 as judge).** At 12.5 Hz in and out, FlexiSLM-Stage3 has an Overall s2t/s2s score of 72.4/67.2. Both values are the highest among the 7B models in the table: Mimo-Audio-7B 70.6/59.0, Kimi-Audio-7B 69.7/57.2, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B 66.7/63.3, Qwen2-Audio-7B 54.9/–. Halving only the output rate to 6.25 Hz gives 72.3/66.2. At 6.25 Hz in and out it reaches 70.2/64.3, still the best s2s among 7B models but slightly below Mimo-Audio on s2t (70.6). Larger models remain ahead on s2s: Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B scores 76.5/69.7 and Fun-Audio-Chat-8B 74.5/67.3. ASR is FlexiSLM's weak spot. Its LibriSpeech clean/other WER at 12.5 Hz is 1.98/5.79, against 1.80/2.45 for Kimi-Audio, 2.38/4.21 for Qwen2.5-Omni and 1.74/4.04 for Qwen2-Audio. Lower rates degrade smoothly: Overall falls to 69.0/60.4 at 5.0 Hz and 67.2/56.5 at 4.0 Hz, and ASR WER rises to 4.47/9.53 at 4.0 Hz. Stage 3 improves the Overall scores over Stage 2 at all four shared operating points.

**Frame-rate control accuracy (Table 4).** With threshold control, τ = 0.90 gives dataset means between 7.91 and 8.34 Hz and per-utterance rates between 3.91 and 10.74 Hz (σ 0.40–0.78). With direct control, the mean lands within 0.01 Hz of the 6.25 or 4.0 Hz target on all four benchmarks, with σ 0.03–0.06.

**Efficiency (Tables 5–6, single A100, batch size 1).** Halving the output rate from 12.5 to 6.25 Hz roughly halves the real-time factor: 1.17 → 0.59 with 12.5 Hz input, and 1.09 → 0.57 with 6.25 Hz input. It also cuts first-1-second-chunk latency from 1.62 s to 1.21 s. Lowering only the input rate helps much less (1.17 → 1.09). Qwen2.5-Omni-7B has an RTF of 1.57, so FlexiSLM is 1.3× faster at 12.5/12.5 Hz and about 2.7× faster with 6.25 Hz output. The estimated model-side compute drops from 5.26 TFLOPs for Qwen2.5-Omni to 2.73 TFLOPs for FlexiSLM at 6.25/6.25 Hz. This saving is far smaller than the 50 Hz vs 6.25 Hz ratio, because FlexiSLM's Thinker keeps running over the full speech-length sequence.

**Ablations at 6.25 Hz, Stage 2 budget (Table 7).**
- Replacing dynamic output merging with uniform pairwise merging lowers s2s from 63.0 to 61.0 and raises TTS WER from 3.11 to 4.95.
- Uniform input merging lowers s2t from 68.7 to 67.5 and raises ASR test-other WER from 7.20 to 7.97; s2s is barely affected.
- Threshold-controlled output instead of direct rate conditioning lowers s2s to 61.7 and raises TTS WER to 3.53.

In the appendix (Table 9), removing the input merging Transformer makes ASR collapse (test-clean 2.92 → 6.45 WER).

**Fixed-rate alternatives (Table 10).** A non-controllable 6.25 Hz grouped-patching variant, which predicts two 12.5 Hz FlexiCodec tokens per step, beats the dynamic model on every metric: OpenAudioBench s2t/s2s 66.3/63.3 vs 65.0/61.0, TTS WER 2.70 vs 3.11. A FlexiCodec retrained at a fixed 6.25 Hz is worse than the dynamic model on s2s (60.7) and TTS WER (4.47).

**Speech generation and understanding (Tables 16–17).**
- TTS WER on LibriSpeech-PC is 2.14% at 12.5 Hz, the lowest in Table 16 (CosyVoice 3.20, Qwen2.5-Omni 3.18, Qwen3-Omni 3.34, Mimo-Audio 14.72). It rises to 2.87% at 6.25 Hz and 4.16% at 5.0 Hz.
- Dialogue-response WER is 4.52% at 12.5 Hz and 4.41% at 8.0 Hz, against 4.32% for Qwen3-Omni and 6.33% for Qwen2.5-Omni.
- Average accuracy on the LLaSO-Eval audio-understanding tasks is 65.8% at 12.5 Hz, the highest in Table 17 (LLaSO-3B 58.3%, Qwen2.5-Omni 48.6%, Gemini 2.5-Pro 48.3%). It stays between 63.4% and 64.7% down to 4.0 Hz input.

**Human listening test (Table 11; 8 expert listeners, 10 prompts).** The overall MOS (mean of naturalness, fidelity and intelligibility) is 4.22 at 12.5 Hz, 4.21 at 6.25 Hz and 4.10 for Mimo-Audio-7B. Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (4.79) and Qwen3-Omni-30B (4.81) are well ahead, mostly on fidelity: 4.79 for both, against 3.94 for FlexiSLM at 12.5 Hz. At 4.0 Hz naturalness falls to 3.69 and fidelity to 3.66, while intelligibility holds at 4.21.

**Contamination check (Appendix D, Tables 12–13).** Exact prompt overlap with the training data is 20% for Llama Questions and 36.35% for SD-QA, and at most 1.5% elsewhere. After removing the overlapping prompts, the 12.5/12.5 Hz s2s Overall falls from 67.2 to 65.9 and every system keeps its rank. FlexiSLM at 6.25/6.25 Hz (62.6) stays above Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (62.4).

## Novelty Assessment

The authors state that the main contribution is system-level integration, and that reading is accurate. The dynamic-rate output tokens, the frame-merging mechanism and the flow-matching decoder all come from FlexiCodec. The thinker-talker layout follows Qwen2.5-Omni, and the Talker-to-Thinker feedback follows DrVoice. Two pieces are genuinely new. The first is **direct frame-rate conditioning**: the Talker and merging module are conditioned on a sinusoidal embedding of the realized average frame rate instead of a merging threshold. This new conditioning and inference procedure is measurably more accurate (Table 4) and gives better generation than threshold control (Table 7). The second is a trainable dynamic merging module on the input side of an SLM, whose local-attention Transformer the ablations show to be essential. The paper also documents the cost of controllability: a fixed 6.25 Hz grouped-patching variant is stronger at its single operating point (Table 10). Its own human test shows audio quality well behind the Qwen-Omni models.

Some of the headline comparisons favour FlexiSLM. The spoken-QA scores come from an LLM judge, and the training data is distilled from Qwen3-Omni, which is also a baseline. The deduplication re-evaluation addresses the most obvious leakage concern.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper presents what the authors describe as the first spoken language model whose speech frame rate is dynamic on both input and output and can be set by the user at inference time. It shows that a single 7B model can run between 12.5 and 4.0 Hz and that halving the output rate roughly halves inference time with little change in benchmark or listening-test scores. It turns dynamic-rate tokenization from a codec-level result into a deployment knob for speech LMs. However, the gains are system-level, and its absolute speech quality trails stronger fixed-rate models.

## Claims

- **supports:** Dynamic frame-rate speech tokens let a spoken language model generate speech at substantially lower average frame rates with little loss in spoken-dialogue quality.
  > *Evidence:* Halving FlexiSLM's output rate from 12.5 to 6.25 Hz changes the s2s Overall score from 67.2 to 66.2 and the listening-test MOS from 4.22 to 4.21, while RTF drops from 1.17 to 0.59. *(§4.2, Table 3; §4.3.2, Table 5; §Appendix C, Table 11)*
- **supports:** Content-adaptive frame merging preserves speech-generation quality better than uniform downsampling at the same average token rate.
  > *Evidence:* At 6.25 Hz, replacing similarity-based output merging with uniform pairwise merging lowers s2s Overall from 63.0 to 61.0 and raises TTS WER from 3.11 to 4.95. A FlexiCodec retrained at a fixed 6.25 Hz also gives higher TTS WER (4.47) than the dynamic model (3.11). *(§4.4, Table 7; §Appendix B, Table 10)*
- **refines:** Conditioning a speech generator directly on the target average frame rate controls token rate more precisely than a similarity threshold, and it gives the generator a less ambiguous target.
  > *Evidence:* Direct frame-rate control gives per-benchmark mean rates within 0.01 Hz of the target with σ ≤ 0.06, against a 7.91–8.34 Hz spread of means and σ up to 0.78 for τ = 0.90. Threshold-controlled output lowers s2s from 63.0 to 61.7 and raises TTS WER from 3.11 to 3.53 in the ablation. *(§4.3.1, Table 4; §4.4, Table 7)*
- **complicates:** Below about 6 tokens per second, speech generation from dynamic-rate tokens degrades mainly through duration prediction and loss of local phonetic and prosodic detail, not through token prediction.
  > *Evidence:* The audio-token loss stays at 3.50–3.59 across rates, while frame-length top-1 accuracy falls from 29% at 6.25 Hz to 19% at 4.0 Hz. At 4.0 Hz, naturalness and fidelity MOS drop to 3.69 and 3.66 while intelligibility stays at 4.21. *(§Appendix E, Table 15; §Appendix C, Table 11)*
- **complicates:** Inference-time frame-rate controllability costs quality relative to a model optimized for a single fixed low rate.
  > *Evidence:* A non-controllable 6.25 Hz grouped-patching variant beats the controllable dynamic model on every reported metric: OpenAudioBench s2t/s2s 66.3/63.3 vs 65.0/61.0, ASR test-other 5.63 vs 6.70, TTS WER 2.70 vs 3.11. *(§Appendix B, Table 10)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] Audio quality and the controllability trade-off
> FlexiSLM's output speech is clearly less natural and lower in fidelity than the Qwen-Omni models in the authors' own listening test (overall MOS 4.22 vs 4.79–4.81). At 6.25 Hz the controllable dynamic model is also beaten on every metric by a non-controllable grouped-patching variant, so the benefit of controllability has to be weighed against absolute quality.

- **Small listening test.** The MOS study uses 8 listeners with speech or audio research experience and only 10 prompts. Confidence intervals for the FlexiSLM settings are ±0.20–0.26.
- **LLM-judged benchmarks and distilled data.** Spoken-QA scores are judged by GPT-5.5. The dialogue training data is distilled from Qwen3-Omni, which is also a baseline, and voiced by Qwen3-TTS with one fixed speaker. Benchmark overlap is substantial for SD-QA (36%) and Llama Questions (20%). Rankings survive deduplication, but absolute scores drop.
- **ASR lags.** LibriSpeech test-other WER (5.79 at 12.5 Hz) is worse than every 7B baseline that reports ASR.
- **Non-streaming.** Both the input merging module and the audio decoder are non-causal. The first-chunk latency figures come from a modified chunk-emission setup, not a streaming model.
- **Smaller compute savings than the rate ratio implies.** The Thinker keeps processing a speech-length sequence, so the saving against Qwen2.5-Omni is smaller than the ratio of frame rates suggests.
- **Scope.** The model is English-centric, single-turn and has no preference or RL post-training. The authors expect such post-training could close part of the quality gap.
- **Open question.** It is not yet known whether direct rate conditioning, combined with better duration modelling, can make operating points of 5 Hz and below competitive.
