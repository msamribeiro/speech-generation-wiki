> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Thonet et al.** (NAVER LABS Europe) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01246) · Demo: ? · Code: ✓
>
> Frames TTS-friendly response generation as a preference alignment problem for the text LLM in a cascade voice assistant, and supplies two preference datasets plus a heuristic metric that agrees with TTS->ASR round-trip errors and MUSHRA ratings of the synthesized responses.

## Problem

LLMs are trained mostly on written text and preferences, so their answers contain symbols, abbreviations, raw URLs, email addresses and compact numeric notation ("$3.45", "1/2 tsp.") that a TTS front end may read badly. The usual fix is a post-hoc text normalization stage, which the authors argue adds latency, needs complete sentences before it can run, and is tied to the tokenization of a specific TTS system. Prior work on preference alignment for speech mostly optimizes the TTS acoustic model itself; the closest text-side work (speechworthy instruction tuning) targets broad speech suitability and mixes helpfulness and speechworthiness in its human labels. No public benchmark existed for TTS-friendly text generation.

## Method

The paper defines TTS-friendliness through a short rule set and builds two English preference datasets of (context, chosen, rejected) tuples: CORA, a synthetic coffee-ordering assistant dataset (262 tuples) built around prices, times, acronyms, emails and URLs, and Recipe, 300 recipes sampled from RecipeNLG for procedural text. Each dataset is split into five random folds of 100 train tuples, with 81/81 (CORA) or 100/100 (Recipe) validation/test tuples (§3, Table 1). Experiments use either 10 or all 100 training tuples.

The alignment method is FaST (Feature-aware Sampling and Tuning), adopted unchanged from the authors' earlier personalized-alignment work. GPT-5.1 is prompted with the training tuples to discover 40 interpretable features (for example `use_of_numeric_formatting`, `abbreviation_density`, `natural_conversational_tone`). Qwen3-4B, prompted as a frozen feature function, scores every response 1 to 5 per feature. A Feature-aware Reward Model (FaRM) is a linear combination of these scores with one learned weight per feature, fit with a Bradley-Terry objective. The generation model (Qwen3-4B with reasoning disabled; SmolLM3-3B in App. F.1) is then trained by iterative rejection-sampling fine-tuning: sample candidates, rank them with FaRM, fine-tune on the top ones (§4.1). Baselines are Zeroshot, a TTS-aware Prompting baseline, SFT on the chosen responses, DPO, GRPO with a conventional 4B reward model (GRPO-RM), and RFT with that reward model (RFT-RM); the chosen responses serve as an Oracle (§4.2).

The evaluation suite has three parts (§3, App. C). A heuristic TTS-friendliness score (1 to 5) counts six regex classes (all-caps acronyms, numbers, URL-like strings, mixed alphanumeric-punctuation tokens, symbols, equations), weights and length-normalizes them, and maps the risk to 1 to 5. A TTS->ASR round trip synthesizes each response with Kyutai Pocket TTS (voice `alba`, 24 kHz, CPU) and transcribes it with Wav2Vec2-large; CER and WER against the original text measure how speakable the text is. Helpfulness (1 to 5) is judged by GPT-4o-mini, reference-based on Recipe and requirement-decomposed and reference-free on CORA. Finally, a MUSHRA-style test on Prolific holds the TTS voice fixed and asks raters to judge the listenability of the spoken message, not the audio quality; the 3.5 kHz anchor is removed because the independent variable is the text (§7.2, App. A).

## Key Results

**Preference prediction (Table 2).** With 100 training tuples the conventional RM is slightly more accurate than FaRM (0.996 vs 0.984 on CORA, 0.998 vs 0.914 on Recipe). With 10 tuples FaRM holds 0.96 on CORA while the RM drops to 0.871; on Recipe the two are close (0.941 FaRM vs 0.946 RM). FaRM has 40 learned parameters against 4B for the RM.

**Generation trade-off (Figure 1).** The headline comparison is a scatter of heuristic TTS-friendliness against helpfulness, so most generation results are reported graphically rather than as table values. The authors report that FaST lies on or near the Pareto frontier in nearly all conditions and that GRPO-RM and RFT-RM are consistently below it. On Recipe, SFT beats FaST on TTS-friendliness in the full-data setting and Prompting beats it in the 10-sample setting (§6.2). With 100 tuples SFT is a strong competitor; FaST's advantage is clearest with 10 tuples, where SFT degrades on Recipe. With SmolLM3-3B, FaST degrades on Recipe in the 10-sample setting (App. F.1).

**Against text normalization (Table 5).** Applying PolyNorm to the Zeroshot output gives higher heuristic TTS-friendliness than FaST (4.92 vs 4.73 on CORA, 4.88 vs 4.56 on Recipe) at about double the latency (3.4 s vs 1.6 s on CORA, 8.5 s vs 4.4 s on Recipe). FaST has higher helpfulness than PolyNorm on CORA (4.86 vs 4.72) and roughly equal helpfulness on Recipe (2.74 vs 2.75). Zeroshot is highest on Recipe helpfulness (2.82).

**Metric validation (§7, Table 6).** On the first split's test set for five systems, FaST has the best heuristic score, CER and WER of the non-oracle systems on both datasets (CORA CER 0.077 and WER 0.297 against Zeroshot's 0.228 and 0.523; Recipe CER 0.066 and WER 0.253 against 0.170 and 0.420). The heuristic and TTS->ASR system rankings are identical on CORA and differ by one DPO/Prompting swap on Recipe. Utterance-level Spearman correlations are negative as expected, since higher heuristic scores should mean lower error: rho = -0.71 (CER) and -0.68 (WER) on CORA, -0.80 and -0.71 on Recipe. In the MUSHRA test (N = 14 raters after quality control, 20 CORA questions), the hidden Oracle scores 94.7, FaST 68.3, DPO 55.9 and Prompting 51.4. FaST is significantly above DPO (+12.4, p = 0.003) and Prompting (+16.9, p = 0.001) under paired Wilcoxon tests. The pooled Spearman correlation between the heuristic and the mean MUSHRA rating over 80 (utterance, system) pairs is +0.84 (App. A).

The comparison is fair within its design: all systems share the base model, data folds and decoding settings. However, the 10-tuple and 100-tuple regimes are small by construction, helpfulness rests on an LLM judge, and only one TTS engine and one ASR model are used.

## Novelty Assessment

The alignment algorithm is not new: FaST comes from the authors' earlier work, and the baselines are standard. The new parts are the task framing (TTS-friendliness as a preference objective for the response LLM, an alternative to a separate normalization stage), the two preference datasets, and the evaluation suite. The heuristic metric is a hand-calibrated regex score. What gives it weight is the validation against both a TTS->ASR round trip and human MUSHRA ratings of synthesized responses. Adapting MUSHRA to rate text content under a fixed voice is a useful protocol detail. The study is small (English, two narrow domains, 3B/4B models, one TTS engine), so it reads as a well-designed first benchmark more than a general result. The arXiv comment states that the paper was accepted to the EMNLP 2026 Main Conference.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper gives the text side of cascade voice assistants a concrete, measurable objective and shows that a cheap surface-pattern score tracks both round-trip intelligibility and listener judgments on one TTS engine. It also documents a latency trade-off between generating speakable text directly and normalizing it afterwards. The underlying alignment method is reused, so the contribution is mainly the benchmark and evaluation design.

## Claims

- **supports:** Aligning the response-generating LLM toward speakable surface forms improves the intelligibility of a cascade system's synthesized output without changing the TTS model.
  > *Evidence:* With Pocket TTS and Wav2Vec2-large held fixed, FaST-aligned Qwen3-4B responses reach round-trip CER 0.077 and WER 0.297 on CORA against 0.228 and 0.523 for the zero-shot model, and 0.066/0.253 against 0.170/0.420 on Recipe. *(§7.1, Table 6)*
- **supports:** Simple pattern-based text heuristics can serve as a reliable low-cost proxy for how intelligibly and naturally text is rendered by a TTS system.
  > *Evidence:* The regex heuristic yields the same system ranking as TTS->ASR CER on CORA and differs by one swap on Recipe. Its utterance-level Spearman correlation with CER is -0.71 (CORA) and -0.80 (Recipe), and its pooled correlation with mean MUSHRA listenability ratings is +0.84 over 80 utterance-system pairs. *(§7.1, Table 6; App. A)*
- **complicates:** Generating TTS-ready text directly trades some normalization quality for latency compared with a separate text normalization pass.
  > *Evidence:* PolyNorm applied after zero-shot generation reaches heuristic scores of 4.92 (CORA) and 4.88 (Recipe), against 4.73 and 4.56 for FaST. However, it roughly doubles per-response latency (3.4 s vs 1.6 s on CORA, 8.5 s vs 4.4 s on Recipe). *(§6.4, Table 5)*
- **complicates:** Reward signals learned from preferences that contrast speakable and written-style text can pick up verbosity as a spurious correlate of speakability.
  > *Evidence:* The learned features give negative weight to conciseness (-0.12, CORA) and brevity (-0.25, Recipe). FaST outputs are longer than most baselines (434.5 characters vs 221.0 for SFT on CORA, full data). Zeroing the conciseness weight cuts length to 335.6 characters with unchanged heuristic TTS-friendliness (4.74 vs 4.73). *(§Limitations; App. F.2, Table 10; App. F.3, Table 11)*
- **refines:** Listening tests can be adapted to evaluate the spoken suitability of generated text itself by holding the synthesizer and voice constant across conditions.
  > *Evidence:* The MUSHRA protocol keeps one Pocket TTS voice for all systems, removes the low-pass anchor, and asks raters to judge how naturally and intelligibly the spoken response answers the question. The hidden reference scores 94.7, and the three trained systems are separated with significant paired Wilcoxon contrasts (FaST vs DPO p = 0.003, FaST vs Prompting p = 0.001). *(§7.2, App. A)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> All speech-side evidence comes from one TTS engine (Kyutai Pocket TTS, one voice) and one ASR model. The MUSHRA study has 14 raters on 20 CORA questions, so the claim that TTS-friendly text helps across TTS systems is not tested.

- **Scope of data and models.** The study covers only English, two narrow domains (coffee ordering, recipes) built from scratch, and 3B/4B generation models; larger LLMs are untested.
- **Helpfulness is LLM-judged.** Helpfulness comes from GPT-4o-mini with a rubric, and Recipe helpfulness is low for all three systems in Table 5 (2.74 to 2.82). The human study tells raters not to judge correctness, so no human measure of helpfulness exists.
- **Heuristic design.** The regex weights and normalizers were set by inspection on a small held-out sample. The metric captures surface patterns (numbers, symbols, URLs), not prosodic or discourse-level suitability for speech. In the MUSHRA comparison the per-system correlation for Prompting is weaker (+0.54) and the Oracle correlation is non-significant because of a ceiling effect.
- **Mostly graphical results.** The main generation comparison (Figure 1) is a scatter plot, so per-method TTS-friendliness and helpfulness values for most baselines are not reported in tables.
- **Cascade only.** The framework assumes an LLM->TTS cascade and does not apply directly to end-to-end speech LMs that emit speech tokens; the authors leave that extension to future work.
