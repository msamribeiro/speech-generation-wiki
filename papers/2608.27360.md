> **Romolo Muletta et al.** (ZHAW School of Engineering) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27360) · Demo: ? · Code: ✓
>
> Turns the frozen XTTSv2 voice cloning model into a multilingual speaker anonymizer by feeding the source utterance's codebook tokens and a composite pseudo-speaker prompt to its GPT-2 backbone, then picking the best of several re-applications with a privacy-utility harmonic mean.

## Problem

Speaker anonymization (voice conversion of a source speaker to an artificial pseudo-speaker so that an automatic speaker verification system cannot link the output to the original) has been studied mostly in English. Multilingual systems exist (SSL-based language-independent anonymization, a GAN-based pseudo-speaker system extended to nine languages with multilingual ASR and TTS), but state-of-the-art pipelines require training several components (speaker encoders, content extractors, vocoders), which is a barrier for lower-resource languages. Prior multilingual work had also found that anonymization quality is largely bounded by the quality of the synthesis component, which suggests that a strong pretrained multilingual voice cloning model could do the job directly.

## Method

The system reuses XTTSv2 from the Coqui TTS library with no retraining. XTTSv2 consists of a VQ-VAE that encodes mel-spectrograms into discrete codebook indices, a Perceiver conditioner (six attention layers plus a Perceiver Resampler) that compresses reference audio into 32 speaker embeddings, a GPT-2 decoder-only transformer that autoregressively predicts codebook tokens, a BPE text tokenizer (6,681 tokens), and a HiFi-GAN decoder conditioned on an H/ASP speaker embedding. The key change is at inference: the VQ-VAE, normally used only during training, encodes the source utterance, and its codebook sequence is passed to the frozen GPT-2 backbone together with the pseudo-speaker embeddings and text tokens. The authors argue the source codebook carries prosodic structure (pitch contour, rhythm, energy) independently of speaker identity, so the backbone re-renders the same prosody in the target voice.

The pipeline per utterance: Whisper-Large-V3 transcribes the speech, the VQ-VAE extracts codebook tokens, and an ECAPA2 speaker embedding is computed. A binary gender classifier selects a language- and gender-specific pool of 10 reference speakers; for each pool speaker the utterance with the lowest ECAPA2 cosine similarity to the source is chosen, and the 10 utterances are concatenated and passed through the Perceiver conditioner (and the speaker encoder) to form a composite pseudo-speaker. The GPT-2 backbone produces a new codebook sequence and HiFi-GAN synthesizes the waveform. Pools are built from MLS training splits by anonymizing held-out utterances with each candidate as target and keeping the 10 per language and gender with the best trade-off of ECAPA2 cosine distance and WER (§2.4).

Because the backbone sometimes reverts to the source speaker mid-utterance (the authors attribute this, tentatively, to attention over the source codebook tokens), the pipeline is re-applied to its own output, and the iteration maximizing a harmonic mean H of (1 - WER) and (1 - Sim_orig) is kept, where Sim_orig is the cosine similarity between anonymized and original speaker embeddings (§2.3, Eq. 1). The paper does not state which ASR transcript serves as the WER reference inside H at inference time, nor the maximum number of iterations, though the reported selection distribution covers iterations 0 to 4. Model size is not reported.

## Key Results

Evaluation covers seven languages (de, en, es, fr, it, nl, pt) on CommonVoice 23.0 (8,784 speakers, 43,879 samples) and MLS (272 speakers, 34,442 samples), dev and test combined (Table 1). Baselines are SALT (WavLM latent-space pseudo-speakers) and MultiLingual (GAN pseudo-speakers with IMS Toucan TTS), both run from official implementations; all systems share Whisper-Large-V3 for WER and ECAPA2 for EER (Table 2; values read from the PDF because the parsed table was misaligned).

- **Privacy (EER, 0.5 = chance).** CommonVoice: proposed 0.49 average (0.48 to 0.50 per language) vs. MultiLingual 0.46 and SALT 0.37; the proposed system has the highest EER in every CommonVoice language. MLS: proposed 0.46 vs. MultiLingual 0.44 and SALT 0.30, but MultiLingual is higher on Italian (0.44 vs. 0.42) and Portuguese (0.45 vs. 0.44).
- **Intelligibility (WER).** CommonVoice: proposed 0.16 average vs. SALT 0.26 and MultiLingual 0.27, lowest in every language (from 0.07 Dutch to 0.28 English). MLS: SALT has the lowest average (0.13) and the lowest or tied-lowest WER in every language; the proposed system averages 0.16 and MultiLingual 0.17. Against MultiLingual on MLS, the proposed system is worse on German (0.15 vs. 0.14), tied on Spanish (0.08) and Dutch (0.24), and better on the other four.
- **Predicted quality (delta UTMOS, anonymized minus original).** CommonVoice: +0.17 vs. -0.74 (SALT) and -0.62 (MultiLingual); the positive value is attributed to clean synthesis replacing noisy crowd-sourced recordings. MLS: -0.35 vs. -1.29 and -1.23.
- **Human CMOS (English MLS, 30 utterances, 9 raters, -3 to +3 vs. original).** Proposed -0.90 (std 1.39), SALT -1.00 (std 1.12), MultiLingual -1.85 (std 0.88) (Table 3). The gap to SALT is small relative to the spread and no significance test is reported.
- **Iterative refinement.** Iteration 0 is chosen for 19.6% of utterances, iterations 1 to 3 each about 18%, iteration 4 for 25.0%. No ablation compares single-pass output with the refined output on EER or WER.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is a re-use recipe rather than a new model: every network component is the released XTTSv2 checkpoint, and the change is to route the VQ-VAE encoding of the source utterance into the GPT-2 input, build a composite pseudo-speaker prompt from a curated pool, and wrap generation in a best-of-iterations selection loop. Pseudo-speaker construction from multiple reference speakers and privacy-utility trade-off selection both have precedents in the anonymization literature; the new element is showing that a general-purpose multilingual zero-shot TTS model can serve as the conversion engine across seven languages without language-specific training. The engineering value is real (fewer trained components, immediate coverage of XTTSv2 languages), but the paper offers no analysis of why the codebook path preserves prosody while losing identity beyond the stated intuition.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper demonstrates that a frozen multilingual voice cloning model can be repurposed for speaker anonymization and evaluated across seven European languages with privacy near chance-level EER under the reported ASV setup. It is a practical data point linking zero-shot TTS and voice-conversion-based anonymization, with the caveat that the attacker model and the selection criterion share the evaluation tools.

## Claims

- **supports:** Pretrained multilingual zero-shot voice cloning models can perform voice-conversion-based speaker anonymization without retraining when the source utterance's discrete acoustic tokens are supplied alongside a pseudo-speaker prompt.
  > *Evidence:* Feeding XTTSv2's VQ-VAE codebook of the source speech plus a composite 10-speaker Perceiver prompt to the frozen GPT-2 backbone yields average EER 0.49 (CommonVoice) and 0.46 (MLS) across seven languages, above both trained baselines on average. *(§2.2, §4, Table 2)*
- **supports:** Anonymizers built on high-quality pretrained TTS synthesis preserve perceived and predicted speech quality better than dedicated anonymization pipelines.
  > *Evidence:* Delta UTMOS is +0.17 on CommonVoice and -0.35 on MLS for the XTTSv2 system vs. -0.74/-1.29 (SALT) and -0.62/-1.23 (MultiLingual); English CMOS is -0.90 vs. -1.00 and -1.85, though the margin over SALT is within one standard deviation. *(§4, Tables 2 and 3)*
- **complicates:** Intelligibility advantages of synthesis-based anonymization depend on recording conditions and do not hold uniformly against content-preserving latent-space methods.
  > *Evidence:* The XTTSv2 system has the lowest WER in every CommonVoice language, but on clean MLS audiobook speech SALT has the lowest average WER (0.13 vs. 0.16) and the lowest or tied-lowest value in every language. *(§4, Table 2)*
- **complicates:** Privacy measured by an ASV system that also drives pseudo-speaker selection and output selection may overstate protection against independent or informed attackers.
  > *Evidence:* ECAPA2 embeddings are used to pick the least similar reference utterances, to build the pseudo-speaker pools, and as the evaluation ASV; the iteration-selection score uses speaker-embedding similarity and WER, and adversarial robustness against informed attackers is left to future work. *(§2.2, §2.4, §3.4, §5)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] Evaluation and selection share models
> The same ECAPA2 model drives pseudo-speaker selection and pool construction and serves as the evaluation ASV, and the iterative selection criterion optimizes speaker-embedding dissimilarity and WER directly. Only an attacker that is not adapted to anonymized speech is evaluated, so the near-0.5 EER may not hold against a different ASV system or an informed attacker, which the authors acknowledge as future work.

- No ablation isolates the effect of iterative refinement, the composite multi-speaker prompt, or the source-codebook input on EER and WER; the claim that the codebook preserves prosody independently of identity is not tested with prosody or emotion metrics, even though the VoicePrivacy framing the paper cites includes emotion preservation.
- The human CMOS test covers only English, 30 utterances and 9 raters, with large standard deviations and no significance test.
- The method depends on Whisper transcripts for the text input, so ASR errors propagate into the content; how WER is referenced inside the selection criterion at inference time is not stated.
- Coverage is limited to seven European languages supported by XTTSv2; the authors list non-European languages and other voice cloning architectures as future work.
- Gender-based pool selection uses a binary gender classifier, and the iterative loop multiplies inference cost (up to five passes), which is not quantified.
