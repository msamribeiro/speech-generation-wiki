> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Zhang et al.** (Newcastle University / University of Oxford / Tencent / Shengrurenxin Ltd.) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07228) · [→ Peer-reviewed version](https://doi.org/10.1515/phon-2025-0072) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✗
>
> Replaces the phone-identity input of a FastSpeech acoustic model with 20 monovalent phonological features from the Featurally Underspecified Lexicon (FUL), shared across English and Mandarin, and tests in two listening experiments whether this theory-driven representation yields intelligible native, cross-lingual and code-mixed speech from small training sets.

*A [peer-reviewed version](https://doi.org/10.1515/phon-2025-0072) appeared in Phonetica (2026). This page, including its metrics and claims, summarizes the arXiv preprint.*

## Problem

Multilingual and code-mixed TTS front-ends usually rely on language-specific phone inventories (ARPABET for English, Pinyin for Mandarin), so each language needs its own symbol set and data cannot be pooled across languages at the segment level. Large codec-based systems avoid explicit phonemes but need very large per-language corpora, which low-resource languages cannot supply. Earlier work had fed phonological features to TTS (sets such as PHOIBLE, PanPhon, SPE-style or articulatory IPA features), but the authors note that the FUL framework, a minimal phonological theory developed for perception, acquisition and loanword research, had not been tested as a language-independent TTS input. The paper is positioned as a proof-of-concept feasibility study by phoneticians, published in a linguistics journal, rather than as a competitive TTS system; an earlier version of the work appeared as arXiv:2110.03609.

## Method

**Feature mapping.** English phones (ARPABET) are converted to SAMPA and Mandarin phones (Pinyin) to SAMPA-SC, and each SAMPA symbol is mapped to a binary vector over 20 FUL features (root, laryngeal, constriction, articulator, tongue height and tongue root classes). FUL features are monovalent (present or absent), consonants and vowels share place features, and only contrastive features are normally specified; here some optional features are also specified so that one feature set can distinguish both languages (§2.4, Tables 1 and 2). English uses 38 phones and Mandarin 37. The authors treat Mandarin alveolo-palatals as allophones of the dentals and give them identical features, and represent English diphthongs as vowel sequences. The SAMPA stage is optional: feature vectors can be written directly for languages without a SAMPA inventory (§3.1). The mapping is released on Zenodo.

**Acoustic model.** The system is the original non-autoregressive FastSpeech (FFT-block encoder and decoder, four blocks on each side) with only the input embedding changed (§3.2, Figure 2). The FUL feature vector is projected to 192 dimensions by a linear layer; lexical tone labels (four tones, neutral tone and silence) and punctuation-derived prosody labels each get a learnable 32-dimensional embedding; the three are concatenated into a 256-dimensional text embedding. A learnable 256-dimensional speaker-ID embedding is added before the encoder. Embedding width is kept identical to the original FastSpeech so that differences can be attributed to the input representation. Phone durations come from Kaldi forced alignment. The model has about 27M parameters, was trained for 200k steps (batch 24, one RTX 2080, FastSpeech optimiser settings), runs at RTF about 0.03 on GPU excluding the vocoder, and uses a pretrained MelGAN vocoder. Tone is a language-specific label, not a feature, so only segmental content is carried by the shared representation.

**Experiments.** Experiment 1 (small data) trains three models on studio data from one male English speaker (EN_MS1, 2.62 h, AI-assistant style) plus 0.5, 2 or 8 h from one male Mandarin speaker (CN_MS1, newsreader style): M0.5, M2, M8. Each speaker is monolingual in training but both voices are asked to synthesize both languages. Experiment 2 trains on a 100-hour proprietary corpus from 12 speakers (9 h English, 76 h Mandarin, 10 h Mandarin-English code-mixed, 5 h L2 English from Mandarin speakers) and evaluates three voices: CN_FS1 (female, 15 h including 2 h English and 3 h code-mixed), CN_MS2 (male, 9 h Mandarin only) and EN_MS1 (English only).

**Evaluation.** Both experiments use the same 13 sentences (5 Mandarin, 5 English, 3 code-mixed) rated for intelligibility on a 5-point scale (intelligibility MOS, distinct from naturalness MOS) without transcripts, online via PsyToolkit. English output was rated by 16 native English listeners and Mandarin and code-mixed output by 19 native Mandarin listeners proficient in English; the same listeners took part in both experiments (§4.1.2). Ratings were analysed with cumulative link mixed models and emmeans post hoc tests. Experiment 2 adds phone error rate (PER) from the Charsiu textless phone aligner, analysed with a linear mixed model (§4.2). Qualitative "expert listening" observations by the authors, with spectral illustrations, are reported separately and are explicitly outside the formal evaluation.

## Key Results

There is no baseline: no phone-input FastSpeech or other system is evaluated, so all results describe the FUL system alone across data and voice conditions.

**Experiment 1, small dataset (§4.1.3, Figure 3):**

| Voice / language | M0.5 | M2 | M8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| CN_MS1, Mandarin (seen) | 2.34 | 3.36 | 4.46 |
| CN_MS1, code-mixed | 2.07 | 2.97 | 3.92 |
| CN_MS1, English (unseen) | 1.31 | 1.55 | 1.66 |
| EN_MS1, English (seen) | 4.54 | 4.75 | 4.65 |
| EN_MS1, Mandarin (unseen) | 1.62 | 1.83 | 1.91 |
| EN_MS1, code-mixed | 2.26 | 3.09 | 3.23 |

Values are mean intelligibility MOS from the value table accompanying Figure 3. More Mandarin data significantly raised intelligibility for the Mandarin voice in Mandarin and in code-mixed speech (all pairwise p < .01; Mandarin and code-mixed did not differ, p = 0.74). The English voice was near ceiling in English with no significant model effect. Neither voice gained significantly in its unseen language (all p > .05), and the EN_MS1 code-mixed gain from 2.26 to 3.23 was not significant either. The authors attribute the weak code-mixed result for EN_MS1 partly to the absence of tone specification for that voice.

**Experiment 2, 100-hour multi-speaker dataset (§4.2.2, Figure 4):**

| Language | CN_FS1 iMOS / PER | CN_MS2 iMOS / PER | EN_MS1 iMOS / PER |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | 4.45 / 0.21 | 3.45 / 0.37 | 4.74 / 0.18 |
| Mandarin | 4.98 / 0.22 | 4.97 / 0.22 | 3.37 / 0.47 |
| Code-mixed | 4.96 / 0.24 | 4.95 / 0.20 | 3.68 / 0.15 |

Values are means from the value table accompanying Figure 4 (PER as a proportion; lower is better). In English, EN_MS1 and CN_FS1 were both rated significantly more intelligible than CN_MS2 (p < .001 and p = .03), and did not differ from each other. CN_MS2, with no English training data, reached 3.45 in English. In Mandarin and code-mixed speech the two Mandarin voices did not differ (p > .9) and were both rated significantly above EN_MS1 (p < .001); EN_MS1, with no Mandarin data, reached 3.37 in Mandarin. CN_MS2 reached 4.95 on code-mixed sentences despite having neither English nor code-mixed training data. PER separated voices significantly only in Mandarin, where both Mandarin voices had lower PER than EN_MS1 (p < .05); in English and code-mixed speech no PER difference was significant, so iMOS and PER diverge there (for example EN_MS1 has the lowest mean code-mixed PER, 0.15, but the lowest code-mixed iMOS, 3.68).

The authors compare the unseen-language scores descriptively with Experiment 1 (English from a Mandarin voice 1.66 to 3.45; Mandarin from EN_MS1 about 1.9 to 3.37), but note that CN_MS2 is a different speaker from CN_MS1 and that no statistical comparison across experiments is made. One prose sentence in §4.2.2 gives CN_MS2's English iMOS as 2.45; the Figure 4 value table and the following sentence give 3.45, which this page uses.

Expert-listening illustrations (outside the formal evaluation): Mandarin voices produced Mandarin-accented English (a diphthong in "bed", Figure 5); EN_MS1's Mandarin affricate in "zàijiàn" moved from a 3735.50 Hz fricative spectral peak in M0.5 to 5402.23 Hz in the 100-hour model, closer to a Mandarin alveolo-palatal (Figure 6); EN_MS1 did not produce the Mandarin rising tone reliably (Figure 7).

## Novelty Assessment

The architectural change is small: the FastSpeech phone embedding is swapped for a linear projection of a binary phonological feature vector, concatenated with tone and punctuation embeddings. Feeding phonological features to neural TTS to share data across languages has prior work that the paper itself reviews (Staib et al. 2020, Gutkin et al. 2019, Maniati et al. 2021, Wells and Richmond 2021). What is new is the choice of the FUL theory specifically, the released ARPABET/Pinyin to SAMPA to FUL mapping for English and Mandarin, and a statistically analysed listening study of native, cross-lingual and code-mixed intelligibility with two data scales. The contribution is best read as an engineering integration of an existing linguistic theory into an existing acoustic model, plus a feasibility evaluation; the evidence is limited by the absence of a phone-input baseline, so any benefit of FUL over phone or IPA input is not measured.

## Field Significance

Low — The paper provides a small, carefully analysed data point that a compact theory-driven phonological feature set can drive a non-autoregressive acoustic model for two typologically distant languages, including cross-lingual output for voices that never saw the target language and code-mixed output with a single voice. Because there is no phone-input baseline and the corpora are proprietary, it serves as feasibility evidence and a cross-disciplinary bridge from phonological theory to TTS rather than as evidence that feature input outperforms conventional inputs.

## Claims

- **supports:** Language-independent phonological feature inputs let a single acoustic model synthesize intelligible speech in a language that a given training voice never recorded, when the model is trained on enough pooled multi-speaker data.
  > *Evidence:* With 100 h of pooled data, a Mandarin-only voice (CN_MS2) reached intelligibility MOS 3.45 in English and an English-only voice (EN_MS1) reached 3.37 in Mandarin, against 1.66 and 1.91 for the corresponding unseen-language conditions in the small-data models; the cross-experiment comparison is descriptive only. *(§4.2.2, Figure 4; §4.1.3, Figure 3)*
- **complicates:** Cross-lingual transfer through shared segmental features does not emerge from a few hours of monolingual data per voice, even as data for the other language grows.
  > *Evidence:* Increasing Mandarin data from 0.5 to 8 h raised the Mandarin voice's Mandarin intelligibility from 2.34 to 4.46, but neither voice improved significantly in its unseen language (all p > .05; English from CN_MS1 1.31 to 1.66, Mandarin from EN_MS1 1.62 to 1.91). *(§4.1.3, Figure 3)*
- **complicates:** Sharing segmental features across languages does not transfer lexical tone when tone is encoded with language-specific labels, which limits cross-lingual intelligibility for tonal target languages.
  > *Evidence:* EN_MS1 produced Mandarin segments with a more target-like affricate place in the 100-hour model but rendered the rising tone of "kùcún" as a short low target, and its Mandarin intelligibility (3.37) stayed significantly below both Mandarin voices (4.98, 4.97; p < .001). *(§4.2.2, Figures 6 and 7)*
- **supports:** A shared phonological representation with a per-speaker embedding allows one voice to produce code-mixed speech without code-mixed or second-language training data for that voice.
  > *Evidence:* CN_MS2, trained on Mandarin only, was rated 4.95 on Mandarin-English code-mixed sentences, not significantly different from CN_FS1 (4.96), whose data included code-mixed and English speech (p > .9). *(§4.2.2, Figure 4)*
- **complicates:** Automatic phone error rate and listener intelligibility ratings can disagree for synthetic speech, so segmental accuracy alone does not capture perceived intelligibility.
  > *Evidence:* PER showed no significant voice differences for English or code-mixed output while intelligibility MOS did; EN_MS1 had the lowest mean code-mixed PER (0.15) but the lowest code-mixed intelligibility MOS (3.68), which the authors attribute to prosodic, tonal or audio-quality factors. *(§4.2.2, Figure 4)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> No baseline is evaluated: the paper never trains the same FastSpeech with phone or IPA input, so it cannot show that FUL features are more data-efficient or transfer better than conventional inputs. All training and test data are proprietary, the test set is 13 sentences, and only intelligibility (not naturalness) is rated.

- Only two languages (English and Mandarin) are tested; the motivating low-resource setting is not evaluated, as the authors acknowledge.
- Tone and prosody are language-specific labels rather than features, so suprasegmental transfer is outside the shared representation; the authors propose generalized tonal or intonational encodings as future work.
- The Kaldi aligner was trained on ARPABET or Pinyin while inputs used the finer SAMPA set, which the authors flag as a possible source of alignment errors.
- Data amounts are unbalanced and confounded with speakers and recording styles (newsreader, AI assistant, announcements), and Experiment 1 to Experiment 2 comparisons involve different speakers and are descriptive only.
- The acoustic model and vocoder (FastSpeech with a pretrained MelGAN) are older designs; whether FUL input carries over to modern codec or end-to-end architectures is left open.
- Spectral and formant observations are single-word illustrations by the authors, not a systematic acoustic analysis.
