> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Xiaoqun Liu et al.** (Centific Research / University of Washington) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09263) · Demo: ✗ · Code: ✗
>
> A controlled audit that crosses male and female input voices with masculine-, neutral- and feminine-stereotyped speech content shows that five end-to-end speech-to-speech models keep their output voice stable, yet all of them decide the speaker's gender from the content rather than the voice.

## Problem

Speech-to-speech models now caption, summarize, translate and re-voice real speakers, so they can misgender a speaker whose words break a gender stereotype. The obvious fairness probe checks whether the output voice drifts toward the stereotype in the content. The authors argue that this probe cannot fail on most deployed systems: they answer in a single fixed output voice, so there is nothing to drift, and a clean result says nothing about fairness. Prior speech-bias benchmarks (spoken StereoSet, VoiceBBQ, spoken dialogue bias tests) mostly use multiple-choice formats ported from text and do not separate the voice cue from the content cue in generative tasks.

## Method

The study is an audit protocol, not a model. It uses a full-factorial 3 × 3 × 2 design: language (English, Spanish, Mandarin) × content stereotype (masculine, neutral, feminine) × input voice gender (male, female). Each (language, stereotype) pair has 10 first-person passages on different themes, and each passage is voiced in one male and one female voice, giving 180 inputs (10 per cell). Misaligned cells pair a male voice with feminine content or a female voice with masculine content; neutral-content cells are the baseline. The three languages vary how referent gender reaches speech: English through obligatory pronouns, Spanish through audible agreement morphology in a pro-drop language, and Mandarin not at all, since spoken tā (他/她) is homophonous *(§3.1)*.

Passages are built in four steps. Stereotype seeds come from occupation and activity terms in WinoBias/WinoGender and BBQ (the benchmarks are not used as evaluation data). An LLM then writes speaker-anonymous, first-person passages with no gendered words or speaker-referring agreement morphology. An LLM judge panel ranks stereotype intensity and the top 10 per pole are kept. Finally, one trilingual human rater screens them for neutrality, naturalness, pole assignment and length. Passages are matched on information load (about 65 English words, 80 Spanish words, 110 Mandarin characters) and voiced with Azure neural TTS (Andrew/Ava, Álvaro/Elvira, Yunxi/Xiaoxiao) *(§3.2, Appendix A, Table 3)*. A carrier gate (H0) certifies the TTS inputs before any model hears them. Two wav2vec2 speaker-gender classifiers flip the intended voice gender on 0/60 inputs per language, and within-voice F-tests find no content effect on a femininity composite (p = .97/.18/.41 for en/es/zh). The one secondary-channel exception (Spanish classifier-1 margin, p = .018) is shown to be a non-directional offset of at most 0.007 logits against a male-female separation of about 13 logits *(§3.4, Appendix G, Table 6)*.

Each input goes through five language-native spoken prompts: readback (verbatim repeat, the pure voice-drift baseline), paraphrase (first person, negative control), summarize (one third-person sentence, forcing a he/she choice), translate (es/zh to English), and describe (say one word for the speaker's gender) *(§3.3, Table 1)*. Two channels are scored:

- **RQ1, voice rendering.** Each output is scored against its own input with a composite femininity index ∆comp: the equal-weight mean of z-scored F0, a formant index over F1–F3, and the pre-softmax logit margins of the two wav2vec2 gender classifiers. Positive values mean a more feminine output. The estimand is the baseline-corrected contrast ∆misaligned − ∆neutral, signed so that feminine content predicts a more feminine output. It is tested with Welch t-tests and with a mixed-effects content × voice interaction model (utterance random intercept) fit per model and language *(§4, Appendix F)*.
- **RQ2, gender attribution.** Describe answers are mapped to male/female, and a logistic model of P(judged female) is fit on a content-femininity ordinal while controlling for the true voice. It reports the odds ratio (OR) per content step, so OR > 1 means more feminine content pushes the judgment toward "female". For summarize, the slope ∆pp = P('she' | feminine) − P('she' | masculine) is computed among outputs that inject a gendered pronoun. Positive values mean the pronoun follows the stereotype. The criterion is invariance: the judgment should not move when only the content changes *(§4)*.

The systems under test are two API models, GPT-4o-audio ("gpt-audio", fixed voice alloy) and Gemini 2.5 Live (fixed voice Kore), and three open checkpoints: GLM-4-Voice-9B, Step-Audio-2-mini and Kimi-Audio-7B. All five answer in a fixed output voice. The open models render through one built-in decoder timbre (a flow decoder, flow plus BigVGAN, and a CosyVoice 2 decoder respectively). The closed models generate their voice natively, so drift is possible in principle *(§4, Appendix B, Table 4)*.

## Key Results

**No voice drift (RQ1).** No content × voice readback interaction is significant for any model or language (Table 2 p-values range from .06 to .83). Across all acoustically scored tasks, only 4 of 40 fits reach p < .05, all on summarize or translate, whose output text changes with condition. The pooled baseline-corrected contrast is −0.018 ± 0.020, indistinguishable from zero. The closed models show 4 isolated significant cells out of 44 misaligned task-cells (three toward the stereotype, one away), and none replicates across task or language. Examples are gpt-audio Spanish male-voice/feminine-content readback (∆comp = +0.19, p = .02) and Gemini English female-voice/masculine-content paraphrase (∆comp = −0.10, p = .004). For the three open models, the null is structural: pooled |∆comp| < 0.05, and their fixed timbre leaves no room to move *(§4.1, Table 2)*.

**Content drives the gender judgment (RQ2).** In describe, feminine content raises P(judged female) significantly for every model. Pooled ORs per content step are 21.9 (gpt-audio), 24.3 (Gemini 2.5 Live), 1.7 (GLM-4-Voice), 3.3 (Kimi-Audio) and 3.4 (Step-Audio-2) *(§4.1, Table 2, Table 7)*. Misgendering rates by cell type *(§4.1, Table 2)*:

| Model | Aligned | Neutral | Misaligned en | Misaligned es | Misaligned zh |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gpt-audio | 2% | 29% | 100% | 85% | 83% |
| Gemini 2.5 Live | 0% | 3% | 20% | 55% | 50% |
| GLM-4-Voice | 36% | 48% | 55% | 44% | 75% |
| Kimi-Audio | 0% | 16% | 0% | 0% | 55% |
| Step-Audio-2 | 3% | 27% | 50% | 20% | 20% |

gpt-audio has the highest misaligned misgendering rate in every language (100/85/83%, about 90% pooled) while erring on only 2% of aligned clips. So the acoustic evidence is available to the model and is overridden, not missing. The two closed models have ORs about 6–14× those of the open models. The authors note that part of this gap may come from attenuation: the open models' higher neutral-cell error flattens their content sensitivity *(§4.1)*. Kimi-Audio is fully voice-faithful in English (OR 1.0) and Spanish (0% misaligned errors) but misgenders 55% of misaligned Mandarin clips. Mandarin is the only language where every model has at least 20% misaligned misgendering *(§4.1, Table 7)*.

**Pronouns follow content.** Among summaries that inject a gendered pronoun (92% overall; 76% for GLM-4-Voice, 50% in its Spanish summaries), P('she') rises from masculine to feminine content for every model. The ∆pp slopes are 0.60 (Step-Audio-2), 0.49 (Gemini), 0.43 (gpt-audio), and 0.09 for both GLM-4-Voice and Kimi-Audio. For gpt-audio (0.37 male voice / 0.50 female voice) and Step-Audio-2 (0.76 / 0.44), the slope holds on both input voices. Gemini's female-voice cells sit near ceiling (at least 83% 'she' in every content condition), so its gradient appears on the male voice (0.81). Paraphrase and translate inject almost no gendered pronouns (about 5% and 0%) *(§4.1, Table 8)*. Gemini's transcripts come from its Live API transcription layer rather than the dialogue model's own text stream, which may affect its Mandarin pronoun counts *(§4.1, Table 2 note; Appendix B)*.

## Novelty Assessment

The main contribution is methodological. The paper shows that a voice-drift fairness probe has a guaranteed null on fixed-voice architectures. It also gives a single crossed design that recovers the hidden bias by aiming the same voiced inputs at attribution tasks, scored on invariance rather than accuracy. Crossing voice and content cues for speech-model bias is not new in itself: VoiceBBQ separates content from acoustics in a multiple-choice format. What is new here is the generative, long-form, multilingual version, combined with the validity argument about fixed output voices. The TTS carrier gate is a careful design step that rules out the synthesizer as a confound. The empirical panel is small (five models, 10 passages per cell, one voice per gender per language), so its per-language results are descriptive.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper shows that a stable output voice in a speech-to-speech system tells you nothing about gender fairness. It provides an invariance-scored protocol that exposes content-driven misgendering in all five tested speech LMs, closed and open alike. It is a cautionary data point for bias audits of spoken conversational agents rather than a new benchmark at scale.

## Claims

- **complicates:** Checking whether a speech-to-speech model's output voice drifts toward a content stereotype cannot detect gender bias in systems that render through a fixed output voice, because such a probe returns a null by construction.
  > *Evidence:* No content × voice readback interaction is significant for any of five models in any of three languages, and the pooled baseline-corrected drift is −0.018 ± 0.020. The three open models render through a single built-in timbre, so the drift channel is architecturally excluded, yet every model still shows content-driven gender attribution. *(§4.1, Table 2; Appendix B, Table 4)*
- **supports:** End-to-end speech LMs that hear the speaker's voice still infer speaker gender largely from the stereotype of what is said, overriding acoustic evidence when content and voice conflict.
  > *Evidence:* In the describe task, the pooled odds ratio of a 'female' judgment per step of more feminine content (voice held fixed) ranges from 1.7 to 24.3 across five models, all significant. The worst model misgenders 83–100% of misaligned clips by language, against 2% of aligned clips. *(§4.1, Table 2, Table 7)*
- **supports:** Stereotype-driven gender inference in speech LMs surfaces only in tasks that force a third-person commitment, such as gendered pronouns in summaries, and is largely absent from first-person re-expression tasks.
  > *Evidence:* Among summaries that inject a pronoun, P('she') rises with content femininity for every model (∆pp 0.09–0.60), and for two models the slope holds on both input voices. Paraphrase and translate inject gendered pronouns in only about 5% and 0% of outputs. *(§4.1, Table 8; Appendix H)*
- **refines:** Language typology changes where content-driven misgendering appears in speech LMs, but not its direction; languages in which spoken pronouns carry no gender still produce errors when output must be written or committed.
  > *Evidence:* Wherever a per-language effect is estimable and significant, it points toward the content stereotype. Mandarin, whose spoken tā is gender-ambiguous, is the only language where every model misgenders at least 20% of misaligned clips, and the only one with paraphrase slips, mostly defaulting to masculine 他. *(§4.1, Table 2, Table 7; Appendix H)*
- **complicates:** Closed commercial speech LMs are not safer than open checkpoints on speaker-gender attribution and can be markedly more content-driven.
  > *Evidence:* The two API models have pooled odds ratios of 21.9 and 24.3 against 1.7–3.4 for the three open models. The authors note that part of this gap may reflect the open models' higher neutral-cell baseline error (16–48% for the open models; 3% and 29% for the closed ones), which attenuates measured content sensitivity. *(§4.1 "Open vs. closed", Table 2)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> The inputs are synthetic Azure TTS voices with one voice per gender per language and 10 passages per cell. Several per-language logistic fits hit perfect separation or singular fits, so only the pooled estimates support inference. Whether the effect sizes hold for natural speakers and more varied voices is untested.

- The passages are LLM-generated and LLM-rated for stereotype intensity, then screened by a single human rater. Probing LLM-based systems with LLM-authored text may share priors *(§4.1 "Limitations", Appendix E)*.
- The design and coding are binary (male/female). This matches the audited behaviours but says nothing about non-binary reference. The authors also note that the describe task (automatic gender recognition) is ethically contested, and they audit it rather than endorse it *(§Ethics Statement)*.
- Non-compliant describe answers are excluded rather than coded (161–180 of 180 scoreable per model). GLM-4-Voice's Spanish compliance collapses (41/60 scoreable describe answers; pronouns in 50% of summaries) *(§4.1)*.
- Gemini's transcripts come from the Live API transcription service, not the dialogue model's own text stream, so its Mandarin pronoun choices may partly reflect that layer *(§4.1 "Limitations")*.
- No voice-preserving speech-to-speech system is tested, so the RQ1 null cannot show whether stereotype-driven voice drift would appear in systems that copy the input speaker. Mitigations (suppressing unsolicited gender inference, weighting voice over content, allowing refusal) are proposed but not evaluated *(§Broader Impact)*.
- No code, passages or prompts-plus-audio release is stated in the paper.
