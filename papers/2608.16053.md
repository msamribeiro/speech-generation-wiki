> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Pengcheng Wang et al.** (Institute of Science Tokyo / Hokkaido University) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16053) · Demo: ? · Code: ?
>
> Two off-the-shelf full-duplex speech models perform an LLM-written dialogue script under a frame-level lexical constraint, so turn-taking timing (overlaps, gaps, backchannels) emerges from their real-time interaction while the words stay fixed, and a TTS model then re-renders the result without changing that timing.

## Problem

Synthetic dialogue corpora are increasingly used to train and test conversational ASR and speaker diarization. Most pipelines write the dialogue with an LLM and then insert overlaps, interruptions and backchannels through markers, behaviour tags or hand-set timing rules, so the conversational timing is prescribed by the pipeline rather than produced by interaction. Full-duplex speech models do produce interaction-driven timing, because they listen while speaking, but they generate free-form content and cannot follow a fixed script. The paper targets the gap between controllable content and emergent timing.

## Method

The system (named Agentic-DuplexGen in the PDF; the arXiv metadata title uses DuplexGen) splits dialogue generation into three stages that never share a decision: an LLM fixes the content, two duplex models fix the timing, and a TTS model fixes the acoustics. No component is trained; the conclusion calls it a zero-training pipeline.

**Content.** DeepSeek-V4 writes a two-speaker dialogue script. The turns of each speaker are concatenated into a target token sequence with a cursor pointing at the next token to speak.

**Timing (script-constrained duplex decoding).** Two instances of a full-duplex model with the inner-monologue design of [[2410.00037|Moshi]] run against each other, each receiving the other's audio in real time. The paper does not name the exact checkpoint. At every frame, the model's text-token prediction is intercepted and its vocabulary is restricted. A floor-eligible speaker may emit only PAD or its next script token; a listener may emit only PAD or a word from a fixed backchannel whitelist (for example "mm-hmm", "yeah"). Each speaker therefore decides only whether to speak the next scripted word now or wait, so the transcript matches the script by construction. Three control parameters bound the interaction: a handoff window around the projected end of the current turn within which the listener may take the floor, a maximum number of consecutive PAD frames before a floor-eligible speaker is forced to start, and a refractory interval between backchannels. The exact onset of each floor transfer, overlap length and backchannel position are left to the two models.

**Acoustics (fidelity re-rendering).** The duplex run is stored as a symbolic interaction score (active speaker, turn boundaries, overlap regions, backchannel positions), which [[2505.17589|CosyVoice 3]] re-renders with a reference voice. Because the duplex model and the TTS model speak at different rates, timing is transferred in relative terms: silent gaps keep their duration in seconds, and an overlapping turn starts at the same fraction of the preceding turn's rendered duration at which it started in the duplex run. Backchannels are placed the same way, synthesized separately and mixed in. A sliding speaker prompt keeps voices consistent over long dialogues, and interrupted speech is cut with a short crossfade. Word timestamps (from CosyVoice alignment), RTTM speaker activity and overlap, interruption, gap and backchannel labels are exported during synthesis rather than recovered afterwards.

## Key Results

**Turn-taking realism (Table I).** Against 5,611 floor transfers from PriMock57, the emergent timing has a floor-transfer-offset (FTO) Wasserstein distance of 0.366 and a KS statistic of 0.197. The stitching baselines score 0.695 and 0.432 (random gap) and 0.783 and 0.557 (fixed gap), so the proposed timing has the lowest distance on both measures among the evaluated systems; the 47% relative reduction is against the random-gap baseline. Stitching produces no overlapped transitions and no long-tail gaps, while the proposed timing gives 38.5% overlapped transitions (real: 42.8%) and 30.8% long-tail gaps (real: 20.5%, so it overshoots). These statistics are computed on general-domain scripts, compared with a clinical reference. Robustness checks keep the ranking: varying the utterance-end constant gives W_fto of 0.435, 0.357 and 0.292; a six-seed bootstrap gives a 95% CI of [0.280, 0.545]; and energy-based VAD boundaries reduce W_fto to 0.240, with a median FTO of 0.08 s against 0.11 s for real dialogue. Figure 3 shows the advantage disappears once the overlap setting is pushed beyond the range seen in real dialogue.

**Stage fidelity (Table II).** Forced WER against the script is 0%, overlap events transfer to the rendered audio without deviation, re-rendering adds at most 1.7% WER, and CAM++ speaker similarity is 0.82.

**Register adaptation and control (Tables III and IV).** With identical control settings, medical scripts yield 7.2% overlap and casual scripts 10.5% (real clinical: 6.3%). Backchannels fall at prosodic boundaries in 95.5% of cases against 71.7% for random placement. The three benchmark tiers rise monotonically in overlap (polite 2.0%, natural 6.6%, adversarial 12.2%). The natural tier matches the real overlap rate, but its overlapped-transition share (20.6% vs 42.8%) and backchannel rate (3.7 vs 1.5 per minute) remain far from real.

**Downstream ASR stress test (Tables V and VI).** On the 180-segment benchmark, Whisper large-v2 overlap-region WER rises from 28.3% to 48.1% to 56.7% across tiers while clean-region WER stays between 1.8% and 3.1%; wav2vec2 shows the same pattern (50.7%, 64.8%, 71.9% overlap-region; 13.3% to 15.2% clean). In a 2×2 ablation at matched overlap rates (n=30), emergent and stitched timing give similar overlap-region WER (18.8% vs 23.2% with truncate-and-fade rendering; 44.1% vs 43.3% with equal-volume overlay, with overlapping CIs), whereas equal-volume rendering roughly doubles the error. Every synthetic cell stays far below the real overlap-region WER of 76.5%. Pyannote 3.1 diarization error barely changes across tiers. No subjective listening test is reported.

## Novelty Assessment

The new element is an inference procedure: constraining a duplex speech model's inner-monologue text stream to a script cursor plus a backchannel whitelist, so the model contributes only emit-or-wait timing decisions. Pairing two such constrained instances and transferring their timing to a separate TTS renderer through relative positions is a simple but distinct way to combine controllable content with interaction-driven timing. All models are used off the shelf, and the timing transfer and rendering steps are engineering. The baselines are limited to two rule-based stitching variants; no marker- or tag-based dialogue generator is compared directly. The paper's own ablation shows that emergent timing has no detectable effect on downstream ASR difficulty relative to stitched timing, which weakens the practical case for the timing mechanism in the ASR use case it targets.

## Field Significance

Low — The paper shows that a full-duplex speech model can be steered at decoding time to perform a fixed script, giving turn-taking statistics closer to real dialogue than rule-based stitching while keeping exact content control and construction-time annotations. The evidence is narrow: nothing is trained, the released corpus is a 175-minute synthetic patient-clinician set, and every evaluation is automatic. Its own ablation also provides a cautionary data point: for overlapped-speech ASR benchmarks, how overlap is rendered matters more than how its timing is generated.

## Claims

- **supports:** Constraining the text stream of a full-duplex speech model to a fixed script lets conversational timing emerge from model interaction while preserving exact lexical content.
  > *Evidence:* Restricting each frame's token choice to PAD, the next script token or a backchannel whitelist gives 0% forced WER, and the emergent timing reaches FTO Wasserstein 0.366 and KS 0.197 against real clinical dialogue, versus 0.695 and 0.432 for random-gap stitching. *(§II-B, §IV-A, Table I, Table II)*
- **supports:** Rule-based stitching of synthesized turns fails to reproduce overlapped transitions and long silent gaps found in real conversation.
  > *Evidence:* Both fixed-gap and random-gap stitching produce 0% overlapped transitions and 0% long-tail gaps, against 42.8% and 20.5% in PriMock57, and collapse to a single near-zero-gap FTO mode. *(§IV-A, Table I, Figure 2)*
- **complicates:** Matching turn-taking timing statistics in synthetic dialogue does not by itself make the data harder or more realistic for overlapped-speech recognition.
  > *Evidence:* At matched overlap rates, emergent and stitched timing give overlapping confidence intervals for overlap-region WER (18.8% vs 23.2%; 44.1% vs 43.3%), while switching from truncate-and-fade to equal-volume rendering roughly doubles WER; all synthetic cells remain well below the real 76.5%. *(§IV-D, Table VI)*
- **refines:** In synthetic conversational speech, recognition difficulty concentrates in overlap regions and can be controlled through interaction density without changing clean-speech difficulty.
  > *Evidence:* Whisper large-v2 overlap-region WER rises from 28.3% to 56.7% across the polite to adversarial tiers while clean-region WER stays at 1.8% to 3.1%; wav2vec2 shows the same pattern. *(§IV-D, Table IV, Table V)*
- **complicates:** Emergent timing from duplex models matches aggregate overlap rates more easily than the full structure of real turn exchange.
  > *Evidence:* The natural tier matches the real overlap rate (6.6% vs 6.3%) but has fewer than half the real share of overlapped transitions (20.6% vs 42.8%) and more than twice the backchannel rate (3.7 vs 1.5 per minute); on general-domain scripts long-tail gaps overshoot real dialogue (30.8% vs 20.5%). *(§IV-A, §IV-D, Table I, Table IV)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> The paper's own 2×2 ablation (n=30) finds no detectable effect of emergent versus stitched timing on overlap-region ASR error, and all synthetic conditions remain far easier than real clinical overlap (76.5% WER), so the benefit of emergent timing for the stated ASR and diarization use case is not demonstrated.

- Interaction is only partly emergent: overlap frequency and backchannel density are still set by explicit control parameters, and overlaps are confined to turn-transition regions with no deep mid-utterance interruptions.
- Because content is fixed before the duplex run, speakers cannot repair, self-correct or abandon a planned utterance after being interrupted.
- The duplex model checkpoint is not named, and the paper describes the benchmark as released without giving a code or data link.
- The realism reference comes from a single corpus (PriMock57), and the distributional realism experiment uses general-domain scripts compared against clinical statistics.
- No human listening test assesses the naturalness of the rendered dialogues; speech quality is checked only through re-rendering WER and CAM++ speaker similarity.
- Only two rule-based stitching baselines are compared; marker- or behaviour-tag-based dialogue generators are discussed but not evaluated.
