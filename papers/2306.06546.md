> A single universal RVQ-GAN codec compresses 44.1 kHz speech, music and environmental audio about 91x into 8 kbps discrete tokens. Factorized, L2-normalized codebooks keep codebook usage near full, and a fully ablated recipe beats EnCodec at a third of its bitrate.

## Problem

Language models over audio need a discrete tokenizer that reconstructs full-band audio with few artifacts, compresses strongly, and handles any kind of audio with one model. SoundStream and EnCodec only partly meet these needs. Their outputs show the usual GAN artifacts: tonal, pitch and periodicity artifacts, plus weak high frequencies. They are also tuned to particular signal types. The authors find two further faults in existing RVQ codecs. Codebook collapse leaves part of each codebook unused, so the effective bitrate falls below the nominal one. Quantizer dropout, used to get variable bitrate from one model, reduces quality at full bitrate.

## Method

The model (called Improved RVQGAN in the paper and released in the descript-audio-codec repository, DAC) follows the VQ-GAN pattern of SoundStream and EnCodec. A fully convolutional encoder downsamples the waveform by strides [2, 4, 8, 8] (512 overall). Residual vector quantization (RVQ) quantizes the encodings, and a mirrored convolutional decoder upsamples them back. Each block is a strided convolution followed by residual layers with Snake activations, a periodic activation taken from BigVGAN, in place of Leaky ReLU. With decoder dimension 1536 the model has 76M parameters (22M encoder, 54M decoder). At 44.1 kHz this gives an 86 Hz frame rate and 9 codebooks of 10 bits, for an 8 kbps maximum and a compression factor of 91.16 (Table 1).

The quantizer borrows two techniques from the Improved VQGAN image model. Factorized codes run the nearest-neighbour lookup in a low-dimensional space (8-d) through input and output projections around the 1024-d embedding. L2 normalization of encodings and codes turns the Euclidean lookup into a cosine-similarity lookup (Appendix A). Training uses only the original VQ-VAE codebook and commitment losses with a straight-through estimator: no k-means initialization, no EMA updates, and no random restarts. Quantizer dropout is applied to each example with probability p = 0.5 rather than always, which keeps low-bitrate operation while reducing the full-bitrate penalty.

The adversarial setup pairs a multi-period waveform discriminator (periods 2, 3, 5, 7, 11) with a new multi-band, multi-scale complex STFT discriminator. It uses window lengths 2048, 1024 and 512, and each STFT is split into 5 sub-bands to target high-frequency aliasing. Training combines a HingeGAN loss, L1 feature matching, and an L1 multi-scale log-mel loss with seven window lengths (32 to 2048) and matched mel-bin counts (5 to 320). Loss weights are fixed (mel 15, feature matching 2, adversarial 1, codebook 1, commitment 0.25), with no EnCodec-style loss balancer. Training data mixes speech (DAPS, DNS Challenge 4 clean speech, Common Voice, VCTK), music (MUSDB, Jamendo) and AudioSet, all resampled to 44.1 kHz. A balanced sampler makes every batch contain a confirmed full-band item and equal shares of speech, music and environmental sound. The final model trains for 400k iterations at batch size 72 on 0.38 s excerpts.

## Key Results

On the 3000-segment test set (1000 ten-second clips each of speech, music and environmental sound), the final model at 8 kbps reaches mel distance 0.93, STFT distance 1.60, ViSQOL 4.18 and SI-SDR 10.75 (Table 3). EnCodec at 24 kbps reaches 1.61, 3.97, 3.16 and 9.59, so the proposed codec is ahead on all four metrics at a third of the bitrate. It also models 22.05 kHz bandwidth against EnCodec's 12 kHz. At matched or lower bitrates the proposed codec also leads Lyra (9.2 kbps, SI-SDR -14.52) and Opus at 8 kbps. Opus at 24 kbps, three times the proposed maximum, has a lower mel distance (0.88 vs 0.93) and a higher SI-SDR (11.65 vs 10.75), while the 8 kbps codec keeps the better STFT distance and ViSQOL (Table 3).

Part of the Table 3 gap reflects bandwidth, since the EnCodec model is a 24 kHz model. Table 4 controls for this by retraining the recipe in EnCodec's exact configuration (24 kHz, 24 kbps, stride 320, 32 ten-bit codebooks). The retrained recipe is ahead of EnCodec at every bitrate from 1.5 to 24 kbps on all four metrics, for example ViSQOL 4.61 vs 4.42 and SI-SDR 16.40 vs 9.69 at 24 kbps. The listening test is MUSHRA-style, with a hidden reference and no low anchor: 10 expert listeners, 12 clips, 4 per domain. It shows the proposed codec at 2.67, 5.33 and 8 kbps scoring above EnCodec at 3, 6 and 12 kbps (Figure 3), and above EnCodec at matched 24 kHz settings (Figure 4). The scores are reported only as plots, and even the 8 kbps model stays below the reference.

The ablations (Table 2, 250k iterations, batch 12) attribute the gains to specific recipe choices:

- **Snake activation:** replacing ReLU raises SI-SDR from 6.92 to 9.12. The authors name it the most influential architecture change.
- **Adversarial losses:** removing all discriminators drops bitrate efficiency from 99% to 62% and SI-SDR to 1.07, and the output audibly buzzes.
- **Codebook dimension:** the projected codebook dimension strongly affects code usage. Dimensions 2 and 256 give 84% and 59% efficiency against 99% at dimension 8.
- **EMA codebook updates:** the EnCodec-style EMA update gives 97% efficiency and SI-SDR 8.33, both below the projected lookup.
- **Quantizer dropout:** p = 0.0 gives SI-SDR 10.14 versus 9.12 at p = 1.0, but it reconstructs poorly with few codebooks (Figure 2). The final model uses p = 0.5.
- **Maximum bitrate:** raising it to 24 kbps gives the best values in the table on every metric (mel 0.73, SI-SDR 13.83).
- **Balanced sampling:** without it, outputs stop near 18 kHz.

## Novelty Assessment

Most components already existed. The encoder-decoder and RVQ come from SoundStream. The Snake activation and much of the GAN recipe come from BigVGAN, and the factorized, L2-normalized codebooks come from Improved VQGAN in the image domain. The genuinely new elements are three:

- the multi-band, multi-scale complex STFT discriminator;
- probabilistic quantizer dropout, together with the diagnosis that always-on dropout harms full-bitrate quality;
- the balanced full-band data sampler.

The paper also shows that codebook collapse measurably reduces the effective bitrate of existing RVQ codecs, using codebook entropy (Figure 1). The main strength is the evidence: every design choice is ablated one at a time on four objective metrics, and the recipe is also tested in EnCodec's exact configuration, which separates recipe gains from sampling-rate and bitrate differences. The subjective test is small and compares against EnCodec only. The contribution is a training recipe plus a quantizer change on an existing architecture family, with code and weights released.

## Field Significance

> [!tip]
> High: the paper shows that an RVQ-GAN codec can reach about 99% codebook usage and full-band 44.1 kHz reconstruction at 8 kbps across speech, music and environmental audio with one model. It also isolates which recipe choices produce that result. With open code and weights, it provides a ready audio tokenizer and a fully ablated baseline recipe for codec and codec-LM work.

## Claims

- **supports:** Low-dimensional, L2-normalized codebook lookup prevents codebook under-utilization in residual vector quantization, raising effective bitrate and reconstruction quality without k-means initialization or codebook restarts.
  > *Evidence:* Projected 8-d lookup gives 99% bitrate efficiency and SI-SDR 9.12, versus 97% and 8.33 for EMA codebook updates. Codebook dimensions of 2 and 256 fall to 84% and 59% efficiency with worse metrics. Per-codebook entropy is lower for EnCodec and the EMA variant (Figure 1). *(§3.2, §4.5, Table 2, Figure 1)*
- **complicates:** Training a single neural codec for variable bitrate with quantizer dropout trades away reconstruction quality at the full bitrate.
  > *Evidence:* Always-on quantizer dropout (p = 1.0) yields SI-SDR 9.12, versus 10.14 without dropout and 9.74 at p = 0.5. The no-dropout model, however, reconstructs poorly with few codebooks (Figure 2). *(§3.3, §4.5, Table 2, Figure 2)*
- **supports:** Adversarial training is necessary for phase-accurate waveform reconstruction and efficient code usage in neural audio codecs, beyond what spectral reconstruction losses provide.
  > *Evidence:* Removing all discriminators drops SI-SDR from 9.12 to 1.07 and bitrate efficiency from 99% to 62%, with buzzing artifacts. Mel and STFT distances change little (1.13 and 1.92 vs 1.09 and 1.82). *(§4.5, Table 2)*
- **complicates:** Spectral-distance and ViSQOL scores can miss phase and aliasing artifacts in codec output, so they cannot be the only basis for codec design decisions.
  > *Evidence:* The discriminator-free model scores a higher ViSQOL (4.12) than the full model (3.96) despite audible buzzing. Aliasing removed by the multi-band discriminator, seen in spectrograms, barely moves the objective metrics. *(§4.5, Table 2)*
- **supports:** Neural codecs trained on mixed-domain data with an improved GAN recipe can exceed earlier RVQ codecs in reconstruction quality at substantially lower bitrates.
  > *Evidence:* At 8 kbps the codec beats EnCodec at 24 kbps on mel distance (0.93 vs 1.61), STFT distance (1.60 vs 3.97), ViSQOL (4.18 vs 3.16) and SI-SDR (10.75 vs 9.59). In EnCodec's exact 24 kHz configuration it leads at every bitrate from 1.5 to 24 kbps. *(§4.6, Table 3, Table 4)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

The MUSHRA test is small (10 expert listeners, 12 clips, no low-pass anchor) and compares against EnCodec only. Its scores are given only as plots with confidence intervals, so the subjective margin cannot be stated numerically. The main 44.1 kHz comparison also mixes bandwidths (22.05 kHz vs EnCodec's 12 kHz). Table 4 addresses this only for objective metrics and Figure 4.

The paper reports no speech-specific intelligibility or speaker metrics, such as WER or speaker similarity. Its claim that the codec suits downstream language modeling is argued from code entropy and frame rate, but no generative model is trained on the tokens. The 86 Hz frame rate with 9 codebooks still produces long token sequences for a language model, and the paper does not test causal or streaming operation. The authors report that quality is best for speech and weaker for environmental sounds and some instruments, such as glockenspiel and synthesizers. Even the 8 kbps model falls short of the reference in MUSHRA, and the 24 kbps ablation suggests that a higher maximum bitrate would narrow the gap. The authors also flag deepfake misuse and suggest watermarking or codec-detection classifiers, which the paper does not build.
