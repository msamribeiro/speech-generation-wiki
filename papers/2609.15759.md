> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Ke Hu et al.** (NVIDIA) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15759) · Demo: ? · Code: ✗
>
> Adds a parallel streaming ASR head to an existing full-duplex speech-to-speech model, so one decoding pass produces both a live user transcript and the agent's response text while turn-taking and barge-in behaviour stay close to the no-ASR baseline.

## Problem

Full-duplex speech-to-speech models listen and speak at the same time, but they do not output a transcript of what the user said. Cascaded ASR-LLM-TTS systems give that transcript for free, and it matters for conversation logging, accessibility and quality monitoring. Prior duplex systems (Moshi, PersonaPlex, SyncLLM, OmniFlatten, SALM-Duplex) lack an explicit user-transcription output. One related approach interleaves ASR and reasoning tokens in a single text monologue, which mixes transcription into a shared channel. Dedicated streaming ASR models (FastConformer, Parakeet, Kyutai STT) are hard to plug into a duplex model, and chunk-based LLM ASR (Qwen3-ASR) ties minimum latency to chunk size.

## Method

The system extends the SALM-Duplex architecture. A 600M-parameter Parakeet streaming encoder turns user speech into continuous embeddings at an 80 ms frame rate. A 9B decoder-only LLM (Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2-Base) receives three time-aligned streams, summed per frame: the user speech encoding, the previous user-transcript tokens and the previous agent-text tokens. It predicts user text and agent text in parallel through separate heads (multi-channel next-token prediction, equal loss weights). Agent text, with turn-taking information carried by `<bos>`/`<eos>` tokens, goes to a separate streaming TTS (VoiceChat-TTS) to produce agent speech. The paper only modifies the speech-to-text part.

The added module is a separate user-text embedding layer plus a user-text prediction head, both initialized from the LLM's existing embedding and output layers. An ASR next-token loss on user transcripts is added next to the agent-text loss. Frame-level targets come from on-the-fly CTC forced alignment (torchaudio with the MMS-FA model). Each word's tokens are placed at the word's start frame (left alignment, which the authors found better than right alignment), and pad tokens fill the rest. A user-text delay d_u shifts transcript targets later to improve recognition, and a separate agent-text delay d_a helps response timing. All duplex experiments use d_u = 1.2 s and d_a = 0.16 s.

Training has a pretraining stage on interleaved speech-to-text conversation data. A supervised fine-tuning stage follows on a mixture of interleaved S2S data, text-to-text conversations, synthesized multi-turn conversational data, multiple-choice QA, single-turn speech instructions and 16k hours of English ASR data. Background noise is added with probability 0.5 at SNRs from -30 to 60 dB during SFT. A standalone ASR variant drops the agent-text head and adds English Granary data (YODAS, YouTube-Commons).

## Key Results

- **Streaming ASR inside the duplex model (Table I):** 10.21% average WER on the Open ASR Leaderboard sets, ranging from 3.9% (LibriSpeech test-clean) to 18.36% (AMI). The authors compare this average with the dedicated streaming FastConformer-80ms (11.71%) and FastConformer-multi (11.27%) from Table V. The lower average comes from the harder sets (SPGI, GigaSpeech, Earnings22, AMI, TED-LIUM). On LibriSpeech clean/other and VoxPopuli the duplex model is worse than both FastConformer models (for example, 3.9% vs 2.57% and 2.19% on test-clean).
- **Turn-taking on the internal set (Table II):** compared with the same model without the ASR head, turn-taking precision rises from 86.1% to 90% and recall falls from 96.9% to 95%. Turn-taking latency rises from 410 to 431 ms. Barge-in accuracy stays at 100% for both, and barge-in latency drops from 393 to 374 ms.
- **Full-Duplex-Bench v1 (Table IV):** against the no-ASR baseline, smooth turn-taking TOR goes from 95.15% to 96.12%, but smooth turn-taking latency nearly doubles (257 to 477 ms). User-interruption TOR goes from 92.5% to 94%, but the interruption GPT score drops from 4.38 to 3.99. Pause-handling TOR (lower is better) improves from 51.4% to 44.4%. Moshi's figures are the published FDB-v1 numbers (TOR 94% smooth, 100% interruption, GPT score 0.77, pause TOR 98%), not a re-run.
- **Intelligence (Table III, VoiceBench):** OpenbookQA rises from 66.59% to 69.01%, AlpacaEval from 3.71 to 3.83, and CommonEval falls from 3.24 to 3.11. Qwen2-Audio (turn-based) scores 67.91%, 4.11 and 3.77, so it is ahead on both open-ended scores. Moshi is far behind on all three (26.15%, 2.01, 1.6).
- **Standalone streaming ASR (Table V):** 8.47% average WER at 1.6 s delay, and 7.73% after adding YODAS and YTC. Nemotron-Speech-0.6B (7.16% at 1.12 s), Qwen3-ASR-1.7B (5.76% at 2 s), Qwen3-ASR-0.6B (6.42%) and Kyutai STT-2.6B (6.4% at 2.5 s) are all better. The lower-latency argument holds only against Qwen3-ASR and Kyutai STT: Nemotron-Speech is both lower-latency and more accurate. In ablations reported in the text only, a 1.2 s delay gives 7.99% and a Qwen2.5-1.5B backbone gives 8.64%.

The comparisons have limits. The duplex-vs-baseline differences have no confidence intervals or significance tests. The turn-taking test set is internal and small (about 60 conversations). Turn timing is measured from predicted agent-text `<bos>`/`<eos>` tokens, not from synthesized speech. The training data of the external ASR models is not matched.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is engineering integration. The base duplex architecture, the multi-channel next-token objective, the Parakeet encoder, CTC forced alignment and delayed text targets all come from existing work. The new part is a second text head with its own embedding table that carries the user transcript as a dedicated parallel channel, trained with an ASR loss. The paper itself describes this as requiring "no significant architectural changes". Its value is the evidence that this channel can be added with little cost to turn-taking, barge-in and response quality, plus the design notes on left alignment and separate user and agent delays. The standalone ASR result shows the architecture can work as a streaming recognizer, but it does not beat current streaming ASR systems.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper shows that a full-duplex speech LM can emit a live user transcript through a lightweight parallel head without a large loss in turn-taking or barge-in behaviour, measured on Full-Duplex-Bench v1 and an internal test set. It is a practical step for deployable duplex agents. The cost appears mainly as higher smooth-turn-taking latency on Full-Duplex-Bench v1 and a small drop in the interruption-response GPT score.

## Claims

- **supports:** A dedicated streaming transcription channel can be added to a full-duplex speech LM with little change to turn-taking and barge-in behaviour.
  > *Evidence:* Adding the ASR head moves turn-taking precision from 86.1% to 90% and recall from 96.9% to 95%, keeps barge-in accuracy at 100%, and changes turn-taking latency from 410 to 431 ms on the internal multi-turn set. *(§IV.B, Table II)*
- **complicates:** Joint user transcription in a duplex speech LM can trade response latency and interruption-response quality for transcript output.
  > *Evidence:* On Full-Duplex-Bench v1, smooth turn-taking latency rises from 257 to 477 ms and the user-interruption GPT score falls from 4.38 to 3.99 relative to the no-ASR baseline. *(§IV.B, Table IV)*
- **supports:** Training a speech LM to also transcribe its user input can improve its spoken question-answering accuracy.
  > *Evidence:* OpenbookQA accuracy rises from 66.59% to 69.01% and AlpacaEval from 3.71 to 3.83 with the ASR head, though CommonEval drops from 3.24 to 3.11. The authors attribute the QA gain to the added text modality. *(§IV.B, Table III)*
- **complicates:** Frame-synchronous streaming recognition inside an LLM-based speech model still trails dedicated streaming ASR systems at comparable latency.
  > *Evidence:* The standalone variant reaches 7.73% average WER at 1.6 s delay, while Nemotron-Speech-0.6B reaches 7.16% at 1.12 s and Kyutai STT-2.6B 6.4% at 2.5 s. Inside the duplex model, WER is 10.21%, and on LibriSpeech test-clean it is 3.9% vs 2.57% for FastConformer-80ms. *(§IV.B, §IV.C, Tables I and V)*
- **refines:** In streaming text-speech alignment for duplex models, separate delays for user transcripts and agent text control the trade-off between recognition accuracy and response timing.
  > *Evidence:* The model uses a user-text delay d_u (1.2 s) and an agent-text delay d_a (0.16 s) as independent hyperparameters. Left-aligning tokens to word onsets works better than right alignment, and lowering the standalone delay from 1.6 s to 1.2 s raises WER from 7.73% to 7.99%. *(§III.C, §IV.C)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- The turn-taking and barge-in evaluation uses an internal set of about 60 recorded conversations, and timing is computed from predicted agent-text tokens rather than synthesized agent speech. No human listening test rates the agent's speech output.
- No variance or significance testing is reported, so the small turn-taking and VoiceBench differences between the ASR and no-ASR models cannot be judged reliably.
- The 1.2 s user-text delay means transcripts lag the user by more than a second. The paper does not measure the latency of the transcript itself in the duplex setting.
- Evaluation is English-only. The streaming TTS that produces agent speech is not evaluated here.
- Code is promised but not released at the time of the preprint. The training data includes in-house ASR data, and the standalone model uses only part of Granary, which the authors give as the likely reason for the gap to Nemotron-Speech.
