> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Kunat Pipatanakul et al.** (Wayu Research / Paxa Labs / Typhoon) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03502) · Demo: ? · Code: ✓
>
> A recipe that turns a short voice reference into a compact 82M-parameter fixed-voice Thai-English TTS model trained only on OmniVoice-generated speech, with controlled ablations of filtering, resampling, initialization and frontend choices, and a Thai evaluation suite for difficult expressions and pause placement.

## Problem

Deploying Thai TTS for a single organization-specific voice (an IVR system, a brand voice) forces a choice between a large zero-shot voice-cloning model that needs reference audio and GPU inference, and a compact fixed-voice model that needs a licensed single-speaker corpus. Using a cloning model as a data generator removes the corpus requirement, but teacher errors become training targets and filtering failed generations removes the hardest texts. Thai adds ambiguous word boundaries, lexical tone, irregular names and loanwords, numeric verbalization and Thai-English code-switching (where the intended rendition is Thai-accented English). The authors also argue that sentence-level CER hides these failures: a sentence can score low CER while mispronouncing its one critical expression, or carry a pause inside a word with an unchanged transcript.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, Thai text is sourced from WangchanThaiInstruct plus an LLM keyword-synthesis pipeline that targets difficult expressions, and an LLM verbalizer rewrites digits and embedded English into pronunciation-oriented Thai or "Tinglish" text, because the teacher sometimes read digits in Chinese and gave English spans the wrong accent. Second, OmniVoice ([[2604.00688|OmniVoice]], 600M) creates 12 seed voices in its Voice Design mode, then renders the corpus in cloning mode from these frozen references. Each candidate passes a quality filter: a CTC Thai ASR (wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53-th) checks every "hard" token (out-of-vocabulary or rare dictionary headword) for an exact phoneme match including tone, and further checks reject disallowed pause positions, speaking rate outside roughly ±10% of the speaker's range, and compressed hard-token durations. The authors prefer a CTC verifier because Whisper-style autoregressive ASR can recover the intended word from context and hide mispronunciations. Pause failures are dropped; other failures are re-rendered up to four times, keeping the best take if none passes. The filter rejects 23.0% of candidates overall, mostly for pause placement (11.3%) and pronunciation (10.4%) (Table 1).

Third, the student is Kokoro-82M, a StyleTTS 2-based fixed-voice model that maps phonemes to speech for a fixed set of voices with no reference audio at inference. The authors add a Thai frontend built on the TLTK grapheme-to-phoneme toolkit; most Thai phones and four of five tones reuse Kokoro's existing IPA tokens, and only the low tone needs a new embedding. A bilingual frontend variant routes Latin spans through Misaki (Kokoro's English G2P) instead of transliterating them. The student initializes PL-BERT, the prosody predictor, text encoder and decoder from Kokoro and the style encoders from a StyleTTS 2 LibriTTS checkpoint, uses StyleTTS 2's ASR aligner and pitch extractor as training-only supervision, and trains new multi-period and multi-resolution discriminators from scratch for eight epochs. The released model adds English utterances rendered from LibriTTS text, for 54.35 hours of synthetic training data.

The evaluation suite has four parts: Thai CER (Typhoon Whisper Large V3, per-utterance CER capped at 100%); Challenge-Set Keyword Accuracy, an exact-match check of one target expression per sentence over 1,531 items in five categories (code-switching, names, rare words, informal spelling, long sentences); Prosody Pause Accuracy on 210 long sentences, scoring each detected pause against allowed positions (author-written spaces and punctuation plus a Gemini-3.1-Pro text-only mask) and reporting pause precision, the pause-placement error rate (PPER, the share of clips with at least one misplaced pause) and the intra-word pause rate; and speaker similarity plus speaking-rate deviation.

## Key Results

On the cumulative recipe ablation (Table 5), pause filtering on the 17.6-hour corpus raises keyword accuracy from 67.5% to 69.6%, lowers CER from 4.0% to 3.5% and raises pause precision from 81.3% to 88.4%. Scaling to 40.58 hours under the content filter lowers PPER and intra-word pauses but drops keyword accuracy (68.7% to 67.2%) and pause precision (88.2% to 85.4%), which the authors attribute to repeated filtering of hard texts. Resampling rejected candidates reverses this: the endpoint (the "Thai-only model") reaches 69.2% keyword accuracy, 3.4% CER, 92.8% pause precision, 6.2% PPER and 1.4% intra-word pauses, the strongest CER and pause values among the five configurations, while the pause-filtered row keeps the highest keyword accuracy (69.6%). Speaker similarity stays within 0.875 to 0.882 across all rows.

Pretrained initialization matters most (Table 6): versus the from-scratch protocol, it lifts keyword accuracy from 51.7% to 69.2%, CER from 6.6% to 3.4%, and code-switch accuracy from 22.8% to 65.5%; name accuracy gains only 6.1 points. Inference-only frontend changes with fixed weights raise keyword accuracy from 68.9% to 70.0% (Table 7); the G2P fix touches fewer inputs (7.3%) yet gains more (0.8 points) than LLM verbalization (12.3% of inputs, 0.3 points).

Against its teacher and Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS (Table 8), the released Wayu-Paxa-TTS-Edge has Thai CER 3.7% (teacher 4.6%, Gemini 3.3%), English CER 1.1% (teacher 0.9%, Gemini 0.8%), pause precision 91.4% (teacher 89.9%, Gemini 96.4%), and the lowest PPER (6.7% vs 17.6% and 13.8%) and intra-word pause rate (1.4% vs 5.2% and 1.9%) of the three systems. It trails both on keyword accuracy (68.2% vs 72.8% and 79.8%). PPER counts clips with any misplaced pause, and the student pauses less often (0.89 pauses per clip against 1.79 and 4.44), so the PPER comparison favors sparse pausers; pause precision is the authors' primary placement measure. Adding English cuts English CER from 4.4% to 1.1% relative to the Thai-only model, at a cost of 1.0 point keyword accuracy and 1.4 points pause precision. Speaker similarity (0.882 vs teacher 0.899) is measured against a centroid of teacher-generated utterances, and Gemini's 0.816 uses different (Kore) references, so that column is not comparable across all three.

The teacher analysis shows oracle best-of-K sampling raises Challenge-Set exact accuracy from 72.8% at K=1 to 87.9% at K=118 (Table 10). At K=118, coverage is 66% for keywords unseen in the teacher's Thai training data and 98% for keywords seen 100 to 10k times, with a frequency-difficulty correlation of ρ = -0.437 over 1,060 Thai keywords (Table 11). An Isan adaptation from a 15-second reference (1.5 hours synthetic, four epochs) yields 5.5% Isan CER against the teacher's 6.6% and 0.842 speaker similarity against 0.854, but degrades Central-Thai CER (3.4% to 5.1%) and keyword accuracy (69.2% to 65.8%) (Table 9).

## Novelty Assessment

The model is an unmodified Kokoro/StyleTTS 2 student with one added tone embedding and a TLTK frontend, trained on data from an off-the-shelf zero-shot teacher; distilling a cloning model into a fixed-voice model through synthetic data is an engineering recipe, not a new method. The value lies in the controlled, cumulative ablations under a matched sampling budget, which isolate the coverage cost of strict filtering and the benefit of resampling rejected texts, and in the best-of-K teacher-support analysis that ties residual errors to teacher training-data frequency. The Challenge Set and pause scorer are a genuine Thai evaluation contribution, but neither is validated against human listeners: the pause scorer's aligner is validated only against a duration-predictor reference on synthetic clips (Appendix A.2, Table 14), and keyword accuracy depends on one Thai ASR model.

## Field Significance

Low — The paper provides a well-controlled data point on distilling a zero-shot cloning teacher into a compact fixed-voice student for a low-resource, tonal, code-switched language, showing that filtering and resampling choices shift pronunciation and pause metrics by several points. Its evaluation is automatic-only and centered on one language (with English as a secondary output), so the evidence is primarily an engineering recipe plus a reusable Thai test suite rather than a general advance.

## Claims

- **supports:** Filtering synthetic teacher outputs for misplaced pauses improves both the phrasing and the content accuracy of a student TTS model trained on them.
  > *Evidence:* At a fixed 17.6-hour corpus, removing candidates with disallowed pause positions raises pause precision from 81.3% to 88.4%, keyword accuracy from 67.5% to 69.6%, and lowers CER from 4.0% to 3.5% and speaking-rate deviation from 12.4% to 8.2%. *(§4.1, Table 5)*
- **complicates:** Strict quality filtering of synthetic training data trades away coverage of difficult texts, and resampling rejected texts rather than discarding them is needed to recover it.
  > *Evidence:* Scaling from 17.63 to 40.58 hours under the content filter drops keyword accuracy from 68.7% to 67.2% and pause precision from 88.2% to 85.4%; resampling rejected candidates then raises them to 69.2% and 92.8% and cuts PPER from 11.9% to 6.2%. *(§4.1, Table 5)*
- **complicates:** Sentence-level character error rate leaves pronunciation errors on difficult expressions and misplaced pauses largely unmeasured.
  > *Evidence:* Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS and OmniVoice differ by 1.3 points in Thai CER but by 7.0 points in Challenge-Set Keyword Accuracy, and an example shows OmniVoice placing a 438 ms intra-word pause in a word that both systems' transcripts render correctly. *(§3.4, Tables 3-4, Figure 5)*
- **supports:** Pretrained initialization is critical for a compact student trained on a few dozen hours of synthetic speech, with the largest effect on code-switched words.
  > *Evidence:* Relative to StyleTTS 2's from-scratch protocol, initializing from Kokoro and StyleTTS 2 checkpoints raises keyword accuracy from 51.7% to 69.2%, code-switch accuracy from 22.8% to 65.5%, and pause precision from 82.0% to 92.8%, while name accuracy gains only 47.1% to 53.2%. *(§4.2, Table 6)*
- **refines:** Distillation from a stochastic TTS teacher is bounded by the teacher's support: repeated sampling recovers correct realizations mainly for expressions well represented in the teacher's training data.
  > *Evidence:* Oracle best-of-K raises teacher exact accuracy from 72.8% (K=1) to 87.9% (K=118), but at K=118 coverage is 66% for keywords unseen in the teacher's Thai data versus 98% for those seen 100 to 10k times (ρ = -0.437). *(§5.1-5.2, Tables 10-11)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> No human listening test is reported. Every quality claim rests on automatic measures: ASR-based CER and keyword accuracy (one Thai ASR model), a heuristic pause scorer validated only against a synthetic duration-predictor reference, and speaker similarity measured against a centroid of teacher-generated utterances. Naturalness, tone correctness as perceived by listeners, and Isan dialect fidelity are untested.

- The 12 target voices are themselves OmniVoice-designed, so speaker similarity measures fidelity to the teacher's rendering rather than to a real speaker.
- The student trails its teacher and Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS on keyword accuracy (68.2% vs 72.8% and 79.8%), the metric targeting the hardest Thai cases.
- Gemini's parameter count (≥405B) is a literature-derived lower-bound proxy, not a reported figure, and its speaker similarity uses different references.
- The Isan adaptation disables the pronunciation filter (the Central-Thai verifier rejects dialect forms) and degrades Central-Thai performance; the authors note CER does not establish dialect fidelity.
- Best-of-K is an oracle analysis on the test set; it shows teacher headroom but not a deployable selection method.
- The authors note that both new metrics rely on rule-based Thai tokenization and G2P and are most reliable as comparisons between systems measured with the same instrument.
