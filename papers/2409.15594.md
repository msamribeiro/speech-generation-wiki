>
> SyncLLM turns a pretrained text LLM (Llama3-8b) into a full-duplex spoken dialogue model by interleaving fixed-duration chunks of both speakers' deduplicated HuBERT units with periodic synchronization tokens, so the model runs in step with a wall clock and can produce overlaps, backchannels and turn-taking without explicit turn boundaries.

## Problem

Spoken dialogue models built on LLMs were half-duplex: they speak only when prompted, relying on explicit user input or end-of-utterance silence to change turns. Human dialogue is full-duplex, with overlaps, backchannels and gaps that are often shorter than within-turn pauses. The authors identify four obstacles: LLMs have no notion of real-world time; spoken dialogue data is scarce (about 3k hours across the main corpora, against much larger text dialogue resources); a full-duplex agent must stream continuously because it may need to speak at any moment; and a cloud-hosted agent sees the user's speech only after network delay. The closest prior system, dGSLM, models two channels with a dual-tower Transformer trained on speech only, so it cannot draw on text pretraining and assumes zero-latency access to the other speaker's state (§1, §2).

## Method

SyncLLM keeps the standard decoder-only architecture of Llama3-8b and changes only the token sequence it models (§3). Speech is represented with HuBERT units at 25 Hz (one token per 40 ms) with a 501-unit vocabulary, following the tokenization of Spirit LM; the vocabulary of Llama3 is extended with these units and two speaker tags, [S0] and [S1] (§3.2, §4). A dialogue is represented as two parallel unit streams, one per speaker, cut into chunks of a fixed duration (the chunk size: 160, 200 or 240 ms) and interleaved chunk by chunk. The speaker tag recurs once per chunk and acts as a synchronization token, so position in the sequence maps to wall-clock time.

Raw HuBERT streams contain long runs of repeated units, mostly from silence; Figure 3 shows deduplicated sequences carry roughly twice the semantic content per token. SyncLLM therefore predicts deduplicated units within each chunk, while the periodic [S0] tag preserves coarse timing. Speaker 1's tag is dropped in chunks where speaker 1 has no new units, but [S0] is always present so chunk boundaries stay unambiguous. At synthesis time, the deduplicated units in each chunk are repeated evenly to fill the chunk's original token count before a HiFi-GAN unit vocoder renders audio; the authors report the resulting duration error is imperceptible even at 240 ms chunks because the error is bounded by the chunk size (§3.2, §7).

(Bottom row) We interpolate deduplicated tokens in each chunk to obtain spoken dialogue sequence in the original format.](assets/2409.15594/figure-2.png)

For interaction with a real user, the model's own prediction of the user stream is replaced by the user's actual speech. To tolerate transmission latency, at chunk N the model has the user's speech only up to chunk N-1; it first predicts an estimated user chunk N, appends it to its context, and generates its own chunk N+1, then discards the estimate once the real user chunk arrives (§3.1). This one-chunk look-ahead is what lets chunk sizes of 160 to 240 ms absorb network latencies of the same size.

Training has three stages (§4, Table 1). Stage 1 converts text supervised-fine-tuning dialogue datasets to speech with Bark TTS (10 speaker presets), about 193k hours, and trains turn-based spoken dialogue with sentence-level text/speech interleaving: each sentence is randomly text or deduplicated units, with the speech fraction sampled from a truncated normal distribution that shifts towards more speech over training (Figure 4); the authors report that all-speech training or stepwise speech increments were unstable. Stage 2 synthesizes about 20k hours of short-turn dialogue in the two-stream chunked format, filling the non-speaking channel with silence, so the model learns the synchronous format without overlaps. Stage 3 fine-tunes on real two-channel conversations from Fisher (1927 hours, 98:1:1 split), where the model learns pauses, overlaps and backchannels. Stage 1 trains on 128 A100 GPUs with 1M-token batches for 40k iterations; stages 2 and 3 use 6000 and 2000 iterations (Appendix A.1). The model is labeled SyncLLM-F when the third stage uses Fisher and SyncLLM-C when it uses CANDOR.

## Key Results

All comparisons are against dGSLM, the only full-duplex baseline, which like SyncLLM-F uses Fisher as its only real spoken dialogue data. Evaluation is in a continuation setting (the model generates both sides of a dialogue from a spoken prompt) and an interaction setting (two SyncLLM instances talk to each other with one-chunk latency).

- **Semantics (ASR perplexity, Figure 5).** Generated dialogues are transcribed and scored by a text dialogue model. The authors read dGSLM's median perplexity as about 70 above ground truth, against about 15 for SyncLLM across chunk sizes; exact values are given only in the figure. Figure 6 shows the same ordering on the out-of-distribution CANDOR prompts (§5.1).
- **Turn-taking timing (Table 2).** Pearson correlation of IPU, pause and floor-transfer-offset durations between generations and ground-truth continuations. SyncLLM-F averages 0.41 to 0.43 on Fisher (dGSLM 0.33) and 0.23 to 0.31 on CANDOR (dGSLM 0.14). The gain is not uniform per event: on Fisher pauses the 240 ms model scores 0.40 against dGSLM's 0.41. The resynthesized-ground-truth topline (0.79 and 0.71 averages) shows how much timing variance the HuBERT tokenization itself introduces (§5.2).
- **Human evaluation (Table 3).** 32 native-level English raters scored 180 prompt-continuation items (three raters per item, 160 ms chunk) for Meaningfulness (M-MOS) and turn-taking Naturalness (N-MOS) on a 5-point scale. Overall M-MOS is 3.74 for SyncLLM-F and 3.40 for SyncLLM-C against 1.55 for dGSLM, with resynthesized ground truth at 3.87. Overall N-MOS is at parity: 3.90 (SyncLLM-F), 3.96 (SyncLLM-C), 3.95 (dGSLM), 4.03 (resynthesis). Per subset, dGSLM has the higher N-MOS on Fisher (4.21 against 3.98) and the lower on CANDOR (3.70 against 3.82). Moving from Fisher to out-of-distribution CANDOR, dGSLM drops 0.24 M-MOS and 0.51 N-MOS, SyncLLM-F drops 0.15 and 0.16; SyncLLM-C, trained on CANDOR, drops 0.52 M-MOS on Fisher with N-MOS unchanged (+0.03) (§5.3).
- **Full-duplex interaction (Figure 7, Table 4).** Two-agent interaction closely matches continuation-mode perplexity (Figure 7). In human ratings, SyncLLM-F-F scores 3.47 M-MOS and 3.72 N-MOS and the cross-dataset pairing SyncLLM-F-C scores 3.39 and 3.78, above dGSLM's 1.55 M-MOS but below single-model continuation (3.74 and 3.90) (§5.4).
- **Latency (Figure 8, Table 6).** Interaction quality is stable up to 200 ms of latency and degrades beyond it; turn-taking correlation for SyncLLM-F-F falls from 0.32 (160 ms) and 0.31 (200 ms) to 0.28 (240 ms) (Appendix C).
- **Interleaving ablation (Table 5).** Sentence-level text/speech interleaving in stage 1 scores above turn-level interleaving on all four ZeroSpeech 2021 tasks: sWUGGY 70.3 against 63.0, sBLIMP 56.3 against 56.0, Topic-StoryCloze 83.0 against 76.5, StoryCloze 61.8 against 55.1 (Appendix A.2).

The dGSLM comparison is uneven in one direction the paper acknowledges: dGSLM's speech representations are fine-tuned on Fisher, while SyncLLM uses general-purpose HuBERT units (§5.3). SyncLLM also benefits from 8B-parameter text pretraining and about 212k hours of synthetic speech (the abstract figure; Table 1 lists 193k + 20k) that dGSLM does not see, so the M-MOS gap measures the whole recipe rather than the synchronization mechanism alone.

## Novelty Assessment

The core novelty is a sequence format, not a new network: periodic synchronization tokens that tie an unmodified decoder-only LLM to wall-clock time, combined with per-chunk deduplication that keeps HuBERT sequences semantically dense while preserving coarse timing, and interpolation to recover a fixed frame rate for the vocoder. The latency-tolerant inference procedure, which predicts the user's next chunk before generating the agent's own and then overwrites the estimate with real input, is also new. Together these amount to a new representation and inference procedure that lets a pretrained text LLM operate as a full-duplex agent, which dGSLM's dual-tower design could not do. The three-stage recipe that bootstraps from TTS-synthesized text dialogue before small-scale real two-channel data is a training contribution; its individual stages are not ablated against each other, except the stage 1 interleaving granularity. The components themselves (HuBERT units, Llama3, Bark TTS, HiFi-GAN unit vocoding, vocabulary extension) are existing.

## Field Significance

> [!tip] High
> high — SyncLLM demonstrates that full-duplex spoken dialogue, including overlaps and backchannels, can be obtained from an unmodified pretrained text LLM by changing only the token sequence format, and that doing so yields far more meaningful dialogue content than a speech-only two-channel model at comparable turn-taking naturalness. It also provides a concrete recipe for compensating the scarcity of two-channel conversational data with TTS-synthesized dialogue, and an explicit treatment of network latency in full-duplex inference.

## Claims

- **supports:** Initializing a full-duplex spoken dialogue model from a pretrained text LLM yields substantially more semantically coherent dialogue than a speech-only dual-channel model trained on the same real conversational data.
  > *Evidence:* SyncLLM-F (Llama3-8b, Fisher as the only real dialogue data) reaches 3.74 overall M-MOS against 1.55 for dGSLM, and median ASR perplexity roughly 15 above ground truth against roughly 70 for dGSLM. *(§5.1, Figure 5; §5.3, Table 3)*
- **supports:** Interleaving fixed-duration chunks of two speakers' units with periodic synchronization tokens lets a standard decoder-only language model represent overlaps, pauses and turn transitions without a dedicated multi-stream architecture.
  > *Evidence:* SyncLLM-F's turn-taking event-duration correlation with ground truth averages 0.41 to 0.43 on Fisher and 0.23 to 0.31 on CANDOR, above dGSLM's 0.33 and 0.14, while N-MOS stays at parity (3.90 against 3.95 overall). *(§3.2; §5.2, Table 2; §5.3, Table 3)*
- **complicates:** Gains in content meaningfulness for full-duplex speech LMs do not translate into higher perceived turn-taking naturalness, which can remain at or below a speech-only baseline in-domain.
  > *Evidence:* On Fisher, dGSLM's N-MOS is 4.21 against 3.98 for SyncLLM-F, while SyncLLM-F leads on M-MOS (3.82 against 1.67); the advantage in naturalness appears only on out-of-distribution CANDOR (3.82 against 3.70). *(§5.3, Table 3)*
- **refines:** Anticipating the interlocutor's next speech chunk allows a full-duplex agent to tolerate transmission latency on the order of its chunk size, but quality degrades once latency exceeds roughly 200 ms.
  > *Evidence:* Two-agent interaction with one-chunk latency keeps turn-taking correlation at 0.32 and 0.31 for 160 and 200 ms but drops to 0.28 at 240 ms, and two-agent M-MOS (3.47) falls below single-model continuation (3.74). *(§3.1; §5.4, Table 4; Appendix C, Table 6, Figure 8)*
- **supports:** Large volumes of TTS-synthesized dialogue converted from text corpora can substitute for most real conversational speech when training a spoken dialogue LM, with finer-grained text/speech interleaving giving better spoken language understanding.
  > *Evidence:* About 212k hours of TTS-synthesized dialogue (193k + 20k in Table 1) precede only 1927 hours of real Fisher data; in stage 1, sentence-level interleaving outperforms turn-level interleaving on all four ZeroSpeech 2021 tasks (e.g., sWUGGY 70.3 against 63.0, StoryCloze 61.8 against 55.1). *(§4, Table 1; Appendix A.2, Table 5)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- Speech quality is bounded by a simple HiFi-GAN unit vocoder on 25 Hz HuBERT units; the resynthesized ground truth reaches only 3.87 M-MOS and 0.79 Fisher turn-taking correlation, so the tokenizer itself caps achievable quality, and acoustic quality is not measured directly (§7, Table 2, Table 3).
- Expressivity and non-verbal sounds such as laughter are not modeled or evaluated (§7).
- Context length is inherited from Llama3 (8192 tokens), which limits long dialogue modeling and rules out higher-rate multi-codebook tokenizers such as EnCodec (§7, Appendix A.1).
- Full-duplex interaction is only simulated between two model instances; no live human-agent conversations are evaluated, and responsiveness to genuine user interruptions is not tested (§5.4).
- dGSLM is the only baseline; no cascaded ASR-LLM-TTS system or half-duplex speech LM is compared, and the contribution of each training stage is not ablated beyond interleaving granularity.
- Only English telephone and video-call conversations (Fisher, CANDOR) are used; speaker identity of the generated voices is not controlled, and raters were told continuation voices may differ from the prompt (Appendix B).
