> **Kun Zhou et al.** (National University of Singapore / Singapore University of Technology and Design / University of Bremen) · [→ Paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2021.11.006) · [→ Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14762) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✓
>
> Releases ESD, a parallel emotional speech database of 20 speakers (10 English, 10 Mandarin) reading 350 sentences in five emotions, framed by a review of emotional voice conversion methods and a database survey that shows why earlier emotional corpora fit synthesis poorly.

## Problem

Emotional voice conversion (EVC) changes the emotional state of an utterance while keeping its linguistic content and speaker identity. Unlike speaker voice conversion, which mostly maps the spectrum and carries prosody over, EVC must also convert supra-segmental prosody (F0, energy, duration). Data-driven EVC, especially speaker-independent and non-parallel EVC, needs a large multi-speaker emotional corpus. The authors' survey of 19 existing emotional databases (Table 1) finds that each falls short on at least one axis: few sentences (RAVDESS has 2, CREMA-D 12, EmoDB 10), few or single-gender speakers (DES, JL-Corpus, SAVEE), English dominance, confounds such as accents, laughter and overlapping speech (eNTERFACE'05, IEMOCAP), or noisy film and TV recordings (CHEAVD, VAM). Speaker VC corpora such as VCTK and the VCC sets are emotion-free.

## Method

The paper has three parts.

**Review of EVC (§2).** It covers emotion representation (categorical vs. dimensional, Brunswik-style perception and production models), then groups EVC methods by training data. Parallel methods follow a feature extraction, frame alignment and feature mapping pipeline, using GMM, exemplar, DNN/DBLSTM or attention-based sequence-to-sequence mapping, often with CWT-decomposed F0. Non-parallel methods fall into three families: emotional domain translation (CycleGAN, StarGAN), disentanglement of emotional style from content with auto-encoders (VAE-GAN, VAW-GAN conditioned on a one-hot emotion code or a deep emotional feature), and leveraging TTS or ASR (multi-task VC and TTS training, PPG inputs from a speaker-independent ASR).

**ESD database (§4).** Ten native North American English speakers and ten native Mandarin speakers (gender-balanced, aged 25 to 35) each read the same 350 parallel sentences in Neutral, Happy, Angry, Sad and Surprise. Recordings are studio-quality (SNR above 20 dB) at 16 kHz, and speakers were told to avoid laughter, sighs and similar confounds. Each speaker-emotion set is split into 20 evaluation, 30 test and 300 training utterances (Table 2). The corpus totals about 29 hours: 56,385 s of Chinese and 48,370 s of English (Table 3). The English scripts contain 997 unique words and the Chinese scripts 939 unique characters. The authors validate emotional consistency with an LSTM speech emotion recognizer on 312-dimensional openSMILE frame features, and visualize its intermediate "deep emotional features" with t-SNE.

**Reference EVC experiments (§5).** Two released EVC baselines from the same group are trained on one English male speaker (0013): CycleGAN-EVC, a pair-wise domain-translation model with adversarial, cycle-consistency and identity-mapping losses, and VAWGAN-EVC, a VAW-GAN that disentangles a content latent and recomposes it with an emotion code. One model per pair (neutral to angry, happy, sad and surprise) is trained without frame alignment, so neither uses the parallel structure. Evaluation uses 24-dimensional MCEP MCD, a 5-point MOS test, and an XAB emotion-similarity preference test with 11 listeners, each hearing 72 utterances.

## Key Results

- **Emotion consistency.** The speaker-independent SER model reaches 92.0% overall accuracy on the Chinese test data and 89.0% on English (Fig. 9). Per-class recall ranges from 0.89 to 0.99 for Chinese and from 0.86 to 0.93 for English.
- **Acoustic statistics.** Within each emotion, female speakers have a higher F0 mean than male speakers in both languages. Happy and Surprise show the two highest F0 standard deviations in every gender-language group and the two highest F0 means in three of the four groups; for Chinese male speakers, Angry (144.34 Hz) sits just above Happy (142.27 Hz) (Table 4). Sad utterances are longest in both languages (Chinese 4.04 s vs. 3.22 s overall; English 2.98 s vs. 2.76 s; Table 3).
- **Objective EVC.** Both baselines lower MCD relative to the zero-effort source-target distance in all four pairs. CycleGAN-EVC is lower than VAWGAN-EVC in every pair, for example 4.32 vs. 5.31 dB on Neutral-to-Sad and 4.57 vs. 5.89 dB on Neutral-to-Angry (Table 5).
- **Subjective EVC.** In the MOS test (Fig. 11), CycleGAN-EVC scores above VAWGAN-EVC in all four pairs, and both score below the natural target speech. The paper gives this result as a bar chart only, with no numeric values. In XAB emotion similarity (Fig. 12), CycleGAN-EVC is preferred for Neutral-to-Angry (67% vs. 8%), Neutral-to-Surprise (54% vs. 13%) and Neutral-to-Happy (50% vs. 29%), and ties for Neutral-to-Sad (29% vs. 29%, 42% no difference).
- **Cost trade-off.** CycleGAN-EVC took about 72 hours to train on one GTX 1080 Ti, against about 7 hours for VAWGAN-EVC. VAWGAN-EVC also supports many-to-many emotion conversion with one model (§5.4).

The baseline comparison is narrow: one English speaker, two systems from the authors' own group, 11 listeners, and no statistical tests on the preference results.

## Novelty Assessment

The main contribution is the dataset. ESD combines parallel text across five emotions, 20 speakers, two balanced languages, 350 sentences per emotion and controlled studio recording. Table 1 lists no previous database with that combination; the paper calls ESD "one of the largest emotional speech databases" publicly available in speaker and lexical variability. The review portion organizes EVC literature by data regime and divides non-parallel methods into three families. That taxonomy is useful but descriptive, and it does not reframe the problem. The EVC experiments are a reference benchmark, not a new method. Both systems are earlier work by the same authors, trained on a single speaker, so they show that ESD can be used for EVC, not how methods compare across ESD's multi-speaker or cross-lingual settings.

## Field Significance

> [!tip]
> High: ESD supplies the parallel, multi-speaker, bilingual and studio-quality emotional speech data that the paper's own survey shows earlier corpora lacked, which makes speaker-independent and cross-lingual emotional VC and emotional TTS studies feasible on a shared public corpus. The accompanying review and baselines provide a reference point for EVC methods as of 2021.

## Claims

- **supports:** Emotion conversion requires converting prosody as well as the spectrum, because emotional expression is carried by supra-segmental F0, energy and duration patterns that frame-wise spectral mapping cannot capture.
  > *Evidence:* The review contrasts speaker VC, which treats prosody as speaker-independent and focuses on spectral mapping, with EVC, where CWT-based multi-scale F0 modeling and sequence-to-sequence duration mapping are needed. ESD's statistics show systematic emotion-dependent shifts in F0 mean and variance and in utterance duration in both languages. *(§1, §2.2.1, §6.1, Tables 3 and 4)*
- **supports:** Emotional speech corpora built for emotion recognition are poorly suited to emotional speech synthesis and conversion, which need clean recordings, many sentences, many speakers and controlled confounds.
  > *Evidence:* Among 19 surveyed databases, many have very few sentences (RAVDESS 2, EmoDB 10, CREMA-D 12), few or single-gender speakers, accented or conversational confounds, or film and TV noise (IEMOCAP, MSP-IMPROV, CHEAVD). Nine of the 19 are English-only. *(§3.1 to §3.5, Table 1)*
- **refines:** Emotion categories in acted parallel speech share cross-lingual acoustic regularities, so a bilingual parallel corpus can support cross-lingual emotional modeling.
  > *Evidence:* In both English and Mandarin ESD data, low-arousal negative emotions (Sad) have longer and more variable utterance durations than high-arousal positive ones (Happy). Happy and Surprise have the two highest F0 standard deviations for both genders in both languages, and the two highest F0 means in all groups except Chinese male speakers, where Angry edges out Happy. *(§4.3.2, §4.3.3, Fig. 8, Table 4)*
- **complicates:** In low-data emotion conversion, domain-translation models can beat disentanglement-based auto-encoders on quality and emotion similarity, but at a much higher training cost and with less flexible many-to-many control.
  > *Evidence:* On one ESD speaker, CycleGAN-EVC has lower MCD than VAWGAN-EVC in all four pairs (e.g. 4.32 vs. 5.31 dB Neutral-to-Sad) and higher MOS in all four. It wins XAB emotion similarity in three pairs and ties on Neutral-to-Sad. It trains in about 72 hours against 7 hours and needs one model per emotion pair. *(§5.2 to §5.4, Table 5, Figs. 11 and 12)*
- **complicates:** Listener-judged emotion similarity is an imperfect target for emotion conversion, because listeners' perception of the emotion can diverge from the speaker's intended emotion.
  > *Evidence:* The XAB test treats the target speaker's intended emotion as ground truth, and the authors note a mismatch between listener assessment and speaker intention that they leave to future work. The SER validation model shows a related confusion between high-arousal categories: Surprise is classified as Happy for 7% of test utterances in both languages. *(§5.4, §4.4, Fig. 9)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

The EVC benchmark covers one English male speaker, neutral-source conversion only, and two baselines from the authors' own group. ESD's multi-speaker, speaker-independent and cross-lingual uses are argued but not demonstrated in this paper. MOS results appear only as a bar chart, and the 11-listener tests report no significance testing for the preference results.

- ESD is acted, read speech with five categorical emotions and no intensity or dimensional labels. Acted portrayals can be stereotypical (§4.1), which limits how well models trained on it transfer to spontaneous emotional speech.
- Audio is 16 kHz, below the 22 to 48 kHz rates common in modern TTS.
- Each language has 10 speakers of a single age group (25 to 35) and a single accent (North American English, standard Mandarin). This controls confounds but limits speaker and accent diversity.
- The release is "available for research purpose upon request" (§4.2) rather than freely downloadable from the paper.
- Emotional validity is checked with an automatic SER model rather than a human perceptual labelling study of the recordings.
