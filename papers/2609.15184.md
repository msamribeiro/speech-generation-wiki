>
> Fine-tunes a pretrained F5-TTS on prompts that the same model synthesizes in each training speaker's voice, which removes both the audio-prompt transcript at inference and the forced-alignment step its predecessor needed to build training pairs.

## Problem

F5-TTS uses the audio-prompt transcript twice. The transcript fills the prompt part of the text condition, and its length relative to the target text sets the output duration. Without the transcript, both roles break, so cross-lingual cloning from a prompt in an unfamiliar or low-resource language is hard. The predecessor, Cross-Lingual F5-TTS, removed the transcript by splitting real utterances into prompt and target segments with MMS forced alignment, and by estimating duration with a syllable-level speaking rate predictor (SRP). Both parts leave problems. Forced alignment makes boundary errors, and its cost grows as more languages are added. The SRP is also unreliable when the prompt starts or ends with silence: the silence lowers the estimated speaking rate, so the predicted duration is too long and the output speech is stretched.

## Method

Cross-Lingual F5-TTS 2 starts from pretrained F5-TTS (F5-TTS-Base: 22-layer Diffusion Transformer, 16 heads, 1024-dim embeddings) and keeps its conditional flow-matching (OT-CFM) text-guided infilling objective. The paper does not report a parameter count. The model generates mel spectrograms, and a pretrained Vocos vocoder converts them to waveforms, so no neural codec is used.

**Real-synthetic pair construction.** No forced aligner is used. Each real Emilia utterance acts as the audio prompt for the pretrained F5-TTS, which synthesizes a new utterance from a same-language text pool in the same speaker's voice. This synthetic clip becomes the training prompt, and the original real recording becomes the target. The acoustic input concatenates the synthetic prompt and the real target, and the infilling mask exposes only the prompt.

**Prompt-token text conditioning.** Pretrained F5-TTS expects the prompt transcript first, then the target text, then filler tokens. Simply deleting the prompt transcript would shift the target text into positions the model associates with the prompt. The method instead fills that region with N learnable prompt tokens, followed by the ". " sequence that F5-TTS already treats as an end-of-sequence (EOS) marker, and then the target characters and filler tokens. N is set from the prompt-to-target duration ratio, so the placeholder occupies roughly the space the transcript would have taken.

![Fig. 1. Overview of the proposed SFT pipeline. (Left) Synthetic Data Generation : a pretrained F5-TTS model is used to synthesize speaker-consistent audio pairs. (Right) SFT with Synthetic Prompt : Cross-Lingual F5-TTS 2 is initialized from pretrained F5-TTS and fine-tuned on the constructed real-synthetic pairs, where the audio prompt transcript is replaced by learnable prompt tokens followed by an EOS marker, forcing the model to condition on acoustic prompts without relying on their transcripts.](assets/2609.15184/figure-1.png)

**Silence-robust speaking rate predictor.** Duration comes from the syllable-level SRP of the predecessor: a 6-layer transformer over mel spectrograms, trained with a Gaussian cross-entropy loss over speaking-rate classes. The architecture and labels do not change. During training, 70% of prompts stay as they are, and 10% each get silence added at the start, at the end, or on both sides. Each silence segment lasts 30% to 70% of the prompt duration. The SRP trains on 500 hours each of Emilia English and Chinese.

**Training and inference.** Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) runs for 100K updates on eight A100s. The main model uses the full English and Chinese Emilia pool (95,282 real hours plus 29,169 synthetic prompt hours). Smaller subsets of 1K, 2K and 5K real hours are selected by DNSMOS. Inference uses the F5-TTS settings: Euler solver, NFE 32, CFG strength 2.0 and sway sampling -1.0.

## Key Results

**Intra-lingual evaluation (Table II).** The comparison covers F5-TTS, which still uses the prompt transcript and a text-length ratio for duration, and Cross-Lingual F5-TTS. Cross-Lingual F5-TTS 2 has the highest SIM-o on all three test sets: 0.687 vs. 0.668/0.658 on LibriSpeech-PC test-clean, 0.683 vs. 0.676/0.659 on Seed-TTS test-en, and 0.768 vs. 0.762/0.763 on test-zh. WER is lowest on LibriSpeech-PC (2.014% vs. 2.205/2.144). It is highest of the three on Seed-TTS test-en (1.629 vs. 1.545/1.613) and test-zh (1.594 vs. 1.475/1.521), although the gaps are small. Naturalness is lowest on every set. UTMOS is 3.704/3.544/2.761, against 3.797/3.582/2.898 for F5-TTS and 3.893/3.635/2.901 for Cross-Lingual F5-TTS. CMOS against ground truth is +0.150/-0.064/-0.068, against +0.480/+0.141/+0.508 and +0.258/+0.003/+0.677. SMOS falls between the two baselines on all three sets: 4.216/4.082/4.098, below F5-TTS (4.324/4.148/4.290) and above Cross-Lingual F5-TTS (4.063/4.051/4.064). The listening test used only 15 utterances per test set, and the paper does not report the number of raters. The authors explain the gap between SIM-o and SMOS as a timbre gain combined with flatter prosody inherited from the synthetic prompts.

**Cross-lingual cloning with out-of-distribution prompts (Table III).** Prompts come from eight languages not seen in training (Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian, French, German, Russian, Maltese), with English or Chinese targets. Against Cross-Lingual F5-TTS only, version 2 has higher SIM-o on 14 of 16 pairs. The two exceptions are Vietnamese prompts with English and with Chinese targets. UTMOS is lower on all 16 pairs. WER is lower on 8 of 16 pairs and swings both ways: Thai-to-Chinese rises from 3.938% to 5.030%, and Russian-to-English falls from 4.337% to 3.614%. F5-TTS is not evaluated in this setting.

**Text-conditioning ablation (Table IV).** Removing the placeholder entirely (A0) gives WER 3.022/7.116/5.066%. Filler tokens (A1) reduce this to 2.028/2.994/1.900%, and learnable prompt tokens (A2) to 1.984/2.018/1.621%. Adding the EOS marker (A3) gives 2.014/1.629/1.594%. A3 is lowest on both Seed-TTS sets, and A2 is slightly lower on LibriSpeech-PC. SIM-o barely changes across variants (0.686 to 0.688 on LibriSpeech-PC). The WER curves in Fig. 3 show that the EOS marker mainly speeds up convergence, most visibly on Seed-TTS test-en.

**Data scale (Table V).** Fine-tuning on 1,000 real hours (B0) already comes close to the full pool. SIM-o rises by at most 0.004 from B0 to B3 (0.683 to 0.687, 0.679 to 0.683, 0.765 to 0.768). WER and UTMOS show no consistent trend, and B1 has the lowest test-zh WER (1.552%).

**Silence robustness (Tables VI and VII).** The test pads each prompt with silence equal to 50% of its duration on each side. The original SRP then fails: MAE is 4.495/3.034/4.708 s and MRE 70.2/71.0/85.5%. The augmented SRP keeps MAE at 0.817/0.652/1.034 s and MRE at 13.2/15.4/18.9%. With the same Cross-Lingual F5-TTS 2 model, padded-prompt WER drops from 3.463/3.241/4.006% with the original durations to 2.454/1.919/1.738% with the robust ones. On clean prompts, augmentation costs a little: MRE rises by 0.15/0.56/0.84 points, and WER rises from 1.979/1.626/1.494% to 2.014/1.629/1.594%.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is a training recipe plus a small change to the input layout. The backbone, the flow-matching objective and the SRP architecture are inherited unchanged. Synthetic speech as TTS training data and same-speaker prompt-target pairs are both established ideas (the paper cites MOSS-TTS, IndexTTS and work on training with purely synthetic data). The new step is using the pretrained model itself to produce transcript-free prompts, so that a short fine-tuning stage replaces forced-alignment segmentation. The learnable prompt tokens with a reused EOS cue are a targeted way to keep the pretrained text-audio layout, and the ablation supports them clearly. Silence augmentation for the SRP is a simple data-augmentation fix for a failure mode the authors identified in their own earlier system.

The evaluation is narrow in scope. Both baselines come from the same group, and the cross-lingual table compares only against the predecessor. No other transcript-free cloning system (speaker-embedding modes, MOSS-TTS, IndexTTS) is compared. The SIM-o gains are small (+0.005 to +0.029 on the intra-lingual sets), and they come with a consistent loss in UTMOS and CMOS.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper shows that a flow-matching TTS model can drop its dependence on prompt transcripts and forced alignment by fine-tuning on prompts it synthesizes itself. A few thousand hours are enough to recover most of the benefit. It also gives concrete evidence that self-generated prompts trade prosodic naturalness for timbre similarity, and that duration predictors based on speaking rate need explicit robustness to prompt silence.

## Claims

- **supports:** Fine-tuning a pretrained zero-shot TTS model on prompts synthesized by the model itself can remove the need for prompt transcripts at inference without forced-alignment preprocessing.
  > *Evidence:* Cross-Lingual F5-TTS 2 is trained on real targets paired with F5-TTS-synthesized same-speaker prompts and run without prompt transcripts. It keeps WER at 2.014/1.629/1.594% on LibriSpeech-PC test-clean, Seed-TTS test-en and test-zh, close to transcript-conditioned F5-TTS (2.205/1.545/1.475%). It also clones speakers from prompts in eight unseen languages. *(§III-B, §V-A, §V-B, Tables II and III)*
- **complicates:** Training on synthetic voice-cloning prompts improves objective speaker similarity but lowers naturalness, because the synthetic prompts carry flatter prosody than real recordings.
  > *Evidence:* SIM-o is highest of the three systems on all intra-lingual sets, and higher than the predecessor on 14 of 16 cross-lingual pairs. However, UTMOS and CMOS are lowest on every intra-lingual set (CMOS +0.150/-0.064/-0.068 vs. +0.480/+0.141/+0.508 for F5-TTS), UTMOS is lower on all 16 cross-lingual pairs, and SMOS stays below F5-TTS. *(§V-A, §V-B, Tables II and III)*
- **supports:** When a pretrained TTS model is adapted to a new conditioning layout, keeping the positional structure of the original text condition matters more for intelligibility than the content of the placeholder.
  > *Evidence:* Dropping the prompt-transcript region raises WER to 3.022/7.116/5.066%. Filling it with N filler tokens (2.028/2.994/1.900%) or learnable prompt tokens (1.984/2.018/1.621%) recovers most of the loss. Reusing the pretrained EOS cue mainly speeds up convergence. *(§V-C, Table IV, Figure 3)*
- **refines:** Duration estimates that rely on the prompt's speaking rate are fragile to leading and trailing silence, and silence augmentation during predictor training largely removes this fragility.
  > *Evidence:* With 50% silence padding on each side, the original syllable-level SRP reaches 70.2 to 85.5% MRE, and the output WER rises to 3.2 to 4.0%. The silence-augmented SRP keeps MRE at 13.2 to 18.9% and WER at 1.7 to 2.5%. On clean prompts the cost is under one MRE point. *(§V-E, Tables VI and VII)*
- **supports:** Adapting a pretrained flow-matching TTS model to a new conditioning scheme needs far less data than pretraining.
  > *Evidence:* Fine-tuning on 1,000 real hours (plus 286 synthetic hours) comes within 0.004 SIM-o and a similar WER band of the full 95K-hour run. *(§V-D, Table V)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- **Naturalness cost.** The authors state that the main limitation is lower naturalness, which they attribute to prosodically flat synthetic prompts. UTMOS and CMOS are below both baselines on every intra-lingual set.
- **Small subjective test.** SMOS and CMOS come from 15 randomly sampled utterances per test set. The number of raters and any confidence intervals are not reported.
- **Narrow comparisons.** Only F5-TTS and Cross-Lingual F5-TTS are evaluated, and only the predecessor appears in the cross-lingual table. The F5-TTS baseline still uses the prompt transcript, so the comparison across Table II mixes transcript-conditioned and transcript-free systems. No other transcript-free cloning approach is benchmarked.
- **Target languages.** Training and targets cover only English and Chinese. Only the prompts are in unseen languages, and extending the method to more target languages is left to future work.
- **Mixed cross-lingual intelligibility.** WER is worse than the predecessor on half the cross-lingual pairs, including a 1.1-point rise for Thai-to-Chinese.
- **Synthesis cost not measured.** The method avoids forced alignment but needs about 29K hours of synthetic prompts. The compute cost of generating them is not compared with the alignment pipeline it replaces.
- **Code release unclear.** The paper says that related resources are public but gives only a demo-page link.
