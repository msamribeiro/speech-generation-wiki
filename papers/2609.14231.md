> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Ziyu Zhang et al.** (Tencent HY Speech Team) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14231) · Demo: ✗ · Code: ✗
>
> A challenge system for tag-controlled nonverbal vocalization (NVV) synthesis: a 1.5B DiTAR backbone extended with dedicated NVV tokens and boundary-aware stop prediction, trained with bilingual NVV pre-training plus long-tail-rebalanced fine-tuning, and decoded with a configuration search and multi-metric Best-of-N selection. It ranked first overall in Track 2 of the ISCSLP 2026 NVVSpeech Challenge.

## Problem

Track 2 of the ISCSLP 2026 NVVSpeech Challenge asks systems to synthesize Mandarin and English speech from transcripts annotated with one or more of 16 NVV tags (laugh, cry, sneeze, yawn, burp, snore and so on), realizing each event at the requested position while keeping the speech intelligible, natural and expressive. The authors identify three obstacles: TTS training data usually has NVVs filtered out, no official training set is provided, and the public NVV corpora are heavily imbalanced, with some categories (English cry, yawn) almost absent. They also argue that quantized codec tokens lose the fine acoustic detail that makes an NVV convincing, which motivates a continuous-latent generator *(§1)*.

## Method

**Backbone.** The system starts from an in-house 1.5B-parameter DiTAR model [[2502.03930|DiTAR]]: continuous VAE latents are grouped into patches (patch size P = 10), each patch is summarized into one embedding, a causal language model produces a patch-level condition, and a local diffusion transformer (LocDiT) generates the next patch of continuous latents. DiTAR's native inference controls (LM-guidance scale w, noise-injection scale τ and the acoustic prompt) are kept and tuned later *(§2.2, §3.1)*.

**NVV special-token conditioning.** Each of the 16 challenge NVV labels is registered as a single special token with its own learnable embedding, instead of being split into subwords. NVV tokens share the text embedding space and self-attention with ordinary text tokens, so every speech-position query attends to the full text-plus-NVV prefix; there is no separate NVV encoder and no explicit event-position input *(§2.2, Eq. 1)*.

**NVV-aware stop prediction.** Mid-utterance NVVs introduce pauses and abrupt acoustic changes that a stop predictor can mistake for the end of the utterance. The authors keep DiTAR's scalar-quantization bottleneck and two-class linear stop head but label only the final valid patch as *stop* (every intermediate patch, including NVV patches, is *continue*), add a fixed positive-class weight and label smoothing, and leave the LM hidden state un-detached so the stop loss also shapes the LM representation *(§2.2)*.

**Training recipe.** Pre-training uses 100,000 hours of bilingual Mandarin-English speech whose NVV portion includes NVSpeech-170K [[2508.04195|NVSpeech]], NonVerbalSpeech-38K [[2508.05385|Scalable Non-Verbal Pipeline]] and other NVV-annotated corpora (NonverbalTTS, CapSpeech [[2506.02863|CapSpeech]]), with all annotations normalized to the 16 challenge categories. For continued supervised fine-tuning (SFT), the authors use expressive commercial TTS systems to synthesize extra utterances for weak categories (tags placed sentence-initially, medially and finally, with several voices), then resample by tag frequency: an utterance's repetition factor is the square root of the ratio of the most frequent tag count to its rarest tag count, multiplied by a manual boost for weak tags and clipped to a maximum. English data is upsampled to a target ratio, and per-speaker contributions and repetitions are capped. Checkpoints are chosen with local objective metrics, not training loss. Optimization uses Adam at learning rate 5e-5 with global batch size 128 on 32 H20 GPUs *(§2.3, Eq. 2, §3.1)*.

**Inference.** Decoding has two stages. First, four (w, τ) combinations are ranked on the development subset with local intelligibility, NVV-detection and audio-quality metrics; 12 official submissions are used only to confirm that the ranking transfers. Second, Best-of-N sampling generates N = 5 candidates per transcript and picks the one with the highest weighted score C = 0.40 L + 0.30 D + 0.20 R + 0.10 Q, where L is a local large audio language model (LALM) judge aggregate, D is a BEATs-based NVV detector score for the requested event at the intended position, R is ASR lexical fidelity (negative CER or WER) and Q is DNSMOS, each min-max normalized across the candidates *(§2.4, Eq. 3)*.

**Evaluation.** Local metrics are Mandarin CER (Paraformer-zh [[2206.08317|Paraformer]]), English WER (Whisper-large-v3 [[2212.04356|Whisper]]), DNSMOS [[2010.15258|DNSMOS]], and NVV detector precision, recall, coverage-adjusted F1 (CA-F1) and normalized tag distance (NTD, lower means more accurate placement). The official protocol combines a Gemini-based LALM evaluation with subjective listening, both rating NVV accuracy, NVV perceptual effect, naturalness, audio quality and expression; the language-specific scores are weighted combinations and the bilingual score is their mean *(§3.1)*.

## Key Results

**Progressive local results (Table 1).** Against the official baseline, the NVV-aware DiTAR alone lowers Mandarin CER from 6.267% to 5.603% and English WER from 3.159% to 2.949%, and raises DNSMOS from 3.026 to 3.126 and detector precision from 0.769 to 0.816. Long-tail SFT brings CER/WER to 5.211%/2.711% and gives the highest precision (0.878) and recall (0.275) of any row. Decoding search gives the lowest NTD in the table (0.068). The final Best-of-N system has the best CER (5.012%), WER (2.194%), DNSMOS (3.221) and CA-F1 (0.589) among the five rows, with NTD slightly back up to 0.069 *(§3.2, Table 1)*. CA-F1 moves little across the whole ladder (0.569 baseline to 0.589 final).

**Long-tail augmentation (Table 2).** Gains in the per-tag score (ΔS, the change from NVV-aware DiTAR to long-tail SFT; the paper does not name the scoring source) are largest for English cry (+23.30), which started with only 37 original utterances and received 1,000 synthetic ones. Mandarin burp improves by +7.30, English laugh by +7.00, and yawn improves in both languages (+3.35 / +2.95). Sneeze gets worse in both languages (−1.15 / −1.98), and snore changes little (+1.50 / +0.78). The authors attribute the failures to synthetic samples that lack naturalness, speaker diversity or acoustic variation *(§3.3, Table 2)*.

**Decoding configuration (Table 3).** With the checkpoint fixed, w = 2.0, τ = 0.9 gives the lowest CER (5.098%) and WER (2.597%) and the highest CA-F1 (0.582). The lowest NTD (0.058) comes from w = 2.5, τ = 0.7, which is worse on CER, WER and DNSMOS, and the highest DNSMOS (3.188) from w = 2.0, τ = 0.7. The authors pick w = 2.0, τ = 0.9 as the most balanced setting *(§3.4, Table 3)*.

**Best-of-N selection (Table 4).** On the same five-candidate pool, the multi-metric selector gives the lowest CER (5.012%) and WER (2.194%). DNSMOS-only selection gives the highest DNSMOS (3.229) but raises CER to 6.012% and WER to 3.480%. Detector-only selection gives the highest CA-F1 (0.591) and the lowest DNSMOS (3.102). Random selection is worse than a single sample on CER (6.311% vs. 5.743%) and WER (3.277% vs. 3.148%). The multi-metric selector also has the highest NTD in Table 4 (0.069, against 0.059 for a single sample), which the paper does not discuss *(§3.4, Table 4)*.

**Official challenge results (Table 5).** The final system scores 61.775 (Mandarin), 63.797 (English) and 62.786 (bilingual), which is 2.075, 0.634 and 1.355 points above the official baseline (59.700 / 63.163 / 61.431). The advantage comes from subjective listening, where the system scores 2.838 / 3.156 / 2.997 against the baseline's 2.714 / 3.072 / 2.893. On the Gemini-based LALM component the baseline is higher in both languages (77.62 vs. 76.22 Mandarin, 70.30 vs. 69.66 English, 73.96 vs. 72.94 bilingual). The system ranks first in Mandarin, second in English and first overall in Track 2 *(§3.4, Table 5)*.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is mainly a careful system recipe. The model changes are small adaptations of an existing architecture: atomic special tokens for NVV tags (a familiar way to add control tags to an LM vocabulary) and a stop-loss relabelling with class weighting and gradient flow into the LM state. The training side combines standard square-root frequency resampling with synthetic data for rare classes, and the decoding side combines a coarse grid over existing DiTAR controls with Best-of-N reranking by a weighted mix of automatic scores. Each piece is known. The value is in assembling them for NVV control and in the ablations, which show where each stage helps and where it does not, especially the per-tag augmentation analysis and the selector comparison. The NVV-token and stop-prediction changes are not ablated on their own; their effect is only visible bundled into the "NVV-aware DiTAR" row of Table 1.

## Field Significance

Moderate — This paper documents a challenge-winning recipe for tag-controlled nonverbal vocalization synthesis on a continuous-latent autoregressive diffusion TTS backbone, with a per-category analysis showing that synthetic augmentation helps most when a category is missing from the data and can hurt when the synthetic samples lack diversity. Its selector ablation shows that reranking candidates on any single automatic metric trades one quality dimension for another. It also records a case where the official LALM judge prefers the baseline while human listeners prefer the proposed system.

## Claims

- **supports:** Dedicated vocabulary tokens for nonverbal vocalization tags, combined with a stop criterion that treats mid-utterance vocalizations as continuations, improve the intelligibility and audio quality of tag-controlled expressive TTS over a baseline system.
  > *Evidence:* The NVV-aware DiTAR (16 atomic NVV tokens, stop label only on the final patch, weighted and label-smoothed stop loss on an un-detached LM state) lowers Mandarin CER from 6.267% to 5.603% and English WER from 3.159% to 2.949%, raises DNSMOS from 3.026 to 3.126 and NVV-detector precision from 0.769 to 0.816 against the official baseline, while CA-F1 barely changes (0.569 to 0.570); the token and stop changes are not ablated separately from the backbone. *(§2.2, §3.2, Table 1)*
- **refines:** Synthetic data augmentation for rare paralinguistic event categories helps most when missing coverage is the bottleneck, and adds little or harms quality when the synthetic samples lack acoustic and speaker diversity.
  > *Evidence:* English cry (37 original utterances, 1,000 added) gains +23.30 points in per-tag score and yawn gains in both languages, while sneeze degrades in both Mandarin and English (−1.15 / −1.98) and snore barely moves after the same kind of commercial-TTS augmentation and frequency-aware resampling. *(§3.3, Table 2)*
- **complicates:** Best-of-N reranking of TTS samples on a single automatic metric improves that metric at the cost of others, so candidate selection needs a balanced multi-metric criterion.
  > *Evidence:* On the same five-candidate pool, DNSMOS-only selection gives the highest DNSMOS (3.229) but raises CER to 6.012% and WER to 3.480%; detector-only selection gives the highest CA-F1 (0.591) but the lowest DNSMOS (3.102); the weighted multi-metric selector gives the lowest CER (5.012%) and WER (2.194%) but the highest tag-position error (NTD 0.069). *(§3.4, Table 4)*
- **complicates:** Large audio language model judges and human listening tests can rank expressive TTS systems differently, so neither replaces the other for evaluating nonverbal vocalization synthesis.
  > *Evidence:* In the official Track 2 results the baseline scores higher on the Gemini-based LALM evaluation in both languages (73.96 vs. 72.94 bilingual), while the proposed system scores higher on subjective listening (2.997 vs. 2.893 bilingual) and on the combined final score (62.786 vs. 61.431). *(§3.4, Table 5)*
- **complicates:** For diffusion-based TTS decoders, guidance and noise-injection settings trade lexical fidelity against event placement accuracy, so no single decoding configuration is best on every metric.
  > *Evidence:* With the checkpoint fixed, w = 2.0, τ = 0.9 gives the lowest CER/WER (5.098% / 2.597%) and highest CA-F1 (0.582), but w = 2.5, τ = 0.7 gives the lowest NTD (0.058) with worse CER, WER and DNSMOS, and w = 2.0, τ = 0.7 gives the highest DNSMOS (3.188). *(§3.4, Table 3)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] Most of the reported numbers come from local automatic metrics on an unspecified development subset, and the official baseline system is never identified. The NVV-token and stop-prediction changes are bundled with the 1.5B in-house backbone in a single row, so their individual contribution cannot be separated from the backbone change.

- **Unreleased backbone and data.** The DiTAR backbone is in-house, the 100,000-hour pre-training corpus is not described beyond its public NVV portion, and the synthetic augmentation comes from unnamed commercial TTS systems. No code or demo is mentioned, so the recipe cannot be reproduced exactly.
- **Inconsistent baseline across tables.** The single-sample row of Table 4 (CER 5.743%, WER 3.148%) does not match the decoding-search row of Table 1 (5.098%, 2.597%), even though Best-of-N is described as running on top of the selected configuration. The final multi-metric row matches Table 1, and the paper does not say whether the single-sample row used a different seed or subset.
- **Weak NVV detection gains.** CA-F1 improves only from 0.569 to 0.589 across the whole pipeline, and detector recall stays below 0.28. By the local detector, most requested events are still not detected.
- **English lags.** The system is second in English and below the baseline on the English LALM score. The authors name English NVV data as the main open direction *(§3.5)*.
- **Inference cost.** Best-of-N with five candidates, a local LALM judge, an ASR model, a BEATs detector and DNSMOS multiplies inference compute, and the paper does not quantify that cost.
- **Selector weights.** The 0.40 / 0.30 / 0.20 / 0.10 weights in Eq. 3 and the LALM dimension weights are fixed by hand and not ablated.
