> **Wen-Chin Huang et al.** (Nagoya University) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31105) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ?
>
> Treats UTMOS as an adversarial target and shows that gradient-based input optimization can produce heavily degraded speech that UTMOS still scores highly, while the reverse attack (low UTMOS, unchanged perceived quality) only partly succeeds, with the EnCodec latent space giving the closest result.

## Problem

Learned, non-intrusive speech quality assessment (SQA) models such as UTMOS are used both as evaluation metrics for TTS, VC, codecs, enhancement and dialogue systems, and increasingly as training losses or preference-alignment rewards. Prior robustness work on SQA models has focused on naturally occurring out-of-domain inputs (noisy speech, singing, other languages, newer synthesis systems, different listening-test setups) and on how those shifts hurt system ranking. Nobody had systematically asked whether samples can be deliberately engineered to decouple the predicted score from perceived quality. That question matters most when a generator is optimized directly against the metric. Attacking an SQA model is also harder than attacking ASR or speaker verification, because quality itself depends on low-level acoustic detail: a waveform perturbation that fools the model may also genuinely change perceived quality.

## Method

The attack starts from a clean, high-quality utterance x₀ and iteratively optimizes the input, following the penalized formulation of the C&W attack. Two untargeted directions are defined:

- **Score-preserving attack:** maximize perceived-quality degradation while penalizing changes to the UTMOS score (weight λ_sp).
- **Quality-preserving attack:** maximize the drop in UTMOS score while penalizing changes in perceived quality (weight λ_qp).

Perceived quality cannot be queried from humans at every step, so the perceived-quality term is replaced by the L2 distance to x₀ in the attack space. This is the usual surrogate in the adversarial-examples literature. λ controls the trade-off: too large a weight stalls optimization, too small a weight reduces the score-preserving attack to plain degradation.

The attacks are run in three input spaces, with gradients backpropagated through any decoder into UTMOS (the official checkpoint, used through a wrapper):

1. **waveform**: raw 16 kHz samples.
2. **mel-spec + HiFi-GAN**: a mel spectrogram decoded by an open-source HiFi-GAN trained on LibriTTS. Because the vocoder is trained on clean speech, it is expected to impose a strong speech manifold.
3. **EnCodec latent**: the latent of the official 24 kHz EnCodec checkpoint. This space has more degrees of freedom than the mel spectrogram and a weaker speech prior, since EnCodec was trained on general audio.

The two decoder spaces were chosen partly because they appear in deployed TTS: HiFi-GAN as the vocoder in mel-based two-stage TTS, EnCodec as the tokenizer in LLM-based TTS. Each run uses Adam for 50 iterations (learning rate 1e-2 for waveform, 5e-2 for the other two spaces). A small ε = 1e-4 offset avoids the zero gradient at initialization x = x₀. The test material is 30 LibriSpeech test-clean utterances (one per speaker, 3 to 5 s, starting UTMOS between 4 and 5). PESQ serves as an objective proxy for perceived quality in screening and plots. The authors avoided DNN-based SQA references such as NISQA or DNSMOS because those may be adversarially fragile themselves. A final MOS listening test (14 participants, 80 samples each) checks the quality-preserving results.

## Key Results

**Random-noise baseline.** Adding Gaussian noise of increasing amplitude in any of the three spaces lowers PESQ and UTMOS together. This is the expected behaviour and shows that the attacks are needed to separate the two *(§IV-B, Figure 3)*.

**Score-preserving attacks succeed in all three spaces.** Every space yields samples with PESQ far below the original while UTMOS stays near its starting value *(§IV-C, Figure 4)*. In the decoder spaces, a weaker λ_sp lowers PESQ further but also lets UTMOS fall. In the waveform space, results are similar across λ_sp values. The single-run dynamics differ by space. The decoder spaces move smoothly: PESQ falls step by step while UTMOS holds. The waveform run first collapses to strong white noise with very low UTMOS and PESQ, then "climbs" back to a low-PESQ, high-UTMOS region *(§IV-C, Figure 5)*. These samples showed severe degradation under PESQ and manual inspection, so the listening test did not cover them. The perceptual evidence for this direction is therefore PESQ plus informal listening only.

**Quality-preserving attacks are much harder.** The paper measures success with Δ_qp = PESQ − UTMOS. Higher values mean a more successful attack, and an ideal attack reaches 3.5 (PESQ 4.5, UTMOS 1.0). Best setting per space *(§IV-D)*:

| Space | λ_qp | PESQ | UTMOS | Δ_qp |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| waveform | 1e6 | 2.20 | 1.48 | 0.72 |
| mel-spec + HiFi-GAN | 1e4 | 2.26 | 1.33 | 0.93 |
| EnCodec latent | 1e5 | 3.12 | 1.32 | 1.80 |

EnCodec latent has the highest Δ_qp of the three spaces, but even its best samples sounded perceptibly different from the originals on manual inspection.

**Listening test (Table I).** On 10 random samples per condition, perceived quality follows PESQ, with linear correlations of 0.90 at sample level and 0.96 at system level. The authors take this as support for PESQ as a proxy. The originals score 4.46 ± 0.12 MOS (UTMOS 4.30). Among the attacked conditions, EnCodec latent with λ_qp = 1e5 keeps the most perceived quality while pushing UTMOS low: MOS 3.11 at UTMOS 1.36. Mel-spec + HiFi-GAN with λ_qp = 1e4 reaches UTMOS 1.35 but only MOS 1.96, below the random-noise baseline (MOS 2.01). The milder waveform (λ_qp = 1e7) and EnCodec (λ_qp = 1e6) settings keep MOS at 3.61 and 3.50 but only lower UTMOS to 3.12 and 2.01. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests show that every attacked condition differs significantly from the originals (p < 1e-14). So no attack fully preserved quality *(§IV-E, Table I)*.

## Novelty Assessment

Gradient-based adversarial examples and the penalized C&W formulation are standard. The novelty is in applying them to a learned speech quality predictor and framing two opposite attack directions around the ideal quality/score diagonal. The paper also uses generative decoders (HiFi-GAN, EnCodec) as structured attack spaces, which links the attack to the representations real TTS generators optimize in. It is a probing study, not a new metric or defense. No robustness fix is proposed, only UTMOS is attacked, and the sample sizes are small (30 utterances, 10 per listening-test condition). The analysis of why decoder spaces behave differently is explicitly labelled a hypothesis.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper gives direct evidence that a learned MOS predictor can keep a high score under severe perceptual degradation. This makes UTMOS questionable as an optimization target or reward for speech generators. It also shows that the opposite failure (unfairly low scores for good speech) is much harder to induce. It adds an adversarial-robustness protocol for SQA metrics to the existing out-of-domain-generalization critiques. The authors stress that the result does not invalidate UTMOS as a passive evaluation metric.

## Claims

- **complicates:** Learned non-intrusive MOS predictors can assign high quality scores to speech whose perceived quality has been severely degraded, so a high predicted score does not guarantee perceptual quality under optimization pressure.
  > *Evidence:* Score-preserving attacks in the waveform, mel-spec + HiFi-GAN and EnCodec latent spaces all produce samples with PESQ far below the original while UTMOS stays near its starting value. Waveform-space runs pass through strong white noise before returning to a high-UTMOS region. *(§IV-C, Figures 4 and 5)*
- **complicates:** Using a learned speech quality predictor as a training loss or preference reward risks rewarding generators for exploiting the predictor, not for improving perceived quality.
  > *Evidence:* The authors conclude that UTMOS may be particularly unreliable as an optimization target or reward because high predicted scores survive severe perceptual degradation, while treating its passive use as an evaluation metric as less affected. *(§V; evidence from §IV-C)*
- **refines:** Adversarial vulnerability of learned speech quality predictors is asymmetric: inflating the score of degraded speech is much easier than deflating the score of clean speech without audible change.
  > *Evidence:* The best quality-preserving result (EnCodec latent, PESQ 3.12, UTMOS 1.32, Δ_qp = 1.80) falls well short of the ideal 3.5. In the listening test, every attacked condition differs significantly from the originals (Wilcoxon p < 1e-14). *(§IV-D, §IV-E, Table I)*
- **refines:** The representation in which a speech signal is perturbed shapes how a learned quality predictor can be fooled; decoders trained on speech keep perturbations on a speech-like manifold, and codec latents give more room than mel spectrograms.
  > *Evidence:* Decoder spaces give smooth score-preserving trajectories, while the waveform space first collapses to white noise. For quality-preserving attacks, EnCodec latent reaches Δ_qp 1.80 against 0.93 (mel-spec + HiFi-GAN) and 0.72 (waveform). In the MOS test it keeps 3.11 MOS at UTMOS 1.36, while mel-spec + HiFi-GAN keeps 1.96 at UTMOS 1.35. The authors label the manifold explanation a hypothesis. *(§IV-C, §IV-D, Figures 5 and 7, Table I)*
- **supports:** PESQ against a clean reference can serve as a practical proxy for perceived quality when screening many perturbed variants of clean speech.
  > *Evidence:* Across 80 listening-test samples rated by 14 listeners, MOS correlates with PESQ at 0.90 (sample level) and 0.96 (system level). *(§IV-E, Table I)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] The headline vulnerability, the score-preserving attack, has no listening test behind it. Degradation is established through PESQ and manual inspection only. The MOS test covers only the quality-preserving direction, on 10 samples per condition drawn from 30 LibriSpeech utterances.

- Only UTMOS (trained on BVCC) is attacked. Whether models trained on noisy or enhanced speech, or on broader quality data, are similarly vulnerable is left to future work.
- The perceived-quality term in the objective is an L2 distance in the attack space, so the attacks never directly optimize against a perceptual model. Their success is judged afterwards with PESQ, an intrusive metric designed for telephony and codecs.
- All attacks are white-box (gradient access to UTMOS) and start from clean, read-speech samples. Black-box or transfer attacks, attacks starting from low-quality inputs, and attacks on LLM-based descriptive quality evaluators are not studied.
- The paper does not show whether a TTS generator trained with a UTMOS loss or reward actually reaches the score-preserving region. The concern about reward use is inferred from the attack results.
- No code release is stated, though audio samples are online.
