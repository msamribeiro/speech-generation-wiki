>
> A roughly 0.2B-parameter streaming TTS model that autoregressively generates patches of continuous VAE latents with a flow-matching head, plus a "guidance-step distillation" that folds classifier-free guidance and multi-step sampling into one interval-conditioned diffusion head.

## Problem

Continuous-autoregressive TTS keeps the fidelity of continuous speech latents while staying causal enough to stream, but compact streaming systems face two costs. First, a low-rate latent sequence has to balance reconstruction quality against how predictable it is for an autoregressive backbone. Second, each autoregressive step runs an iterative diffusion or flow sampler, and LM-level classifier-free guidance (CFG) doubles that work with an unconditional branch. Both costs land directly on streaming latency. Prior guided-model distillation and few-step methods (progressive distillation, consistency models, mean-flow and interval-conditioned models) treat guidance removal and step reduction separately and were not targeted at the per-patch head of a continuous-autoregressive TTS model.

## Method

CuteTTS has five parts: a causal audio VAE, a patch encoder, a causal language-model backbone, a diffusion head, and a speaker encoder. It follows the patch-based factorization of [[2502.03930|DiTAR]]: the causal backbone models dependencies across patches and a bidirectional head models correlations within a patch.

**Audio representation.** A separately trained causal VAE with a DAC-style fully convolutional encoder and decoder maps 24 kHz audio to 64-dimensional continuous latents at 12.5 Hz (127.9M parameters, Table 8). To avoid posterior variance collapse it is a sigma-VAE: the encoder predicts only a mean and the scale is fixed (standard deviation 0.15, Appendix A.1). Following [[2509.22167|Semantic-VAE]], a cosine loss aligns the latents with projected features from a frozen WavLM teacher. The VAE loss combines multi-resolution mel reconstruction, adversarial and feature-matching losses from multi-period and multi-resolution discriminators, a lightly weighted KL term, and the semantic-alignment term. Training includes 5% of examples band-limited to 16 kHz and 5% to 8 kHz, with the original 24 kHz signal as the target.

**Speaker conditioning.** A compact ECAPA-style speaker encoder is distilled from a frozen WavLM Large speaker-verification model with cosine, pairwise-similarity and soft-distribution matching losses. Its embedding enters the causal backbone through a learned projection and conditions the diffusion head through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN).

**Patch autoregression.** Two latent frames form a patch, giving an LM token rate of 6.25 tokens per second. A bidirectional patch encoder (31.0M) summarizes each patch with a learned summary token. The causal Transformer backbone (126.9M, 7 layers) consumes text, the speaker embedding, encoded reference patches and the previously generated patch embeddings. The diffusion head (70.5M, 4 layers) is trained with conditional flow matching on a linear path. It predicts the next patch from the backbone hidden state, the speaker embedding and the preceding latent patch. A stop head ends generation, and the causal VAE decoder turns each completed patch into a waveform chunk. Conditional inputs are dropped with probability 0.1 so the model can use CFG at inference.

The final-layer LM hidden state at the current autoregressive position, preceding latent patch, and speaker embedding condition the diffusion head to generate the next continuous patch; for the distilled model, CFG weight w is supplied as an additional condition. A causal VAE decoder converts completed patches to audio incrementally.](assets/2608.08638/figure-1.png)

**Guidance-step distillation.** The base model uses LM-level CFG (w = 2). Its unconditional branch drops the target text and reference audio from the LM input and zeros the head's speaker condition, but keeps the generated patch history. Each patch costs 10 head function evaluations (NFEs) on each of the two branches, on an Euler grid with a Sway coefficient of -0.8. For distillation, the teacher is this CFG-guided velocity, integrated numerically on an eight-step base grid. The student is only the diffusion head, initialized from the base model and given new guidance-strength and step-size embeddings (3.1M extra parameters, Table 9). It receives the start time, the interval length and the guidance scale w. It regresses the teacher's average velocity over the interval, so one forward pass jumps from x_t to x_{t+Δt}. Training updates only the head for 100K steps, with a curriculum that moves from four-step to mixed four-, two- and one-step targets. The backbone is not retrained. The single distilled checkpoint runs at 4, 2 or 1 NFEs on a single branch with no unconditional pass; the main results use 4 NFEs. Guidance removal and step reduction are therefore learned in one objective rather than as two sequential distillation stages.

The TTS model, VAE and speaker encoder are all trained on an internal multilingual corpus of about 550,000 hours. Ablations use a subset of about 50,000 hours.

## Key Results

**Zero-shot cloning (Table 1).** CuteTTS has the highest speaker similarity of all 15 rows on LibriSpeech test-clean (78.9; the best baseline is [[2606.06928|VoxCPM2]] at 74.0, and CuteTTS-distill is second at 76.8). It also has the highest on Seed-TTS-eval EN (76.5 against 75.3 for VoxCPM2). On Seed-TTS-eval ZH it ranks third (77.8, behind VoxCPM2 at 79.5 and [[2505.17589|CosyVoice 3]] at 78.0). It does not have the lowest WER on any set:

- On LibriSpeech, its WER of 2.16 sits behind Pocket TTS (1.59), MOSS-TTS (1.98), CosyVoice 3 (1.99), VibeVoice-Realtime (2.00) and [[2506.13053|ZipVoice]] (2.05).
- On Seed-TTS EN, its WER of 2.04 ranks 11th of 15; Pocket TTS (1.63) and [[2601.15621|Qwen3-TTS]] (1.66) are lowest.
- On Seed-TTS ZH, its WER of 1.41 ranks 10th of the 13 systems that report it (ZipVoice is 1.40); Qwen3-TTS is lowest at 0.91.

Apart from the 0.1B systems (MOSS-TTS-Nano, ZipVoice, Pocket TTS), CuteTTS at 0.2B is smaller than every baseline, most of which are 0.5B to 8B parameters. Baseline numbers come either from official implementations or from the original papers, so the comparison mixes reproduced and reported values.

**Distillation cost and quality (Tables 1 and 2).** The distilled student lowers mean first-audio latency from 49.0 to 37.6 ms (23.3%), P50 from 48.7 to 37.1 ms, P95 from 52.3 to 40.8 ms, and RTF from 0.184 to 0.109 (40.8%). Both percentages check against the table cells. The quality cost shows up mostly in speaker similarity. SIM drops 2.1 to 2.3 points on all three sets (78.9 to 76.8, 76.5 to 74.2, 77.8 to 75.6). WER rises on LibriSpeech (2.16 to 2.41) and Seed-TTS ZH (1.41 to 1.47) and is unchanged on Seed-TTS EN (2.04 to 2.03). On the efficiency benchmark, CuteTTS-distill has the lowest mean, P50 and P95 first-audio latency of all systems in Table 2, ahead of the base CuteTTS (49.0 ms) and Pocket TTS (51.0 ms). Its RTF of 0.109 is second lowest, behind ZipVoice (0.030). All timings are batch-size-one sequential requests on a single RTX 4090 with warm caches (Appendix C).

**Robustness at low NFE (§5.4, Figure 5).** At 4 NFEs, the base model has higher SIM than the student at every CFG weight, and lower WER at w = 2 to 3. At 2 and 1 NFE, the base model degrades sharply: around 90% WER at 1 NFE and high CFG. The student keeps low WER and stable SIM in both cases. The student has higher UTMOS than the base model at every tested NFE budget and CFG weight.

**Subjective preference (Figure 2, Appendix B).** Seven annotators ran blinded pairwise comparisons on 50 LibriSpeech utterances against MOSS-TTS-Nano, [[2025.acl-long.313|F5-TTS]], ZipVoice and Pocket TTS (400 items in total). On sound quality, both variants win more often than they lose against all four baselines (for example, base CuteTTS against ZipVoice: 74% win, 8% loss). On naturalness, base CuteTTS wins more than it loses against MOSS-TTS-Nano, F5-TTS and ZipVoice, but against Pocket TTS it is 16% win, 64% tie, 20% loss. The student loses more often than it wins on naturalness against F5-TTS (28% win, 30% loss) and Pocket TTS (18% win, 32% loss). The claim that it "closely matches" the base model on the subjective test is therefore weaker for naturalness than for sound quality.

**Granularity ablations (§5.1, Tables 3 to 5, Figure 3).** VAE reconstruction improves steadily from 6.25 to 50 Hz (SIM 0.732 to 0.976, PESQ-WB 2.336 to 4.166), with the largest step between 6.25 and 12.5 Hz. With one frame per patch, TTS WER rises with the frame rate: 2.53, 4.67, 10.59 and 70.33% at 6.25, 12.5, 25 and 50 Hz. At a fixed LM rate of 6.25 tokens per second, the 12.5 Hz, two-frame-patch setting raises SIM from 62.5 to 71.3 and UTMOS from 3.733 to 3.878 over 6.25 Hz single-frame patches, with WER moving only from 2.53 to 2.70. Larger patches raise SIM further (72.5, 74.1) but raise WER (3.92, 7.04) and lower UTMOS. When the depth of one module is doubled:

- At 50 Hz with eight-frame patches, a deeper backbone mainly cuts WER (7.04 to 4.91), while a deeper diffusion head mainly lifts SIM (74.1 to 76.8) and UTMOS (3.649 to 3.924).
- At 50 Hz with single-frame patches, only the deeper backbone reduces WER (70.33 to 60.03). The deeper head makes WER worse (90.84).

**Semantic alignment and speaker conditioning (§5.2, §5.3).** Semantic alignment leaves VAE reconstruction nearly unchanged: SIM is equal at 0.881, and PESQ-WB, STOI and UTMOS are fractionally lower (2.844 against 2.885, Table 6). The aligned latents give lower TTS WER at every CFG weight from 1 to 5, while SIM is comparable and slightly lower at w ≥ 2 (Figure 4). Removing the explicit speaker embedding cuts SIM from 71.3 to 53.4 (WavLM evaluator) and from 82.5 to 74.5 (WeSpeaker evaluator) (Table 7). Feeding the embedding only to the diffusion head recovers almost all of it (71.2, 82.5) and gives the lowest WER of the four variants (2.52 against 2.70 for the default). The WeSpeaker check addresses the risk that a WavLM-distilled speaker encoder inflates a WavLM-based SIM score.

## Novelty Assessment

Most of the system is an assembly of published components:

- the DiTAR-style split into a patch-level causal backbone and a bidirectional flow head;
- a sigma-VAE (used by [[2508.19205|VibeVoice]]);
- a DAC-style causal VAE like [[2509.24650|VoxCPM]]'s;
- Semantic-VAE's WavLM alignment;
- an ECAPA speaker encoder distilled from WavLM.

The new piece is guidance-step distillation: a single student head that regresses interval-averaged velocities of the CFG-guided teacher trajectory, conditioned on both the interval length and the guidance weight. It draws on CFG distillation, shortcut models, mean flows and [[2510.07979|IntMeanFlow]], but here guidance removal and step reduction are folded into one objective inside an autoregressive TTS head. This is a new training objective on an otherwise unchanged architecture. The measured gain is real but modest at the operating point used (23.3% latency, 40.8% RTF at 4 NFEs, with a 2-point SIM cost). The clearer benefit is that the head stays usable at 1 to 2 NFEs, where the base model collapses.

The controlled granularity study is the paper's most reusable empirical contribution. It separates the effect of VAE frame rate, patch size and module capacity at a fixed LM token rate, which earlier continuous-autoregressive papers usually fixed by design. There is no comparison against a CFG-only or step-only distillation baseline, so the advantage of doing both jointly is asserted rather than isolated.

## Field Significance

Moderate — This paper shows that a 0.2B continuous-autoregressive model with a semantically aligned 12.5 Hz VAE and explicit speaker conditioning can reach speaker similarity above much larger systems on English zero-shot benchmarks. It also provides a single-objective way to remove both the unconditional CFG pass and most solver steps from a per-patch flow head. Its controlled frame-rate, patch-size and capacity ablations give evidence about how temporal granularity splits the modeling burden between the autoregressive backbone and the local generator.

## Claims

- **supports:** Distilling a classifier-free-guided flow-matching head into a single-branch, interval-conditioned student reduces streaming TTS latency with a small loss in speaker similarity.
  > *Evidence:* Guidance-step distillation of only the diffusion head cuts mean first-audio latency from 49.0 to 37.6 ms and RTF from 0.184 to 0.109. Speaker similarity drops by 2.1 to 2.3 points on LibriSpeech test-clean and Seed-TTS EN/ZH, and WER changes by at most 0.25 points. *(§4.3, Table 1, Table 2)*
- **supports:** Joint guidance-and-step distillation keeps continuous-autoregressive TTS usable at one or two sampling steps, where an undistilled guided sampler collapses.
  > *Evidence:* At 1 NFE the base model's WER rises to roughly 90% at high CFG weights while the distilled head keeps low WER and stable SIM; the distilled head also has higher UTMOS at every tested NFE budget and CFG weight. *(§5.4, Figure 5)*
- **refines:** In patch-based continuous-autoregressive TTS, the autoregressive token rate alone does not determine synthesis difficulty; frame rate and patch size shift the modeling burden between the causal backbone and the local generator.
  > *Evidence:* At a fixed 6.25 tokens per second, going from 6.25 Hz single-frame to 12.5 Hz two-frame patches raises SIM from 62.5 to 71.3 at near-constant WER, while 50 Hz eight-frame patches raise SIM to 74.1 but WER to 7.04. Doubling backbone depth mainly lowers WER, and doubling head depth mainly raises SIM and UTMOS under large patches. *(§5.1.2, §5.1.3, Table 4, Table 5)*
- **supports:** Aligning continuous speech latents with self-supervised speech features improves the intelligibility of autoregressive TTS built on them without measurably degrading reconstruction.
  > *Evidence:* A 12.5 Hz VAE with WavLM cosine alignment matches the unaligned VAE on reconstruction (SIM 0.881 both, PESQ-WB 2.844 against 2.885), and the TTS model trained on it has lower WER at every CFG weight from 1 to 5 with comparable SIM. *(§5.2, Table 6, Figure 4)*
- **supports:** An explicit speaker embedding is a major contributor to speaker similarity in prompt-based continuous-autoregressive TTS, even when the reference audio is already in the model's context.
  > *Evidence:* Removing the speaker embedding while keeping the reference latents in the LM prefix lowers SIM from 71.3 to 53.4 (WavLM) and 82.5 to 74.5 (WeSpeaker); conditioning only the diffusion head recovers 71.2 and 82.5. *(§5.3, Table 7)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> All training data (about 550,000 hours) is internal and undescribed beyond being multilingual, so the main comparison cannot be reproduced. Baseline numbers mix official-implementation runs and values copied from original papers.

- The subjective test is small: 50 utterances, seven annotators, and A/B preference against only four similarly sized baselines. The distilled model loses more than it wins on naturalness against F5-TTS and Pocket TTS, which tempers the "comparable subjective quality" claim.
- The distillation is not compared against CFG-only or step-only distillation, so the benefit of learning them jointly is not isolated. The headline efficiency gains are measured at 4 NFEs. The 1 to 2 NFE regime, where distillation matters most, is reported only on LibriSpeech ablation plots.
- The speaker encoder is distilled from WavLM and the main SIM evaluator is WavLM-based. The WeSpeaker cross-check covers only the conditioning ablation, not Table 1.
- Evaluation covers only English and Mandarin, read-speech benchmarks, and voice cloning. Expressiveness, long-form generation, and other languages in the multilingual training set are not tested.
- Ablations use a 50,000-hour subset, so the granularity conclusions may not hold at the full data scale. The module-depth study is not parameter-matched, as the paper notes.
