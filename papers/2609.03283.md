>
> A controlled comparison of upstream feature extractors for naturalness MOS prediction (SSL models, acoustic-only codecs, and semantically distilled "unified" codecs) under one fixed downstream predictor, in frozen and fine-tuned settings, on BVCC plus two zero-shot out-of-domain sets.

## Problem

Most automatic MOS predictors for synthesized speech use a self-supervised (SSL) speech model such as wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT or WavLM as the feature backbone. The authors argue that masked-prediction pre-training abstracts away fine-grained acoustic detail (noise, waveform distortions), which could cap how well such features track perceived quality. Neural audio codecs preserve that detail by construction but lack semantic structure, and no prior study had compared the three representation families (SSL, acoustic-only codecs, codecs distilled from or fused with SSL features) under a single predictor. The authors state the paper was accepted by ISCSLP 2026.

## Method

The study fixes a two-stage predictor: an upstream feature extractor and a shared downstream network (§2). For SSL models the input is the hidden states of a chosen layer (layer 12 for wav2vec 2.0 base and HuBERT base, layer 24 for HuBERT large, a layer average for WavLM base); for codecs it is the continuous pre-quantization encoder output, chosen to avoid quantization loss (§2.1, Table 1). Nine extractors are compared across three paradigms: SSL (w2v2 base, hubert base, hubert large, wavlm base), acoustic-only codecs (EnCodec at 75 Hz, DAC at 50 Hz), and unified codecs that inject SSL semantics via distillation or feature fusion (X-Codec with a HuBERT or WavLM semantic branch, SpeechTokenizer). Extractor sizes range from 7M (EnCodec) to 316M (HuBERT large) parameters.

The downstream network applies a feed-forward layer (hidden size 4096), four 8-head self-attention layers, and a hybrid pooling that concatenates mean-pooled attention outputs with max-pooled feed-forward outputs, followed by a linear head with a sigmoid scaled to [1, 5] (§2.2, Eq. 1). Training uses L2 loss and SGD (learning rate 1e-4, momentum 0.9, batch size 2) for 30 epochs, selecting the checkpoint with the best validation SRCC and averaging three seeds (§3.2). Two protocols are run: a frozen setting where only the downstream network is trained, used as a probe of the representation's intrinsic quality information, and a fine-tuning setting where the upstream encoder is unfrozen end to end (§2.3).

All predictors train on BVCC (7,106 English utterances from past Blizzard and Voice Conversion Challenge systems and ESPnet samples, 8 ratings each). Out-of-domain evaluation is zero-shot on SOMOS-clean (3,000 English neural TTS utterances, cross-corpus, matched language) and the Blizzard Challenge 2019 test set (540 Mandarin utterances, cross-lingual) (§3.1). Metrics are MSE, LCC, SRCC and Kendall's tau, all at the utterance level; system-level correlations are not reported.

## Key Results

All values below are utterance-level (Tables 2 and 3); the paper reports no system-level results.

**BVCC, frozen (Table 2).** WavLM base gives the best correlations (LCC 0.862, SRCC 0.863, KTAU 0.683) and SpeechTokenizer the lowest MSE (0.233). The unified codecs follow closely (Xcodec-wavlm SRCC 0.859, SpeechTokenizer 0.856, Xcodec-hubert 0.855) and sit above w2v2 base (0.836), hubert base (0.833) and hubert large (0.810). Acoustic-only codecs are far behind (DAC 0.715, EnCodec 0.666 SRCC). So in the frozen probe, the strongest single representation is an SSL model (WavLM), with unified codecs second; the authors group WavLM with the unified codecs as "semantic-acoustic" representations because of its denoising pre-training objective.

**BVCC, fine-tuned (Table 2).** Xcodec-wavlm is best on all four metrics (MSE 0.201, LCC 0.882, SRCC 0.882, KTAU 0.710), but margins are small: the next SRCC values are Xcodec-hubert 0.879, hubert base 0.878 and w2v2 base 0.877. Fine-tuning lifts hubert large most among SSL models (SRCC 0.810 to 0.874). Xcodec-hubert's MSE rises from 0.263 frozen to 0.364 fine-tuned while its correlations improve. A gradient-norm analysis over 100k iterations shows the semantic module of the unified codec receiving larger gradients than its acoustic module throughout fine-tuning (§3.3, Figure 2), which the authors read as the task being semantic-dominant.

**SOMOS, zero-shot (Table 3).** Only six extractors are evaluated out of domain (hubert large and the acoustic-only codecs are absent). Frozen: Xcodec-hubert has the best correlations (LCC 0.450, SRCC 0.455, KTAU 0.311) and SpeechTokenizer the lowest MSE (0.425); Xcodec-wavlm, however, is the weakest of the six on SRCC (0.309, below w2v2 base at 0.317 and wavlm base at 0.404). Fine-tuned: SpeechTokenizer is best on all four metrics (MSE 0.323, LCC 0.444, SRCC 0.440, KTAU 0.302). The authors' statement that unified codecs "consistently outperform" SSL baselines on SOMOS therefore holds for the best unified codec in each setting rather than for every unified codec row. Absolute correlations on SOMOS are low for every extractor (SRCC at most 0.455).

**BC2019, zero-shot cross-lingual (Table 3).** Frozen: wavlm base is best on all four metrics (MSE 1.859, LCC 0.647, SRCC 0.674, KTAU 0.478). Fine-tuned: wavlm base keeps the lowest MSE (1.966) and the best KTAU (0.493, against 0.492 for Xcodec-wavlm), while Xcodec-wavlm has the best LCC (0.665) and SRCC (0.682). SpeechTokenizer degrades with fine-tuning (SRCC 0.610 to 0.577, MSE 3.497 to 3.864). MSE values above 1.8 for every extractor indicate a large score-scale offset on this set even where rank correlation is moderate.

No comparison is made against published end-to-end MOS predictors such as UTMOS; all comparisons are among extractors under the one shared downstream network.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is a controlled representation comparison, not a new predictor: the downstream network and training recipe are standard, and every extractor is an existing pre-trained model. What is new is placing acoustic-only and SSL-distilled codecs alongside SSL models under identical frozen and fine-tuned protocols for naturalness MOS prediction, and testing cross-corpus and cross-lingual transfer. The evidence for the headline thesis is mixed: acoustic-only codecs clearly underperform, but the best frozen BVCC representation is an SSL model, fine-tuned BVCC margins are a few thousandths of SRCC with no variance reported across the three seeds, and SSL features hold up better cross-lingually. The cross-lingual explanation (unified codecs constrained by discrete codebooks biased toward the pre-training language) is offered without a test, and sits awkwardly with the design choice of using pre-quantization continuous latents.

## Field Significance

Low — The study provides a useful controlled data point that codec latents trained only for reconstruction carry much less naturalness information than SSL features, and that SSL-distilled codecs reach roughly SSL-level accuracy on BVCC and can transfer better to English neural TTS on SOMOS. Gains over the strongest SSL baseline are small and inconsistent across datasets and settings, results are utterance-level only on standard benchmarks, and there is no comparison with established predictors, so it is mainly confirmatory guidance on feature choice for MOS predictors.

## Claims

- **supports:** Representations trained only for waveform reconstruction carry substantially less naturalness information than self-supervised speech features when used as inputs to an automatic MOS predictor.
  > *Evidence:* Under an identical downstream network on BVCC, frozen EnCodec and DAC latents reach SRCC 0.666 and 0.715 against 0.810 to 0.863 for SSL models, and the gap persists after fine-tuning (0.701 and 0.740 against 0.874 to 0.878). *(§3.3, Table 2)*
- **complicates:** Adding acoustic reconstruction objectives to semantic speech representations yields at most marginal in-domain gains for naturalness MOS prediction over a strong self-supervised baseline.
  > *Evidence:* Frozen WavLM base gives the best BVCC correlations (SRCC 0.863 vs 0.859 for Xcodec-wavlm); after fine-tuning Xcodec-wavlm leads with SRCC 0.882 against 0.874 to 0.878 for SSL models, and gradient norms show the semantic module dominating fine-tuning. *(§3.3, Table 2, Figure 2)*
- **refines:** The benefit of codec-based representations for MOS prediction under domain shift depends on whether the shift is within or across languages.
  > *Evidence:* Zero-shot on English SOMOS, the best unified codec beats every SSL model (fine-tuned SpeechTokenizer SRCC 0.440 vs at most 0.390), whereas on Mandarin BC2019 frozen WavLM is best on all metrics and fine-tuned SSL and unified codecs split the metrics. *(§3.3, Table 3)*
- **complicates:** Automatic MOS predictors trained on one listening-test corpus transfer poorly to other corpora regardless of the feature representation used.
  > *Evidence:* BVCC-trained predictors reach SRCC of at most 0.455 on SOMOS-clean and MSE above 1.8 for every extractor on BC2019, compared with in-domain SRCC of 0.810 to 0.882 for SSL and unified-codec features. *(§3.3, Tables 2 and 3)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- All results are utterance-level; system-level correlation, the usual target when ranking TTS systems, is not reported.
- Margins between the top representations on BVCC are a few thousandths of SRCC, and only seed-averaged means are given, so significance cannot be judged.
- Only one downstream architecture and one training recipe are used; whether the ranking of representations holds for other predictor heads (or with the listener-dependent modelling used by strong VoiceMOS systems) is untested.
- Out-of-domain tests omit hubert large and the acoustic-only codecs, and no recalibration is applied, so the large BC2019 MSE values mix scale offset with ranking ability.
- Representation families are confounded with pre-training data size and model scale (960h to 84,000h, 7M to 316M parameters, Table 1), which the analysis does not control for.
- The cross-lingual explanation based on discrete codebook bias is not tested, although the features used are pre-quantization latents.
