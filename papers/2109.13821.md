>
> Diff-VC performs one-shot any-to-any voice conversion with a score-based diffusion decoder that starts from a speaker-independent "average voice" mel-spectrogram, and pairs it with a maximum likelihood reverse-SDE solver that keeps conversion quality usable at 6 sampling steps without retraining.

*A [peer-reviewed version](https://openreview.net/forum?id=8c50f-DoWAu) appeared at ICLR 2022. This page, including its metrics and claims, summarizes the arXiv preprint.*

## Problem

One-shot many-to-many voice conversion (VC) must copy a voice from a single reference utterance when neither source nor target speaker was seen in training. Earlier one-shot systems are autoencoders that try to strip speaker identity from the content code with information bottlenecks, vector quantization, instance normalization, VAEs or phonetic posteriorgrams (PPGs), and then add speaker identity back in the decoder. Diffusion models had produced strong results for vocoding and mel generation, but the two existing diffusion VC models (VoiceGrad and DiffSVC) did not address the one-shot setting and needed at least 100 reverse steps. Fast-sampling methods for diffusion models existed, but most applied only to particular model types or required retraining or a schedule search.

## Method

Diff-VC is an encoder-decoder model built on the continuous-time, data-dependent-prior diffusion of [[2105.06337|Grad-TTS]] from the same group. The encoder maps a source mel-spectrogram to an "average voice" mel-spectrogram. To build its target, the authors align LibriTTS with the Montreal Forced Aligner, average mel features per phoneme over the whole corpus, and replace every phoneme's frames with that average. The encoder uses the Grad-TTS Transformer encoder (with mel input instead of phoneme embeddings) and is trained separately from the decoder with an MSE loss against this target.

The encoder output X̄ sets the mean of the diffusion prior N(X̄, I). The forward SDE pulls the mel-spectrogram toward X̄ while adding noise (the authors call this a Mean Reverting Variance Preserving, MR-VP, diffusion). The decoder is a U-Net score network, the same as in Grad-TTS but with four times more channels. It is trained with the standard weighted L2 score-matching loss, with linear noise schedule β₀ = 0.05 and β₁ = 20. A jointly trained speaker conditioning network g_t(Y) feeds a 128-dimensional vector to the U-Net. Three input variants are compared: a pre-trained speaker-verification d-vector alone (d-only), the d-vector plus the noisy target mel Y_t at the current diffusion time (wodyn), and the d-vector plus the whole forward trajectory of the target (whole). At inference the reference utterance supplies Y, so conversion needs no fine-tuning on the target voice. A pre-trained universal [[2010.05646|HiFi-GAN]] vocoder produces 22.05 kHz audio. Model size is not reported.

The second contribution is a sampler. Section 3 considers every fixed-step reverse-SDE solver whose update is a linear combination of the current sample, the score and isotropic Gaussian noise; this class includes Euler-Maruyama (EM). Theorem 1 derives the coefficients that maximize the log-likelihood of discrete forward-diffusion sample paths for a score network trained to optimality. When the data distribution is a constant or an isotropic Gaussian, the solver is also exact. The optimal coefficients differ from EM only when the step count is small or near the end of sampling, and they cost nothing extra apart from a data-dependent variance term. The paper derives the solver for VP, MR-VP, sub-VP and VE diffusions (Appendices D to F). For VC, the authors drop the data-dependent variance term, assuming strong conditioning makes it small, and call the resulting sampler ML-N for N steps.

## Key Results

All subjective tests use Amazon Mechanical Turk Master raters on 5-point scales for naturalness and speaker similarity, with audio downsampled to 16 kHz and loudness-normalized. Conversion is unseen-to-unseen over 25 speakers (9 VCTK, 10 LibriTTS, 6 internal).

**Speaker conditioning (Table 1).** In a most/least-similar forced choice over 92 pairs, wodyn is picked most often as most similar for both training sets (38.0% for Diff-LibriTTS, 46.7% for Diff-VCTK). The authors note that the input type has little effect on either similarity or naturalness overall, and keep wodyn.

**VCTK-trained comparison (Table 2).** Diff-VCTK outperforms AGAIN-VC, FragmentVC and VQMIVC on all four columns, even with 6 steps. On the VCTK test set, ML-30 reaches MOS 3.73 and similarity 3.57; ML-6 reaches 3.73 and 3.47. The strongest baseline, VQMIVC, scores 2.89 and 2.60 there and drops to 2.48 and 1.95 on the whole test set. Diff-VCTK also degrades off-domain: on the whole test set, ML-30 scores MOS 3.44 and similarity 2.71.

**Large-scale comparison (Table 3).** Diff-LibriTTS is compared with BNE-PPG-VC, the only baseline with a public model trained on large-scale data (LibriTTS + VCTK). On the VCTK test set the two are tied on naturalness (3.96 vs. 3.95) and BNE-PPG-VC is slightly ahead on similarity (3.27 vs. 3.23), though BNE-PPG-VC saw VCTK in training and Diff-LibriTTS did not. On the whole test set, Diff-LibriTTS-ML-30 is ahead on both naturalness (4.02 vs. 3.83) and similarity (3.39 vs. 3.03). With 6 steps, ML-6 stays ahead on similarity (3.27 vs. 3.03) but falls slightly below BNE-PPG-VC on naturalness (3.80 vs. 3.83). The authors observe that BNE-PPG-VC, whose PPG extractor is trained on LibriSpeech, mispronounces less but has more artifacts.

**Sampler comparison (Table 3, §4.2).** At 6 steps the choice of solver dominates quality. On the whole test set, naturalness MOS is 3.80 for ML-6, 2.99 for probability-flow sampling (PF-6) and 1.57 for Euler-Maruyama (EM-6). Moving from 30 to 6 ML steps costs 0.22 MOS on naturalness (4.02 to 3.80) and 0.12 on similarity (3.39 to 3.27). The 6-step schemes run at a GPU real-time factor of about 0.1, and ML-30 runs at about 0.5.

**Image generation (Table 4).** To test generality, the solver is applied to the CIFAR-10 VP, sub-VP and VE score models of Song et al. Here ML is used only for t ≤ τ, with EM above τ, because the trained networks are not optimal at high noise. ML gives the lowest FID in five of the six columns. Examples: 24.45 vs. 229.6 for EM on VP at 10 steps, and 48.51 vs. 462.1 for EM on VE at 10 steps. Probability flow is best on sub-VP at 100 steps (4.42 vs. 6.43 for the best ML setting). τ matters: with τ = 0.1, ML on VP at 10 steps (260.3) is worse than EM (229.6). Toy experiments in Appendix G show ML converging in fewer steps than EM and reconstructing a two-point distribution's 50/50 mix, where EM yields 54% of one point.

No objective intelligibility (WER/CER) or speaker-embedding similarity metrics are reported.

## Novelty Assessment

The paper makes two separable contributions. The VC model is architectural: it treats a data-dependent-prior diffusion model as an autoencoder, uses a phoneme-averaged "average voice" mel-spectrogram as the speaker-independent prior mean, and conditions the score network on the noisy target mel at the current diffusion time. The diffusion framework, encoder and U-Net all come from Grad-TTS, so the VC-specific novelty lies in the average-voice target and the time-dependent speaker conditioning. The average-voice target needs a forced aligner and phoneme labels at training time, though not at inference.

The maximum likelihood SDE solver is the more general contribution. It is a training-free change to the reverse-SDE update with a likelihood-optimality proof, and the paper shows it works across diffusion types, including on images. Its practical form drops a data-dependent variance term, and the image experiments need a per-setting τ hyperparameter, so the theorem's guarantee does not fully carry over to the evaluated samplers. The VC baselines are reasonable for 2021 one-shot VC, but only one baseline is trained at scale, and its training data overlaps the VCTK test speakers' domain.

## Field Significance

> [!tip]
> High — Diff-VC shows that a score-based diffusion decoder conditioned on a single reference utterance can do one-shot any-to-any voice conversion, with clear subjective gains over the VCTK-trained one-shot baselines it tests. Its maximum likelihood reverse-SDE solver gives a training-free way to cut diffusion sampling to a handful of steps, and the paper shows the same solver helps across VP, sub-VP and VE diffusions.

Later corpus systems build on this design: [[interspeech-2025-1101|ZSDEVC]] adapts the Diff-VC backbone and its average-voice encoder for emotional VC, and [[interspeech-2025-0998|Voice-ENHANCE]] derives its restoration stage from Diff-VC.

## Claims

- **supports:** Diffusion decoders can perform one-shot voice conversion to speakers unseen in training, from a single reference utterance and without target-speaker fine-tuning.
  > *Evidence:* On unseen-to-unseen conversion, Diff-VCTK-ML-30 scores naturalness 3.73 and similarity 3.57 on held-out VCTK speakers, against 2.89 and 2.60 for VQMIVC, the strongest of three VCTK-trained one-shot baselines; Diff-LibriTTS-ML-30 scores 4.02 and 3.39 on a 25-speaker set versus 3.83 and 3.03 for BNE-PPG-VC. *(§4.2, Tables 2 and 3)*
- **supports:** The choice of reverse-SDE solver largely determines diffusion sample quality at very low step counts, independently of the trained model.
  > *Evidence:* With the same Diff-LibriTTS model at 6 steps, naturalness MOS on the 25-speaker test is 3.80 with the maximum likelihood solver, 2.99 with probability-flow sampling and 1.57 with Euler-Maruyama; on CIFAR-10 the ML solver gives the lowest FID in five of six diffusion-type and step-count settings. *(§4.2, Table 3; §4.3, Table 4)*
- **refines:** Reducing diffusion sampling steps trades a small, measurable quality loss for a large speed-up rather than coming free.
  > *Evidence:* Going from ML-30 to ML-6 lowers naturalness by 0.22 MOS (4.02 to 3.80) and similarity by 0.12 (3.39 to 3.27) on the 25-speaker test, while GPU real-time factor improves from about 0.5 to about 0.1. *(§4.2, Table 3)*
- **complicates:** One-shot voice conversion trained on a small studio corpus degrades on out-of-domain target speakers, so in-domain test sets overstate generalization.
  > *Evidence:* Diff-VCTK-ML-30 similarity falls from 3.57 on VCTK speakers to 2.71 on the 25-speaker set with LibriTTS and internal speakers; VQMIVC falls from 2.60 to 1.95; training on the larger LibriTTS corpus lifts whole-set similarity to 3.39. *(§4.2, Tables 2 and 3)*
- **complicates:** Speaker-independent content representations learned from acoustic targets can trade intelligibility for cleanliness relative to ASR-derived phonetic features.
  > *Evidence:* The authors report that BNE-PPG-VC, whose PPG extractor is trained on LibriSpeech ASR data, has fewer mispronunciations than Diff-VC but more sonic artifacts; the observation is qualitative, since no WER or CER is reported. *(§4.2)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- The evaluation is subjective only. No WER/CER or speaker-embedding similarity is reported, so the admitted mispronunciation problem is not quantified.
- The guarantee of Theorem 1 assumes a score network trained to optimality and keeps a data-dependent variance term. The VC sampler drops that term, and the image experiments switch to EM above a tuned threshold τ. How to estimate the dropped term is left as future work.
- The fair large-scale comparison has one baseline, BNE-PPG-VC, trained on LibriTTS plus VCTK, while Diff-LibriTTS saw only LibriTTS. The VCTK-trained baselines are run from public checkpoints rather than retrained.
- Ground-truth recordings were rated only for VCTK speakers, so Table 2's whole-test ground-truth row repeats the VCTK values and gives no upper bound for LibriTTS or internal speakers.
- Diff-LibriTTS outputs are post-processed with mel-domain spectral subtraction to suppress background noise the model sometimes produces (Appendix I), so part of its naturalness may come from that step.
- The encoder target needs forced alignment and phoneme labels at training time, and all experiments are English. Model size and training compute are not reported.
- Table 1 shows only modest differences between speaker-conditioning inputs, and the "whole" variant is judged least similar most often on VCTK (48.6%), so the benefit of richer trajectory conditioning is not established.
