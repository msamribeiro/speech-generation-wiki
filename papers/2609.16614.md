> RoleBreak is an open benchmark of 310 roles and 6,688 human-verified spoken turns (averaging 21.57 turns per conversation) that stresses whether spoken dialogue systems keep a role, stay safe and express the right vocal emotion over long conversations. Across nine full-duplex, omni-modal and cascaded systems, the first persona failure comes no later than turn 10.4 on average, and vocal emotion stays near floor.

## Problem

Speech-to-speech dialogue systems increasingly expose voice and role control, but existing spoken role-playing benchmarks are character-centric (roles drawn from movies, TV series or prior role resources) and short: most instances span one or two turns (Table 1). Text-LLM work and general spoken-dialogue benchmarks show that persona consistency, instruction following and safety degrade as dialogue history accumulates, yet none of these specifically tests whether a spoken system sustains a role under targeted, context-dependent pressure. Role inconsistencies that only emerge late in a conversation cannot be observed in one- or two-turn evaluations.

## Method

RoleBreak frames spoken role-playing as a robustness problem: keeping role-consistent behaviour as context accumulates and under targeted pressure on role-specific constraints (§3.1). Each of the 310 instances holds a role specification, a scenario description and one coherent multi-turn interaction, giving 6,688 turns in total (average 21.57 turns, against 18.69 for ActorMindBench, the longest prior benchmark in Table 1).

**Construction (§3.2, Figure 1).** Five annotators curate roles from five prior text role-play benchmarks (RoleLLM, CharacterEval, CharacterBench, RAIDEN and a MiniMax role-play set), translating non-English specifications into a unified format, then add well-known characters and original *user-centered* roles defined by their relationship, responsibilities and behavioural constraints toward the user. For each role, annotators design a scenario in which later turns depend on earlier facts, commitments or events, and insert contextual probes and stress interventions covering identity, knowledge, agency, affect, interaction, safety and protected information. An LLM drafts user turns, turn-level criteria and target emotions; annotators revise them. Spoken user input is produced by zero-shot voice cloning with CosyVoice 3 from real-speaker reference utterances in LibriTTS-R and VCTK, in a neutral speaking style. For affect-sensitive turns, annotators list one or more acceptable response emotions from the eight-class RAVDESS taxonomy; 1,856 turns carry an expressive emotion target. Five human reviewers rate examples on a five-point scale for role quality, scenario coherence, naturalness and target consistency; the retained set averages 4.42/5, with 90.36% of examples rated at least 4.

**Metrics (§4.2).** The benchmark has 11,743 fine-grained criteria, each labelled as interaction, persona or safety. DeepSeek-V4-Pro judges whether each applicable criterion is satisfied, and each dimension reports the mean criterion pass rate. Two long-horizon metrics are defined: persona first-failure turn (P-FFT) and safety first-failure turn (S-FFT), the earliest turn at which any criterion of that type fails (the dialogue length if none fails). The authors check the judge on 312 randomly sampled rubric-response pairs, each assessed by three human annotators, and report 94.55% agreement. Acoustic scoring reuses existing predictors: naturalness is UTMOSv2 predicted MOS mapped linearly from 1-5 to 0-100, and vocal emotion is scored by emotion2vec+, where a response scores 100 if the predicted emotion matches any acceptable emotion and 0 otherwise. Turns whose acceptable set includes neutral or calm are excluded, so that flat delivery is not rewarded. All metrics are higher-is-better.

**Systems (§4.1).** Nine configurations: PersonaPlex (full-duplex); Qwen2.5-Omni-7B, Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct, MiniCPM-o-4.5 and Covo-Audio-Chat (omni-modal); and four cascades of Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 ASR, a Qwen3.5 text LLM (2B, 4B, 9B or 27B) and Qwen3-TTS, with ASR and TTS held fixed. A user-emotion study re-synthesizes the same user utterances in happy, angry and sad styles with CosyVoice 3 and runs them through PersonaPlex with all else fixed (§4.4).

## Key Results

**Main comparison (Table 2).** The Qwen3.5-27B cascade has the highest interaction (62.6), persona (80.3), safety (71.7), P-FFT (10.4) and S-FFT (11.6) scores of all nine systems. Among omni-modal models, Qwen3-Omni leads on interaction (56.9), persona (69.5), safety (54.7), P-FFT (8.8) and S-FFT (9.4), and it has the highest naturalness of any system (63.9). PersonaPlex, the only full-duplex system, is lowest on every semantic dimension (interaction 31.9, persona 50.6, safety 34.5, P-FFT 5.6, S-FFT 6.7) and on emotion (10.0); the lowest naturalness belongs to Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (34.2).

**Long-horizon brittleness.** Even the strongest system first fails a persona criterion at turn 10.4 and a safety criterion at turn 11.6 on average, roughly half of the 21.57-turn average conversation. Across all systems, P-FFT ranges from 5.6 to 10.4.

**Vocal emotion near floor.** Emotion match rates span only 10.0 to 14.7 across the nine systems, with no system above 14.7 (MiniCPM-o-4.5). The cascades share one TTS model and score 12.9 to 14.1, comparable to the end-to-end omni models (13.8 to 14.7).

**LLM scaling (Table 2, cascaded rows).** Moving the cascade LLM from 2B to 27B raises interaction from 36.3 to 62.6, persona from 58.6 to 80.3, safety from 42.2 to 71.7, P-FFT from 6.0 to 10.4 and S-FFT from 7.7 to 11.6. Naturalness stays between 59.8 and 60.0, as expected with fixed ASR and TTS, and emotion moves only from 13.0 to 14.1 (non-monotonically; the 4B cascade scores 12.9).

**User speech emotion (Table 3).** With linguistic content fixed, changing PersonaPlex's input emotion shifts interaction by up to 4.2 points (29.6 to 33.8), persona by 5.6 (50.4 to 56.0) and safety by 4.5 (34.5 to 39.0). Angry input gives the highest persona score (56.0); happy input gives the highest interaction (33.8) and safety (39.0) scores, so no single input emotion helps every dimension. Naturalness varies by 1.3 points and output emotion by 0.5 points (9.5 to 10.0).

Comparisons are fair in that all systems face identical inputs and judges, but they mix very different system sizes and paradigms, and the user-emotion study covers only one system.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is a benchmark and an evaluation protocol, not a model. What is new relative to prior spoken role-play benchmarks (VoxRole, SpeechRole, ActorMindBench) is the combination of long scripted multi-turn scenarios with deliberate stress interventions, user-centered roles alongside fictional characters, turn-level rubric criteria at scale, and first-failure-turn metrics that expose *when* a role breaks rather than only how often. The acoustic side is assembled from existing tools (UTMOSv2, emotion2vec+, CosyVoice 3 for inputs), and the semantic judge is an off-the-shelf LLM checked against humans on a small sample. The controlled user-emotion manipulation is a useful probe, but it is a single-system study.

## Field Significance

moderate — RoleBreak provides an open, long-horizon stress test for role-conditioned spoken dialogue and demonstrates two gaps across current full-duplex, omni-modal and cascaded systems: role and safety adherence breaks within the first half of a conversation, and expressive vocal emotion in system responses is near floor regardless of LLM scale. The cascaded results separate these two axes cleanly, showing that a larger text LLM improves semantic role robustness without improving vocal expressiveness when the TTS component is fixed.

## Claims

- **complicates:** Aggregate role-adherence scores for spoken dialogue systems can hide failures that appear early in long conversations, so long-horizon evaluation needs time-to-failure measures alongside mean pass rates.
  > *Evidence:* The best system's persona pass rate is 80.3, yet its first persona failure comes at turn 10.4 and first safety failure at turn 11.6 on average, against a 21.57-turn average conversation; P-FFT ranges from 5.6 to 10.4 across nine systems. *(§4.3, Table 2)*
- **supports:** Scaling the text LLM in a cascaded spoken dialogue system improves semantic role and safety robustness but does not improve the emotional expressiveness of the spoken response when the TTS stage is fixed.
  > *Evidence:* Moving Qwen3.5 from 2B to 27B with fixed Parakeet ASR and Qwen3-TTS raises persona 58.6 to 80.3, safety 42.2 to 71.7 and P-FFT 6.0 to 10.4, while the emotion match rate moves only 13.0 to 14.1 and naturalness stays at 59.8-60.0. *(§4.3, Table 2)*
- **complicates:** Current spoken dialogue systems rarely produce the vocal emotion a role-play context calls for, whether they are end-to-end omni-modal models or cascades with a dedicated TTS model.
  > *Evidence:* On 1,856 expressive-target turns scored by emotion2vec+ (neutral and calm targets excluded), all nine systems score between 10.0 and 14.7; the cascades (12.9-14.1) and omni models (13.8-14.7) overlap. *(§4.2, §4.3, Table 2)*
- **supports:** Paralinguistic properties of user speech influence the behaviour of end-to-end spoken dialogue models even when the linguistic content is held fixed.
  > *Evidence:* Re-synthesizing identical user utterances as neutral, angry, sad or happy shifts PersonaPlex's persona score by 5.6 points, safety by 4.5 and interaction by 4.2, with different emotions favouring different dimensions, while output emotion varies by only 0.5. *(§4.4, Table 3)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

All user speech is synthetic: turns are rendered by CosyVoice 3 from LibriTTS-R and VCTK reference speakers, so robustness to real, spontaneous user speech is not tested, and the user-emotion conditions are TTS-generated styles rather than natural emotional speech.

The semantic scores depend on a single LLM judge (DeepSeek-V4-Pro); its 94.55% agreement is measured on 312 pairs, and the paper does not report per-dimension agreement or whether the human annotators listened to response audio or read transcripts. The acoustic metrics reuse UTMOSv2 and emotion2vec+ without validation on this data; the emotion score is a hard match of a single predicted class against an acceptable set, so near-floor scores partly reflect classifier behaviour on conversational speech, which the paper does not examine.

The user-emotion study covers only PersonaPlex, so it is unknown whether omni-modal or cascaded systems (where ASR discards most paralinguistic information) show the same sensitivity. Differences across paradigms are confounded with model size and training data, and the cascades vary only the LLM, so the contribution of the TTS stage to the emotion gap is not isolated. The paper does not state language coverage beyond translating non-English role specifications into a unified format (user speech is cloned from English reference corpora), and no human listening test of system speech is reported.
