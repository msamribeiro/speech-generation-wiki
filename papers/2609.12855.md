> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Xiao Zhou et al.** (ConnexAI) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12855) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ?
>
> Adds a CTC alignment loss on the backbone's own alignment-bearing attention heads inside DPO, applied only to the chosen samples, which lowers content hallucination in a decoder-only codec-LM TTS model without any architectural or inference-time change, and shows that robustness peaks at a moderate, not maximal, degree of attention sharpness.

## Problem

Decoder-only (codec language model) TTS has no explicit text-speech alignment, so autoregressive generation can skip, repeat, or fabricate content words. Existing remedies either change the model (transducer-style modelling, monotonic alignment modules, encoder-decoder designs), depend on an external forced aligner or a teacher model, or constrain attention at decoding time (attention-constrained inference, ACI). Preference optimization such as DPO improves intelligibility but leaves the alignment structure implicit. The paper asks where a monotonic-alignment constraint should be applied (supervised training, preference optimization, or inference) and how strongly it should sharpen the alignment heads.

## Method

The backbone is a 530M-parameter LLaMA-style decoder-only Transformer (14 layers, hidden size 1600, 24 query and 6 key-value heads) in a VALL-E-style formulation, trained from scratch with cross-entropy on 980 h of Whilter-filtered GigaSpeech-M. Text is phonemized with espeak-ng and speech is tokenized by a single-codebook WavTokenizer at 40 tokens/s (24 kHz). Inference uses a prompt pair (prompt text and prompt audio tokens) followed by the target text, so the model clones the prompt speaker.

Because the model is not initialized from a text LLM, a small set of attention heads develops near-monotonic speech-to-text maps (alignment-emerged attention maps, AEAMs). AlignDPO identifies them with a single criterion: each head's row-normalized speech-to-text map, augmented with a blank column (prior 0.367, effective 0.268 after renormalization), is scored by a length-normalized CTC negative log-likelihood over all monotonic paths, computed in one teacher-forced pass over ground-truth pairs. Heads with a score below 2 are selected; in the Base model these are three heads (L4H19, L4H17, L6H16). The same CTC score then serves as the training term, so neither an external aligner nor a teacher is needed for head identification or supervision.

Post-training combines DPO (frozen reference = the base model, beta 0.05, learning rate 6e-7) with the CTC term on the selected heads, weight lambda annealed to 0.1, applied only to the chosen sample of each pair ("preference-gated"). Preference pairs are built automatically: the base model generates 20-30 candidates per prompt-text pair from a 600 h YODAS prompt pool and a text pool of 500k normal and 3k LLM-generated challenging sentences; candidates are split into hallucination-free and hallucinated sets by exact word match, then Pareto-ranked on WER, speaker similarity, and alignment scalars from an external wav2vec2-CTC forced aligner (mean word posterior and a stretch ratio that flags stalling). Pairs are kept only if the two ASR systems agree and the chosen sample also has higher similarity, giving 156k pairs (1,600 h). Eager attention is used only during this post-training to read the maps; pretraining and deployed inference stay on optimized kernels.

Comparison systems hold scale, data, and tokenization fixed: Base, Base+M (the same CTC term applied uniformly during supervised continued training), plain DPO, DPO+M (AlignDPO), ACI applied at decoding to each model, an ungated variant (CTC on chosen and rejected samples), a lambda sweep (0.05 to 1.0), and DPO or DPO+M initialized from Base+M.

## Key Results

On Seed-TTS-Eval English (706 utterances after removing overlaps with training data), DPO+M had the lowest error and hallucination rates among the Table 1 systems that leave decoding unchanged (the ungated variant in Table 3 is marginally lower in-domain, see below): WER 0.052 vs. 0.070 for DPO and 0.142 for Base; content hallucination rate (HAL) 11.33% vs. 15.01% and 29.75%; severe hallucination rate (SEV-HAL, at least four hallucinated content words) 0.57% vs. 1.42% and 4.39%. The HAL difference against DPO is significant (McNemar over 126 discordant utterances, p = 0.026) and the WER difference too (paired bootstrap p = 0.0008); both survive Holm correction. Speaker similarity (0.601 to 0.622) and predicted MOS (WhiSQA pMOS, 4.01 to 4.13) are close across all systems. Adding ACI on top of DPO+M gave the lowest WER, HAL, and SEV-HAL in Table 1 (0.049, 9.63%, 0.14%), whereas ACI on DPO changed little (HAL 14.87%).

The HAL detector has about 95% recall and about 49% precision on a 160-utterance human-validated set. Human labels on 53 clips per system give the same ordering (Base 24, DPO 10, DPO+M 5 hallucinated clips; trend p < 0.001).

Placement and strength (Table 2, Figure 3): Base+M is as sharp as any model (free-running attention entropy 0.08) yet hallucinates almost as much as Base (28.61% vs. 29.75%, not significant). Initializing DPO or DPO+M from Base+M gives HAL 14.31% and 15.58%, back at the DPO level. Across the lambda sweep, entropy falls monotonically while HAL is lowest in a moderate band (lambda about 0.1 to 0.3); HAL tracks the free-running CTC misalignment score rather than entropy.

Gating (Table 3): in-domain, the ungated control is statistically indistinguishable from gated DPO+M (HAL 10.34% vs. 11.33%, p = 0.57, the ungated one marginally lower; WER 0.050 vs. 0.052). On the 500-utterance hard set, gated DPO+M has the lowest WER (0.270 vs. 0.302 ungated and 0.297 DPO) and the lowest severe rate (29.6% vs. 32.2% and 33.2%). The binary HAL detector saturates near 67% on the hard set, so it is not reported there.

ACI's effect is U-shaped in sharpness (Figure 4): it helps the soft Base, is lowest at the moderate DPO+M, and collapses on the over-sharpened models, where the entropy-derived window radius shrinks to 2. A control with a wide fixed radius (7) brings the over-sharpened DPO+M back from 61.9% to 17.4% HAL, close to its plain decoding (15.6%) but no better.

In an A/B naturalness test (15 listeners, 30 utterances, pairs verified hallucination-free), DPO+M was preferred over Base (54.7% vs. 28.2%, 17.1% no preference) and over DPO (46.0% vs. 33.6%, 20.4% no preference), with binomial p < 0.001 and p = 0.004 (Table 4).

## Novelty Assessment

The training objective is new: a CTC alignment term on the model's own emergent alignment heads, placed inside DPO and gated to chosen samples, with one CTC score used both to find and to supervise the heads. Each ingredient has precedent (CTC or forward-sum alignment losses from RAD-TTS-style alignment learning, emergent alignment heads and ACI from prior work, DPO for TTS), so the contribution is a specific combination plus the placement study. The more transferable finding is the analysis: sharpness and alignment decouple under heavy sharpening, so a low attention entropy is necessary but not sufficient, and both training-time and inference-time constraints work best at a moderate sharpness. The experiments are well controlled (fixed data, scale, and tokenizer; paired significance tests; human validation of the detector), but the scale is small (530M model, 980 h supervised data) and there is one backbone, one codec, and one English test set.

## Field Significance

Moderate. The paper adds a lightweight post-training recipe for content hallucination in decoder-only codec-LM TTS and gives controlled evidence that more alignment sharpening is not better, in training or at decoding. Its experiments isolate the contribution of the alignment term against a strong DPO baseline, which makes it a useful data point for preference-optimization work on TTS robustness, though generality beyond one small backbone is untested. The authors state that the paper is accepted to IEEE SLT 2026; this page summarizes the arXiv preprint.

## Claims

- **supports:** Adding an explicit monotonic-alignment loss on a decoder-only TTS model's emergent alignment heads during preference optimization reduces content hallucination beyond preference optimization alone, without architectural or decoding changes.
  > *Evidence:* DPO+M lowers HAL from 15.01% to 11.33% (McNemar p = 0.026), WER from 0.070 to 0.052 (bootstrap p = 0.0008), and SEV-HAL from 1.42% to 0.57% relative to plain DPO on Seed-TTS-Eval English, with similarity and pMOS unchanged. *(§5.2, Table 1)*
- **complicates:** Sharpening alignment attention in autoregressive TTS has a non-monotone effect on robustness: over-sharpened heads can be confidently misaligned and hallucinate as much as an unaligned model.
  > *Evidence:* Base+M has free-running attention entropy 0.08 (vs. 1.01 for Base) but HAL 28.61% vs. 29.75%; models initialized from Base+M return to DPO-level HAL, and the lambda sweep shows HAL minimal at moderate weights while entropy falls monotonically; HAL tracks the free-running CTC score, not entropy. *(§5.3, §5.4, Table 2, Figure 3)*
- **refines:** Where an alignment constraint is applied in the training pipeline matters: applying it to preferred samples during preference optimization helps, whereas applying it uniformly in supervised training or to all preference samples does not carry over to harder inputs.
  > *Evidence:* Ungated CTC (on chosen and rejected samples) matches gated DPO+M in-domain (HAL 10.34% vs. 11.33%, p = 0.57) but regresses to DPO level on the long hard set (WER 0.302 vs. 0.270 gated, p = 0.005); supervised Base+M does not beat Base (p = 0.64). *(§5.3, §5.4, Table 3)*
- **complicates:** Inference-time attention constraints for decoder-only TTS help soft-attention models but can break down on models whose alignment heads are already very sharp.
  > *Evidence:* ACI cuts Base HAL from 29.75% to 22.24% and further improves DPO+M, but on the over-sharpened models its entropy-derived radius collapses to 2 and HAL rises sharply (61.9% for DPO+M from Base+M, recovering only to 17.4% with a fixed radius of 7). *(§5.4, Table 1, Figure 4)*
- **supports:** Reducing content hallucination in codec-LM TTS through preference optimization need not cost naturalness, and can improve listener preference even on hallucination-free outputs.
  > *Evidence:* In a 15-listener A/B test on pairs verified hallucination-free, DPO+M is preferred over Base (54.7% vs. 28.2%) and over DPO (46.0% vs. 33.6%), binomial p < 0.001 and p = 0.004. *(§5.5, Table 4)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

- The authors state that the result is shown for one backbone, one codec, and one corpus: a 530M model trained from scratch on 980 h of English with a single-codebook acoustic tokenizer. Whether the alignment-head analysis transfers to LLM-initialized TTS backbones, where the paper notes alignment emerges differently, or to multi-codebook or semantic-token systems is untested.
- The hallucination detector has about 49% precision; the paper argues that shared false positives cancel in paired tests and backs the ordering with human labels, but those cover only about 53 clips per system.
- The binary detector saturates (about 67%) on the hard set, so out-of-domain robustness rests on WER and the severe rate; the hard set is in-house.
- In-domain, the ungated variant is marginally better than the gated one, so the case for gating rests on the hard set alone.
- The naturalness test is small (15 listeners, 30 utterances) and uses only hallucination-free pairs, so it does not measure how listeners perceive the remaining hallucinations.
- Only English is evaluated, and there is no comparison against strong external systems; comparisons are internal to the authors' backbone. Code release is not stated.
