> **Ben Maman et al.** (International Audio Laboratories Erlangen / Fraunhofer IIS) · [→ Paper](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/details/JH-U-JCdFOitz-vQE985ZA) · [→ Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13278) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ?
>
> A T5-style mel-spectrogram diffusion model built for multi-instrument music synthesis is repurposed for speech and singing voice conversion by adding PPG and f0 conditioning and treating its FiLM timbre conditioning as performer identity, and it matches or beats a flow-matching VC baseline on naturalness and singer similarity while trailing it on phonetic fidelity.

## Problem

Speech VC, singing voice conversion (SVC) and instrumental music synthesis each have strong specialized diffusion or flow-matching models, but real recordings often mix these content types (singing with accompaniment, rap, Sprechgesang). The authors ask whether a model designed for one domain can cover the others. Specifically, they test whether a diffusion model developed for multi-instrument music synthesis, which conditions on a piano roll and a recording-level timbre embedding, can perform speech and singing VC within one framework. A second motivation is data: singing data with lyric and melody annotations is scarce, so they test whether off-the-shelf feature extractors can replace manual annotation.

## Method

The acoustic model generates mel spectrograms, which an off-the-shelf BigVGAN vocoder converts to waveforms. The authors pick a general-purpose vocoder rather than a vocal-only one so that vocal-instrumental mixtures stay possible, accepting a possible quality cost for vocal-only output.

The generator is the authors' earlier T5-based spectrogram diffusion model for multi-instrument music synthesis (Maman et al., ICASSP 2024 and IEEE/ACM TASLP 2024, building on Hawthorne et al., ISMIR 2022). It has a self-attention conditioning encoder and a self-attention spectral decoder. The decoder predicts the noise added to a noisy mel spectrogram and attends to the encoder output through interleaved cross-attention. Training uses an L1 loss on the predicted noise. Model size and the number of sampling steps are not reported; training and sampling "follow the original publications".

The adaptation changes only the conditioning:

- **Time-varying conditioning.** The piano roll channels are extended with a phonetic posteriorgram (PPG) and an f0 contour. Each phonetic or pitch class is a separate channel, concatenated along the channel axis. PPGs come from a wav2vec 2.0 XLS-R 300M model fine-tuned for TIMIT phoneme recognition (a speech-only model, also applied to singing). f0 comes from CREPE. For vocal-instrumental mixtures, vocals are first separated with Hybrid Transformer Demucs, and piano rolls are estimated with a multi-instrument Onsets and Frames extension.
- **Global conditioning.** FiLM layers in every block of both encoder and decoder take a TRILL audio embedding of the performer (speaker or singer). In the music model this mechanism conditioned on timbre and recording acoustics. The diffusion timestep enters through FiLM in the decoder only.
- **Condition dropout.** Each condition is dropped independently, and all are dropped jointly with a separate probability. This allows generation from partial conditions (for example PPG only, with free pitch) and enables classifier-free guidance; the paper does not state the guidance scale used at inference.
- **Pitch range adaptation.** The source f0 contour is mean-shifted in the semitone domain to the target performer's mean pitch.

Because all conditioning features come from automatic extractors, the authors describe training as "self-supervised" on pseudo-annotated data. In the controlled vocabulary this is supervised denoising training on automatically derived conditions, so the page tags it `supervised`.

The dedicated VC baseline (MAC-Voc) is FlowMAC, a flow-matching decoder based on the Matcha-TTS decoder (with the text encoder removed), conditioned on the coarse spectrogram output of PAD-VC, a ForwardTacotron-style convolution and LSTM model trained with spectral L1 reconstruction. PAD-VC alone (PAD-Voc) serves as a lower anchor. All three vocal models (T5-Voc, MAC-Voc, PAD-Voc) are trained on the same vocal data: an internal ~33 h English speech set from five speakers plus ~31 h of singing (SingStyle111 and source-separated vocals). T5-All adds ~90 h of vocal-instrumental and instrumental music, a piano-roll condition and a one-hot data-type flag.

## Key Results

**Naturalness (MUSHRA-style test with hidden vocoded reference and PAD-Voc as anchor, 25 listeners per test after post-screening, Table 1).** The task here is resynthesis with the source performer, not conversion.

| System | Speech | Singing |
|--------|--------|---------|
| T5-Voc | 68.63 ± 18.11 | 59.11 ± 17.54 |
| T5-All | 53.15 ± 17.66 | 49.94 ± 19.27 |
| PAD-Voc | 20.12 ± 15.55 | 15.90 ± 10.57 |
| MAC-Voc | 68.34 ± 17.98 | 55.84 ± 17.53 |
| Reference (vocoded) | 99.28 ± 4.49 | 99.89 ± 1.64 |

T5-Voc and MAC-Voc are statistically tied on speech (Wilcoxon p = 0.69). T5-Voc is rated higher on singing (p = 7.24e-3). Adding instrumental training data (T5-All) costs about 15.5 points on speech and 9.2 points on singing relative to T5-Voc.

**Singer similarity (5-point Likert, 20 listeners, 10 questions, §4.2.2, Figure 4).** T5-Voc scores 3.25 ± 1.09 when conditioned on the reference singer versus 1.76 ± 1.06 when conditioned on another singer of the same gender at the same pitch. T5-Voc beats MAC-Voc, 3.25 versus 2.75 (p < 1e-7). The authors attribute this to performer FiLM conditioning in both encoder and decoder, whereas MAC-Voc conditions only its encoder; this is a hypothesis, not an ablation.

**Fréchet Audio Distance on conversion to random other performers (TRILL embeddings, Table 2, lower is better).** T5-Voc has the lowest FAD among generated systems against the test sets (speech 0.162 vs MAC-Voc 0.171; singing 0.108 vs 0.110) and on Performer-FAD (speech 0.156 vs 0.163; singing 0.141 vs 0.178). Against the training sets MAC-Voc is marginally lower (speech 0.113 vs 0.118; singing 0.092 vs 0.093). The vocoded source has the best All-FAD but the worst Performer-FAD (0.573 speech, 0.369 singing), as expected since its performer is unchanged.

**Pitch accuracy (CREPE plus mir_eval, Table 3).** T5-Voc has the highest score in all six columns: speech RPA 83.0, Ov50 81.9, Ov25 71.6; singing RPA 94.7, Ov50 92.5, Ov25 83.1. Its margin over MAC-Voc is 0.7 to 3.2 points. T5-All loses 0.4 to 0.9 points on singing and 1.1 to 2.6 points on speech.

**Phonetic fidelity (PPG distance between source and output, Table 4, lower is better).** MAC-Voc is best in all four columns (speech J-S 0.322 vs T5-Voc 0.338; speech Wasserstein 4.334 vs 4.495; singing J-S 0.245 vs 0.266; singing Wasserstein 2.151 vs 2.319). T5-All is at or near the PAD-Voc anchor on these metrics. The authors report an example where T5-Voc rendered a German guttural "r" as an English alveolar approximant.

**Vocals with accompaniment (Table 5).** For resynthesis of mixtures with a new performer condition, adding f0 and PPG to the piano roll lowers All-FAD from 0.277 to 0.236 on D_test_mix and from 0.25 to 0.213 on the training mix. There is no listening test or external baseline for this setting.

The comparison is fair in data: all vocal systems share training data, feature extractors and vocoder. The baseline is the authors' own FlowMAC plus PAD-VC stack, not a published SVC system; the paper argues speech VC systems are adequate SVC benchmarks because the designs are analogous.

## Novelty Assessment

The architecture, objective and FiLM conditioning are inherited from the authors' prior music synthesis work. The new elements are adding PPG and f0 channels to the existing time-varying conditioning and pointing the existing timbre FiLM at a speaker or singer embedding. This is cross-domain transfer of an existing model, not a new structure. The authors themselves frame the main contribution as the evaluation. The evaluation is broad (two listening tests on naturalness, one on similarity, FAD, pitch and PPG metrics, across speech and singing). Its scale is small, though: five speakers, eight test singers, internal data, and only targets seen in training. Using a speech-trained wav2vec 2.0 phoneme recognizer for singing PPGs is a useful practical result, though no comparison against singing-specific features is made.

## Field Significance

Moderate — The paper provides controlled evidence that a mel diffusion model designed for instrumental music can match a flow-matching VC model on speech and singing naturalness and exceed it on singer similarity, while losing on phonetic fidelity. It also gives a measured cost for adding instrumental training data to a single model (about 9 to 15 MUSHRA points on vocals). Both results are useful data points for unified speech, singing and music generation, but they rest on small internal datasets and seen target performers.

## Claims

- **supports:** A generative model designed for multi-instrument music synthesis can be transferred to speech and singing voice conversion by swapping its score conditioning for phonetic and pitch features, reaching naturalness comparable to a dedicated voice conversion model.
  > *Evidence:* T5-Voc scores 68.63 vs 68.34 for the FlowMAC plus PAD-VC baseline on speech (p = 0.69) and 59.11 vs 55.84 on singing (p = 7.24e-3) in MUSHRA-style tests with 25 listeners each. *(§4.2.1, Table 1, Figures 2-3)*
- **complicates:** In voice conversion, high perceptual naturalness and performer similarity do not guarantee preservation of the source phonetic content.
  > *Evidence:* T5-Voc matches or beats MAC-Voc on naturalness, singer similarity and pitch accuracy but has higher PPG distances in all four speech and singing conditions (for example speech J-S 0.338 vs 0.322), and it rendered a German guttural "r" as an English approximant. *(§4.2.5, Table 4)*
- **complicates:** Training one generative model jointly on vocal and instrumental audio degrades vocal synthesis quality relative to a vocal-only model.
  > *Evidence:* T5-All drops from 68.63 to 53.15 on speech and from 59.11 to 49.94 on singing naturalness, with worse FAD and PPG distances, while pitch accuracy changes little (0.4 to 0.9 points on singing). *(§4.2.1-4.2.5, Tables 1-4)*
- **supports:** Phonetic posteriorgrams from a speech-trained self-supervised phoneme recognizer provide usable content conditioning for singing voice conversion without singing-specific annotation.
  > *Evidence:* A wav2vec 2.0 XLS-R model fine-tuned on TIMIT phonemes supplies PPGs for singing training and conversion, and the singing systems are trained without manual lyric annotation yet reach 92.7-94.7% raw pitch accuracy and T5-Voc singing naturalness of 59.11, above the dedicated baseline. *(§3.4, §4.2.4, Tables 1, 3)*
- **refines:** Applying performer conditioning in both the content encoder and the generative decoder may give stronger target-speaker similarity than conditioning the encoder alone.
  > *Evidence:* T5-Voc, with FiLM performer conditioning in encoder and decoder, scores 3.25 vs 2.75 for MAC-Voc (encoder-only conditioning) on a 5-point singer similarity scale (p < 1e-7), and has lower singing Performer-FAD (0.141 vs 0.178); the cause is the authors' hypothesis, not ablated. *(§4.2.2, §4.2.3, Figure 4, Table 2)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning]
> All training and test data except SingStyle111 and the Schubert Winterreise Dataset are internal, the speech set has only five speakers, and conversion targets are always performers seen in training. The results do not show any-to-any or zero-shot conversion, and the only baseline is the authors' own FlowMAC plus PAD-VC stack rather than a published singing voice conversion system.

- The naturalness tests measure resynthesis with the source performer, not conversion to a different target. Conversion quality is assessed only by FAD and, for singing, by the similarity test.
- Phonetic fidelity is measured only by PPG distances from the same wav2vec 2.0 model used for conditioning. No WER/CER or intelligibility listening test is reported; the authors list a listening test as future work.
- Speech pitch accuracy is notably lower than singing for all systems, which the authors suspect reflects less stable pitch estimation on speech; this is not resolved.
- The vocal-with-accompaniment experiment is a single FAD comparison without a listening test or external baseline.
- Model size, sampling steps and any guidance settings are not reported. Code release is not stated; a demo page exists.
