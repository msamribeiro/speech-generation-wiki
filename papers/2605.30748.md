> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Deokjin Seo et al.** (Resemble AI / Seoul National University / KAIST) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30748) · Demo: ✓ · Code: ✓
>
> Converts a pretrained autoregressive codec-token TTS decoder into a block-diffusion decoder that unmasks tokens in parallel within each block while still streaming block by block, and adds an inference-time scoring rule that keeps parallel decoding from committing high-frequency tokens such as silence too early.

## Problem

Autoregressive language models over discrete codec tokens give strong zero-shot TTS quality and stream naturally, but they emit one token per forward pass, so wall-clock time grows linearly with output length. Diffusion language models (DLMs) decode several tokens per step, and block diffusion keeps a left-to-right order across blocks that is compatible with streaming. The authors find that DLM decoding recipes do not transfer directly to speech. Codec token distributions are long-tailed: a few dominant tokens (silence, low-energy frames) get high model confidence regardless of context. Confidence-based position selection then commits these tokens first, and an early wrong commitment near a block boundary corrupts the context for all later blocks (boundary-induced context truncation). Prior speech DLM work was small in scale, did not stream natively (OmniVoice), or paired masked diffusion with a separate AR module that passes only a hidden state (DiSTAR).

## Method

The system extends Chatterbox-TTS (Resemble AI, open source), a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 (T3) is a Llama-style Transformer decoder over 25 Hz discrete speech tokens, conditioned on a GE2E speaker embedding, the input text tokens and the prompt speech tokens from the reference clip. Stage 2 is a flow-matching vocoder with a CosyVoice 2-style architecture, distilled to few steps with MeanFlow. The vocoder is reused unchanged; all changes are in Stage 1. The model has 0.5B parameters.

**What is trained.** The T3 decoder keeps its architecture and weights as initialization; only the training objective and attention mask change. The speech sequence is split into non-overlapping blocks of size D. Training uses a hybrid attention mask: causal over the conditioning prefix (to preserve the pretrained embedding space and the text-to-speech alignment), bidirectional within each speech block, and causal across blocks. The loss is masked denoising cross-entropy with a token-shift parameterization taken from Fast-dLLM v2 (a masked position is predicted from the hidden state at the previous position, preserving the backbone's next-token interface). Each sample is paired with its complementary mask so every position is supervised under both masked and unmasked contexts. Training starts from a pretrained Chatterbox checkpoint, uses D = 32, AdamW with a cosine schedule (peak learning rate 1e-5) and an effective batch of 440, on about 70k hours of English (43.8M utterances, 528k speakers) from public corpora plus privately collected audiobook, podcast, IVR, conversational and short-form data.

**What is inference-only.** Decoding commits one block at a time, left to right, with key-value caches for the prefix (computed once) and for committed blocks. Within a block, up to K denoising steps unmask positions in parallel. Two inference-time techniques are added, with no extra parameters:

- **Prior-calibrated scoring (PMI).** Positions are ranked by log-confidence of the predicted token minus the log-probability of that token under an unconditional block prior. The prior is computed once per model from one forward pass on an all-masked block with conditioning zeroed, then cached. The score measures how much the local context, rather than token frequency, licenses the prediction.
- **Early-decoding schedule.** On top of the time-shifted (TS) schedule from LaViDa (also OmniVoice's decoding rule), positions whose calibrated score exceeds a per-step quantile threshold are also committed. A parameter α sets how fast the threshold relaxes; larger α ends blocks in fewer than K steps.

Classifier-free guidance (w = 1.0) chooses the committed token, while the PMI ranking uses the conditional branch only. Gumbel-perturbed position selection (β = 5) and token temperature 0.2 complete the canonical inference setting (D = 16, K = 8, τ = 0.5). The serving engine uses FlashInfer paged caches, CUDA graph replay and an early-emit rule that sends a contiguous committed prefix of a block to the chunk-wise vocoder before the block finishes.

## Key Results

**Standard benchmarks (Table 1, offline).** All baseline rows are copied from OmniVoice's report; the authors state that their test sets and metrics match. With PMI and no early decoding, Chatterbox-Flash reaches SIM-o 0.717 / WER 1.67 / UTMOS 4.29 on LibriSpeech-PC test-clean and 0.704 / 1.96 / 4.09 on Seed-TTS test-en.

- Against the Chatterbox AR model it was converted from, SIM-o rises (0.707 to 0.717; 0.685 to 0.704) and WER falls (1.99 to 1.67; 2.20 to 1.96) on both sets, with UTMOS level (4.29 vs. 4.29; 4.09 vs. 4.10).
- It does not lead the full table on any objective metric. OmniVoice is ahead on LibriSpeech-PC SIM-o (0.729) and WER (1.30). On LibriSpeech-PC WER, OmniVoice-Emilia (1.57), CosyVoice 3 (1.59), Qwen3-TTS (1.60) and ZipVoice (1.64) are also lower. Qwen3-TTS has higher UTMOS on both sets (4.41, 4.16).
- Its UTMOS is higher than every NAR baseline on both sets (4.29 vs. OmniVoice 4.28 on LibriSpeech-PC; 4.09 vs. OmniVoice 3.91 on Seed-TTS).
- The §3.2 text says it "trails only OmniVoice on LibriSpeech-PC SIM-o and WER" within the NAR group and surpasses OmniVoice-Emilia on WER. Table 1 (checked against the PDF) supports the SIM-o part only: OmniVoice-Emilia (1.57) and ZipVoice (1.64) have lower LibriSpeech-PC WER, and OmniVoice-Emilia is also lower on Seed-TTS WER (1.72 vs. 1.96).

**Decoding ablation (Table 1).** Fast-dLLM v2 decoding, applied as-is, fails on speech tokens (WER 15.36 and 14.49). The TS schedule alone and PMI give nearly identical quality at K = 8 (WER 1.69 vs. 1.67 on LibriSpeech-PC, 1.97 vs. 1.96 on Seed-TTS). With early decoding at α = 0.5, PMI keeps WER at 1.67 / 2.04 while TS degrades to 1.83 / 2.20. The step budgets differ (6.4 vs. 5.55 average steps per block), so part of this gap may come from PMI+ED using more steps.

**Streaming and hard inputs (Tables 2, 3).** Moving from offline to streaming changes WER little on the standard sets (PMI α = 0.5: 1.67 to 1.80 and 2.04 to 1.96). On EmergentTTS-Eval, the offline-to-streaming WER rise at α = 0.5 is 3.78 points for TS (35.75 to 39.53) and 0.89 points for PMI (34.79 to 35.68). The gap comes from the pronunciation category (TS streaming 85.88 vs. PMI 72.36); the foreign-word and paralinguistic categories show no PMI benefit.

**Human evaluation (Table 4).** On 10 Seed-TTS test-en utterances with 7 ratings each (70 per system), NMOS is 3.91 ± 0.23 for Chatterbox-Flash vs. 4.04 ± 0.26 for ElevenLabs v3 (overlapping intervals), and SMOS is 4.56 ± 0.18 vs. 3.50 ± 0.35. ElevenLabs v3 has more ratings of 4 or above (80.0% vs. 67.1%); Chatterbox-Flash has fewer ratings of 2 or below (8.6% vs. 12.9%). The rater pool is not described.

**Efficiency (Table 9, Figure 1).** At D = 16, α = 0.5 on an H100 at concurrency 1, time-to-first-packet (TTFP) is 118 ms and RTF 0.107. At D = 32, α = 0.75, TTFP is 103 ms and RTF 0.076, but production-server WER on LibriSpeech-PC rises to 3.70 (Table 10). The Qwen3-TTS figures in the same table (TTFP 97 to 150 ms, RTF 0.234 to 0.313) come from that model's own report under unstated hardware, and the authors explicitly decline to claim a controlled speedup. Early decoding at α = 0.5 saves about 20% of denoising steps at negligible WER cost; α = 1 saves about 41% at roughly +0.6 WER.

## Novelty Assessment

The block-diffusion training recipe is borrowed largely from text DLMs: block-causal attention from Block Diffusion, complementary masking and token-shift loss from Fast-dLLM v2, and the TS schedule from LaViDa via OmniVoice. The adaptation choices for speech (keeping the conditioning prefix causal, converting an existing AR codec TTS checkpoint rather than training from scratch) are sensible but incremental. The genuinely new piece is the inference procedure: PMI scoring against a cached unconditional block prior, and a quantile threshold on those calibrated scores for adaptive early termination. The paper is candid that PMI alone does not improve quality on saturated read-speech benchmarks; its measured value is as a reliable stopping signal and as protection against streaming degradation on hard inputs. The negative result for off-the-shelf Fast-dLLM v2 decoding on codec tokens is a useful data point in itself.

## Field Significance

Moderate — Chatterbox-Flash shows that an existing AR codec-token TTS model can be fine-tuned into a streaming block-diffusion decoder without changing its architecture, matching or improving its parent's objective quality while decoding several tokens per step. It also documents a speech-specific failure of text DLM decoding (frequency-biased position selection over long-tailed codec tokens) and a cheap calibration fix. The evidence base is narrower than the headline suggests: quality gains over a top-confidence baseline appear only under early decoding or on hard streamed inputs, and the latency comparison is against reported, not re-measured, numbers.

## Claims

- **supports:** An autoregressive codec-token TTS decoder can be converted into a block-diffusion decoder by changing only the training objective and attention mask, keeping zero-shot quality while decoding several tokens per step.
  > *Evidence:* Fine-tuning the 0.5B Chatterbox T3 decoder with masked denoising and a hybrid block-causal mask improves SIM-o and WER on LibriSpeech-PC test-clean and Seed-TTS test-en over the AR original, with UTMOS level, at 8 denoising steps per 16-token block. *(§2.2, §3.2, Table 1)*
- **complicates:** Confidence-threshold parallel decoding recipes from text diffusion language models do not transfer directly to discrete speech tokens, because long-tailed codec distributions make frequent low-information tokens the most confident.
  > *Evidence:* Applying Fast-dLLM v2 decoding with a tuned threshold to the same block-diffusion model yields WER 15.36 and 14.49, against 1.69 and 1.97 with a time-shifted top-k schedule. *(§2.3.2, §3.2, Table 1)*
- **refines:** Calibrating parallel-decoding confidence against a token-frequency prior matters less for final quality on read speech than for adaptive step reduction and for robustness on difficult inputs under streaming.
  > *Evidence:* PMI and top-confidence TS selection are nearly identical at fixed K on standard benchmarks, but with early decoding PMI holds WER at 1.67 vs. 1.83 for TS, and on EmergentTTS-Eval its offline-to-streaming WER rise is 0.89 points vs. 3.78 for TS, driven by the pronunciation category. *(§3.2, §3.4, Tables 1, 3, 7)*
- **complicates:** In block-diffusion speech generation, larger blocks raise parallelism and lower latency but degrade intelligibility once the model must commit more positions per step than it can rank reliably.
  > *Evidence:* With a model trained at D = 32, WER stays within noise up to inference block size 16 and degrades sharply at D of 24 or more; on the production server WER rises from 2.03 at D = 16 to 3.70 to 3.86 at D = 32, and the model collapses at D of 128 or more. *(§3.6, Figure 2, §D.1, Table 10, §Limitations)*
- **refines:** Within-block bidirectional context appears necessary for prosodic quality when speech tokens are generated in parallel blocks.
  > *Evidence:* A fully causal block variant (CARD-style) is intelligible only for blocks of 4 or fewer tokens and shows prosodic collapse and flat intonation from D = 5 onward; this evidence is qualitative and reported without metrics. *(§Appendix F)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] Most baseline numbers are not reproduced
> All baseline rows in Table 1 are copied from OmniVoice's report, and the streaming latency comparison uses Qwen3-TTS numbers from its own report under unstated hardware. Only Chatterbox-Flash variants were run by the authors, so cross-system comparisons depend on evaluation pipelines matching.

- The §3.2 summary of NAR-group WER rankings does not match Table 1 (see Key Results), so the prose overstates the WER position relative to ZipVoice and OmniVoice-Emilia.
- The human evaluation is small (10 utterances, 70 ratings per system) against a single commercial system, and the raters are not described.
- Training uses about 70k hours with a large privately collected share (Table 6), so the data cannot be reproduced. The paper does not state whether the LibriTTS-R test subsets, which share speakers with LibriSpeech test-clean, were excluded from training, so unseen-speaker status on LibriSpeech-PC is not explicitly established.
- Evaluation is English-only.
- Block size cannot scale toward full-sequence parallelism: the model collapses at D of 128 or more while OmniVoice's full-sequence formulation stays stable, and alternative recipes (fully causal blocks, block-size annealing with self-distillation) failed beyond small D.
- The comparison against non-streaming NAR baselines does not include pseudo-streaming variants of them (e.g., chunked OmniVoice), so it remains open whether the streaming advantage comes from block diffusion itself.
- The paralinguistic category of EmergentTTS-Eval stays near WER 19 to 20 across all settings, which the authors attribute to the backbone's handling of non-lexical vocalizations.
