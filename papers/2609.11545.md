> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Zuo et al.** (Northwestern Polytechnical University (ASLP@NPU) / iFLYTEK) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11545) · Demo: ? · Code: ?
>
> Builds a category-controlled complex-text test for Thai, Vietnamese, Swahili and Indonesian and scores three released multilingual TTS systems on content (CER), language (LID accuracy) and duration stability (DAR), showing that numbers, dates and mixed-script inputs, not sentence length, drive most failures, and that a training-free Text Risk Score computed from the input text alone correlates moderately with content errors.

## Problem

Multilingual TTS systems now cover many low-resource languages, but they are usually evaluated on ordinary sentences with naturalness, speaker similarity and an average intelligibility score. Real deployment text contains numbers, dates, prices, phone numbers, named entities, long compound sentences, English insertions and punctuation-driven structures, which stress text normalization, pronunciation and language control. In low-resource languages these front-end resources (normalization rules, lexicons, G2P tools) are least mature. The authors argue that average-quality evaluation cannot show how and where a multilingual system fails under such inputs, and that failures are multidimensional: a system can sound natural yet omit content, drift into another language, repeat or truncate.

## Method

The paper proposes a diagnosis-oriented evaluation framework in four stages: a complex-text test set, synthesis by off-the-shelf systems, automatic diagnosis on three dimensions, and failure attribution combining text categories, a text-level risk score and manual inspection of sampled cases.

**Test set.** Four languages are chosen to vary script and front-end difficulty: Thai (non-Latin script, no whitespace word boundaries), Vietnamese (Latin script with dense tone diacritics), Swahili (Latin-script Bantu language with limited speech tooling) and Indonesian (regular spelling, frequent code-switching, used as a lower-complexity reference). Each language has 240 texts, 40 in each of six categories: ordinary short sentences, numbers and dates (dates, times, prices, phone numbers, percentages, units), named entities (local and international), long or compound sentences, code-switched or mixed-script expressions (English abbreviations, brands, app names), and punctuation-related structures (questions, exclamations, quotes, colons, parallel clauses). Ordinary sentences and part of the named entities come from FLEURS and Common Voice; the remaining categories are template-generated, localized and checked by native speakers (§III-B, §IV-A, Table I).

**Systems and protocol.** OmniVoice (released `k2-fsa/OmniVoice` checkpoint, size not reported by the authors), VoxCPM2 (`openbmb/VoxCPM2`, described as a 2B-parameter tokenizer-free model generating continuous representations) and language-specific MMS-TTS checkpoints synthesize all 960 texts once each (2,880 syntheses), through each system's official inference pipeline, with the target language label set where the interface allows it. There is no fine-tuning, speaker prompt, voice cloning, text rewriting or cherry-picking (§IV-A, §IV-B).

**Diagnostic metrics.** Audio is resampled to 16 kHz mono; missing, undecodable, NaN/Inf or sub-0.3 s outputs are logged as invalid and excluded from metric computation (§IV-C).
- *CER* (content consistency): Whisper-large-v3 with the language forced to the target, beam size 5, compared with a normalized reference. For numbers and dates the reference is the expected spoken form, and CER is taken as the lower of the scores against the spoken form and the original written input, so that ASR inverse text normalization (transcribing spoken "twelve" back as "12") is not counted as a TTS error. CER is used instead of WER because Thai word boundaries are unstable.
- *LID-Acc* (language consistency): Whisper-large-v3 run again without a language hint; the detected label must match the target language.
- *DAR* (generation stability): the ratio of audio duration to normalized reference length is computed per sample, and a sample is flagged as abnormal if the ratio falls outside the 1.5 x IQR fences of its own model-language distribution. DAR is the share of flagged samples.

**Text Risk Score (TRS).** Six rule-based features are extracted from the input text alone: length, digit ratio, punctuation ratio, non-target-script ratio, a binary named-entity flag and a category prior (low for ordinary sentences, medium for entities and punctuation, high for numbers/dates, long and mixed-script). Continuous features are min-max normalized within each language and combined with equal weights (1/K), so no annotation or training is needed (§III-C, Table II). TRS is analyzed through within-language tertile groups and through Spearman correlation with CER, DAR and LID-Err (defined as 1 minus LID-Acc, so a positive correlation means riskier texts produce more errors on every indicator).

## Key Results

**Per system and language (Table III).** No system is uniformly robust. OmniVoice has the lowest CER of the three in Thai (7.13% vs. 14.97% VoxCPM2 and 22.04% MMS-TTS), Vietnamese (2.09% vs. 29.39% and 13.81%) and Swahili (6.02% vs. 14.62% and 10.36%), but the highest in Indonesian (6.85% vs. 2.69% VoxCPM2 and 6.53% MMS-TTS); it also has the highest DAR in Vietnamese (17.50%) and Swahili (12.50%). VoxCPM2 has the lowest Indonesian CER but the lowest LID-Acc in the table (84.17%, Indonesian), and its DAR is the lowest or tied-lowest in every language (0.83% to 4.17%). MMS-TTS has the highest Thai CER (22.04%), the highest Indonesian DAR (13.33%) and LID-Acc of 89.17% in Swahili.

**Per category (Table IV, averaged over systems and languages).** Numbers and dates are the hardest category on both content and stability: CER 23.40% and DAR 17.92%, the highest of the six categories on both. Mixed-script inputs follow on CER (17.77%). Long sentences have the lowest CER (2.06%) and a DAR of 0.00%, which the authors read as evidence that length alone is not the dominant risk when no high-risk tokens are present. Ordinary short sentences have the lowest LID-Acc (94.17%), attributed to short duration and few language cues.

**Per system and category (Table V).** Each system has a distinct weak spot. OmniVoice's CER stays at or below 4.01% outside numbers and dates (20.57%), but its DAR is 28.75% on numbers/dates and 15.00% on ordinary sentences. VoxCPM2's highest CER is on mixed-script inputs (32.09%, the highest CER cell in the table), with number/date CER (21.54%) close to OmniVoice. MMS-TTS has the highest CER of the three on numbers/dates (28.08%) and named entities (12.04%), is second on mixed-script (18.31%) and has a number/date DAR of 23.75%.

**Per language (Table VI, averaged over systems and categories).** Vietnamese has the highest average CER (15.10%) despite 100.00% LID-Acc, with Thai close behind (14.72%), and Vietnamese also has the highest DAR (9.17%). Indonesian has the lowest CER (5.36%) but also the lowest LID-Acc (93.33%), which the authors note could reflect either language-control errors or the classifier confusing Indonesian with related languages.

**TRS (Tables VII and VIII).** The high-risk tertile has the highest CER (16.89% vs. 8.80% low and 9.50% medium). DAR is not monotonic across tertiles (5.76% low, 0.45% medium, 11.43% high), and LID-Err is highest in the low-risk group (4.36%, vs. 1.35% and 2.39%). Spearman correlation with CER is positive for all three systems (0.42 OmniVoice, 0.24 VoxCPM2, 0.48 MMS-TTS; average 0.38); with DAR it is positive for OmniVoice (0.43) and VoxCPM2 (0.32) but slightly negative for MMS-TTS (-0.06; average 0.23); with LID-Err it is negative for every system (average -0.08). The authors conclude that TRS flags content risk but not language-identification risk.

**Case analysis (Appendix, Table IX).** Sampled cases separate measurement artifacts from real failures: two number/date outputs score 0.00% CER only because of the format-robust matching (ASR wrote the digits back), while real failures include an MMS-TTS Vietnamese output that loses the room numbers entirely (54.17% CER), a VoxCPM2 Swahili output that loops one phrase (uncapped CER 634.00%), "newsletter" heard as "next time", and "Elon Musk ... Starlink" heard as "Trump ... Stalin".

All comparisons use the same texts, inference settings and scoring backend, so the relative ranking is internally fair, but every score depends on one ASR/LID model, and the systems are run without speaker prompts, which is not the mode zero-shot systems such as OmniVoice are designed around.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is evaluation design rather than modeling. Robustness test sets for hard sentences and normalization-heavy inputs exist for high-resource languages, and the paper's category list resembles standard text-normalization semiotic classes; what is new is a category-controlled, cross-language version for four low-resource languages, paired with three separate failure axes and a few practical protocol choices (format-robust CER for number/date items, DAR fenced per model-language pair, decoupled forced-language vs. free LID decoding). TRS is a deliberately simple equal-weight combination of hand-picked features; its category-prior feature encodes the test set's own labels, so part of its correlation with errors is category membership rather than an independent text signal, and no feature ablation is reported. The empirical findings (number/date and code-switched inputs dominate errors, long clean sentences do not) are informative but come from 40 texts per category per language and three systems.

## Field Significance

moderate — The paper provides a concrete, cross-language diagnostic for low-resource multilingual TTS that separates content, language and duration failures, and its results show that recent large multilingual systems still fail on number verbalization and code-switched inputs in ways ordinary-sentence evaluation does not expose, with a different weakness profile per system. Its format-robust CER for numeric items and its case analysis are useful cautions for anyone scoring multilingual TTS with ASR. TRS is shown to be a coarse pre-synthesis screen for content risk, not a general failure predictor. The authors state that the paper is accepted to NCMMSC 2026; this page summarizes the arXiv preprint.

## Claims

- **supports:** Multilingual TTS evaluation on ordinary sentences underestimates deployment failures, because number/date and code-switched inputs produce substantially more content and duration errors than plain text.
  > *Evidence:* Averaged over three systems and four low-resource languages, number/date inputs reach 23.40% CER and 17.92% DAR and mixed-script inputs 17.77% CER, against 8.60% CER for ordinary sentences and 2.06% CER with 0.00% DAR for long sentences. *(§V-B, Table IV)*
- **refines:** Input length alone is not the main driver of content errors in multilingual TTS; specific written token types (digits, foreign words, named entities) matter more.
  > *Evidence:* Long or compound sentences have the lowest CER of all six categories for every system (0.50%, 0.79% and 4.88%), while each system's highest CER falls on number/date or mixed-script inputs, and a long, clean Indonesian MMS-TTS sample transcribes at 0.00% CER. *(§V-C, Table V; Appendix, Table IX)*
- **complicates:** Robustness of multilingual TTS cannot be summarized by a single metric, since content accuracy, language consistency and duration stability rank systems differently.
  > *Evidence:* VoxCPM2 has the lowest Indonesian CER (2.69%) but the lowest LID-Acc in the table (84.17%), and OmniVoice has the lowest Vietnamese CER (2.09%) alongside the highest Vietnamese DAR (17.50%). *(§V-A, Table III)*
- **complicates:** ASR-based intelligibility scoring of numeric content in synthesized speech is confounded by inverse text normalization in the recognizer, so spoken-form references alone overstate TTS errors.
  > *Evidence:* Whisper-large-v3 transcribes correctly spoken Vietnamese dates and an Indonesian phone number back into digit form, which scores as errors against spoken-form references and as 0.00% CER under the format-robust matching that takes the lower of the spoken-form and written-form CER. *(§IV-C; Appendix, Table IX)*
- **supports:** Simple, training-free features of the input text can partially predict content errors in synthesized speech, but not language-identification errors.
  > *Evidence:* An equal-weight Text Risk Score over six rule-based text features correlates with CER at Spearman 0.42, 0.24 and 0.48 for the three systems (average 0.38), while its correlation with LID-Err is negative for all three (average -0.08). *(§V-E, Table VIII)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] Every score comes from one automatic backend
> CER and LID-Acc both rely on Whisper-large-v3, with no human listening test, pronunciation scoring or native-speaker check of the audio (native speakers only verified the input texts). Per-language conclusions, especially Thai CER and Indonesian LID-Acc, may reflect the recognizer's weaknesses as much as the TTS systems', which the authors acknowledge.

- **Small cells.** Each category has 40 texts per language and each text is synthesized once, so per-system, per-language, per-category estimates rest on few samples and no variance or significance testing is reported.
- **DAR is relative.** Abnormality is defined by IQR fences within each model-language pair, so DAR measures outliers relative to that system's own duration distribution rather than an absolute failure rate, and it is not directly comparable to a human-verified count of truncations or repetitions.
- **TRS design.** The category prior feeds the test set's own category labels into the score, equal weights are fixed by assumption, and no ablation shows which features carry the correlation. The DAR tertile pattern is non-monotonic and the LID-Err correlation is negative.
- **Evaluation setup.** OmniVoice and VoxCPM2 are run without speaker prompts or voice cloning, so the results describe their default-voice behavior. Counts of invalid outputs per system are said to be logged but are not reported.
- **Coverage and release.** Only four languages and three systems are tested, and the paper does not state whether the test texts, references or scoring code are released.
- **Open questions.** Whether front-end normalization or risk-aware decoding removes the number/date failures, and whether native-speaker ratings agree with the CER and DAR rankings, are left to future work.
