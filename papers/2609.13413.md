> [!abstract] arXiv · 2026 · Preprint
> **Lucas Rafael Stefanel Gris et al.** (Federal University of Goiás / Federal University of Mato Grosso / São Paulo State University) · [→ Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13413) · Demo: ? · Code: ✓
>
> Builds a fully synthetic English-to-28-languages speech-to-speech translation corpus (about 16,000 hours) by reversing the direction of CVSS: real English Common Voice source speech is paired with target speech generated by machine translation and a multilingual TTS model, in a canonical-voice variant and a cross-lingual voice-cloning variant.

## Problem

Public parallel speech corpora for speech-to-speech translation (S2ST) are scarce. CVSS, the main public synthetic S2ST corpus, covers only a many-to-one setting (21 source languages into English), so work on translation out of English or between non-English pairs lacks training data. The alternatives each have a drawback: SpeechMatrix is mined from European Parliament speech with approximate alignment and covers 17 European languages, and SeamlessAlign (from [[2308.11596|SeamlessM4T]]) releases only metadata that users must reconstruct from Common Crawl (§2, Table 1).

## Method

CVSS-X is a data-generation pipeline, not a new model (§3, Figure 1). The pipeline has four parts:

- **Source speech.** English utterances from Common Voice version 17 are aligned by normalized text matching to the Common Voice version 4 utterances used by CVSS and CoVoST 2. This recovers 240,192 of 264,037 original English utterances (91.0%) with human source audio. Because the matched dev split had only 871 samples, it is topped up from the training split to 10,000. The final splits per target language are train 222,349, dev 10,000 and test 7,843 (§3.1).
- **Text translation.** Transcripts are translated with distilled NLLB-200. It was chosen from seven candidate MT models using an LLM-as-a-judge rating of 100 English-to-Portuguese samples (1 to 10 for accuracy, fluency and terminology) and for its language coverage (§3.1).
- **Speech synthesis.** Target speech is generated with [[2604.00688|OmniVoice]], an off-the-shelf multilingual zero-shot TTS model, chosen for its cross-lingual voice cloning (an English reference driving target-language output) (§3.1).
- **Two variants.** CVSS-X-C (Canonical) uses two fixed reference voices per language, one male and one female, designed with ElevenLabs' voice design feature. The voice is chosen from source-speaker gender metadata (male, empty or other map to the male voice), which yields an 81.4% male / 18.6% female split. CVSS-X-T (Timbre-transferred) clones the voice of the original English speaker zero-shot from the source audio; about 3.4% of samples are skipped because the signal is too weak for cloning (§3.2).

The target languages are 28 languages from 12 families, grouped into 7 macro-categories (Romance, Germanic, Slavic, CJK, Uralic, Indo-Iranian, Other) (§3.3, Table 2). Each language has 240,192 samples, for 6,725,376 translated pairs in total. CVSS-X-C is about 6,730 hours (3.6 s average utterance) and CVSS-X-T about 9,340 hours (5.0 s average), about 16,070 hours combined (§3.4, Table 3). The paper describes this as eight times the size of CVSS. The release is CC-BY-NC 4.0, a license inherited from NLLB-200. The paper does not train any S2ST model on the corpus.

## Key Results

Quality is measured on 200 stratified dev utterances per language (5,600 per variant). Intelligibility is Whisper large-v3 WER/CER and ASR-BLEU against the translated text prompt, a TTS-to-ASR round-trip that measures synthesis fidelity, not translation quality. For ZH, JA and TH the paper uses CER and language-specific tokenizers. Naturalness is UTMOS and speaker similarity is ECAPA-TDNN. CVSS is re-evaluated with the same pipeline (§4.1).

- **Overall versus CVSS (Table 5).** UTMOS is 3.55 for CVSS-X-C and 3.21 for CVSS-X-T, against 4.43 and 3.61 for CVSS-C and CVSS-T. ASR-BLEU is 82.4 and 79.4 against 94.2 and 93.8. WER/CER is 12.1% and 14.1% against 3.5% and 4.0%. So CVSS-X trails CVSS on every shared metric: 0.88 UTMOS points lower (CVSS-C minus CVSS-X-C) and 11.8 ASR-BLEU points lower for the canonical variant. The authors attribute the gap to the task: CVSS synthesizes only English with a TTS model trained on LibriTTS, while CVSS-X uses one multilingual model for 28 languages. The directions also differ (X→EN against EN→X), so the comparison is asymmetric (§4, §4.2).
- **Voice cloning.** CVSS-X-T loses 0.34 UTMOS relative to CVSS-X-C (3.55 to 3.21). Source-to-target speaker similarity is 0.607 on average, 0.648 for Germanic and 0.567 for Slavic (§4.2, Table 5).
- **Per family (Table 4).** UTMOS stays in a narrow band for the canonical variant (3.48 to 3.62). Romance has the highest ASR-BLEU in both variants (90.3 C / 88.0 T). Indo-Iranian has the lowest (63.7 / 60.0), followed by Other (65.0 / 64.9). The Other family's 24.8% WER/CER is driven mainly by Thai (77.0% WER, attributed to Whisper output without tone diacritics) and Hebrew (37.4% WER; ASR-BLEU 46.1). With language-specific tokenization, ZH reaches 87.6 (C) / 70.4 (T) tokenized BLEU, JA 88.9 / 85.7 and TH 49.1 / 47.8 (§4.2).

No human listening test is run, and no S2ST model is trained on the corpus.

## Novelty Assessment

The contribution is a dataset built with a recipe that is incremental by design: it mirrors CVSS (canonical and voice-transferred variants, source audio drawn from Common Voice) and swaps the translation direction. The components are all off-the-shelf (NLLB-200, OmniVoice, ElevenLabs voice design). What is new is the scale and coverage: EN→X pairs for 28 languages, about 16,000 hours, released in full rather than as mined metadata, plus a sentence-level alignment back to CVSS/CoVoST 2 utterances that makes the two corpora pivotable through English. The evaluation is a corpus-quality check with automatic metrics, not a downstream validation.

## Field Significance

moderate — CVSS-X provides the first openly downloadable one-to-many synthetic S2ST corpus at this scale, and its alignment with CVSS enables bidirectional and English-pivoted training. The paper shows that one current multilingual zero-shot TTS model can generate a 28-language parallel corpus with fairly uniform automatic naturalness. It also shows a sizeable intelligibility and naturalness gap to the English-only CVSS, and a large spread in round-trip fidelity across scripts and tonal languages. Whether the corpus actually improves S2ST models is left untested. The authors state that the paper is accepted to the non-archival SALMA Workshop at EMNLP 2026; this page summarizes the arXiv preprint.

## Claims

- **supports:** A single multilingual zero-shot TTS model can generate parallel target speech for a speech-to-speech translation corpus across many typologically diverse languages with roughly uniform automatic naturalness.
  > *Evidence:* OmniVoice-generated CVSS-X-C speech has UTMOS between 3.48 and 3.62 across all seven language-family groups covering 28 languages. *(§4.2, Table 4)*
- **complicates:** Moving synthetic S2ST corpora from one high-resource target language to many target languages with one multilingual TTS model costs both naturalness and round-trip intelligibility relative to a dedicated single-language synthesizer.
  > *Evidence:* Re-evaluated with the same pipeline, CVSS-X-C trails CVSS-C at 3.55 against 4.43 UTMOS, 82.4 against 94.2 ASR-BLEU and 12.1% against 3.5% WER/CER. The comparison is direction-asymmetric (EN→X against X→EN). *(§4.2, Table 5)*
- **complicates:** Cross-lingual voice cloning for preserving the source speaker in synthetic translation targets trades away naturalness and intelligibility compared with clean canonical target voices, partly because crowdsourced source recordings carry their artifacts into the clone.
  > *Evidence:* CVSS-X-T against CVSS-X-C gives 3.21 against 3.55 UTMOS, 79.4 against 82.4 ASR-BLEU and 14.1% against 12.1% WER/CER, at 0.607 ECAPA speaker similarity. About 3.4% of source clips are too weak to clone. *(§3.2, §4.2, Table 5)*
- **complicates:** ASR round-trip metrics used to validate synthetic multilingual speech are confounded by ASR and tokenization limits on non-Latin, unsegmented and tonal languages, so low scores do not cleanly isolate TTS failures.
  > *Evidence:* Thai reaches 77.0% WER, which the authors attribute to Whisper output lacking tone diacritics. Whitespace SacreBLEU yields zero for ZH/JA/TH until language-specific tokenizers are used, after which ZH and JA reach 87.6 and 88.9 tokenized BLEU for the canonical variant. *(§4.1, §4.2)*
- **supports:** Cross-lingual speaker similarity of cloned translation targets varies with the phonetic proximity of the target language to the source language.
  > *Evidence:* ECAPA-TDNN similarity between English source and cloned target speech is 0.648 for Germanic languages and 0.567 for Slavic languages; the authors suggest phonetic proximity to English as the cause. *(§4.2)*

## Limitations and Open Questions

> [!warning] No downstream or human validation
> No S2ST model is trained or evaluated on CVSS-X (deferred to future work), and every quality number comes from automatic metrics (Whisper round-trip, UTMOS, ECAPA). The corpus's usefulness for S2ST training and its perceptual quality across non-Western phonologies are therefore unverified.

- Translation quality rests on NLLB-200, selected from an LLM-as-a-judge evaluation on only 100 EN→PT samples. ASR-BLEU is computed against the machine-translated prompt, so translation errors are invisible to the reported metrics. Cross-validation against human references (such as the CoVoST 2 English-to-15 subsets) is still pending (§3.1, Limitations).
- The comparison with CVSS is direction-asymmetric, and CVSS uses an English-only TTS model trained on LibriTTS, so the gap mixes language difficulty and synthesizer differences (§4, Limitations).
- The canonical variant's gender mapping assigns empty and other gender metadata to the male voice, giving an 81.4% male skew (§3.2).
- The source audio is crowdsourced read speech, so the voice-cloned variant inherits recording artifacts. Spontaneous speech is planned for later versions (§4.2, §5).
- The CC-BY-NC license restricts commercial use; the authors plan a regeneration with a more permissively licensed MT model (Limitations, §5).
